What Are AI Agents? Fifty Years of History

The idea of software that acts on its own is decades old: actors and negotiating agents in the 1970s–80s, sensor-and-actuator agents in the 1990s, superhuman but single-task reinforcement-learning agents in the 2010s. What changed is the large language model: once a model could reason step by step and call tools, the agent loop of ReAct, BabyAGI and AutoGPT became possible, and stronger base models keep making it work better.

1 · What the course is about ▶ 2:29

LLMs and agents can be seen from two sides. Modelling people know how models are made: pretraining, post-training, prompting, alignment, evaluation. Systems people know how to build reliable systems: tools, APIs, environments, infrastructure. Building agents is mostly engineering that needs both, so the course covers the foundations but puts the weight on hands-on building plus evaluation of agents.

The course assumes you already know basic LLMs (it won't teach pretraining, fine-tuning or alignment). Its main policy message is use AI to learn, and Prof. Yang backs it with two figures ▶ 5:55:

  • Homework vs exams. With AI, almost everyone's homework scores are great; but on the exam, the same population has many more people doing worse than before AI.
  • Anthropic's study of its own engineers. Using a coding agent cut task completion time, but quiz scores measuring mastery dropped significantly. An agent can hand you 1,000 lines of code; it's unclear you understand them.
Lecture goal: get familiar with the agent loop on top of an LLM and its key components. First, where the idea came from, how it developed, and why it suddenly took off with LLMs.

2 · Before LLMs: actors, societies, sensors ▶ 8:23

The word. "Agent" comes from Latin: one who acts. It entered English in the late 14th century.

  • 1973, the ACTOR formalism (Hewitt, Bishop and Steiger): the first indexed use in AI. Each actor is an active agent that waits for a message and then does what the message tells it. Software entities acting on their own are older than most people think.
  • The Contract Net Protocol, a few years later: agents divide tasks among themselves and negotiate.
  • Marvin Minsky's Society of Mind: a group of sub-agents work together to get something done; in hindsight, an early form of multi-agent orchestration.
  • 1990s: more and more software systems serving as agents, from work on designing autonomous agents to attempts to define agents by properties: autonomy, social ability, reactivity, proactivity.
  • Russell and Norvig, the definition most of us know: an agent is anything that perceives its environment through sensors and acts on it through actuators.

3 · The reinforcement-learning era ▶ 10:18

The classic agent–environment loop (Sutton): in state $s_t$ the agent takes action $a_t$, the environment returns a reward $r_t$ and a new state, and the agent acts again based on state and reward.

agent ──action──▶ environment ──state, reward──▶ agent

It delivered: DeepMind's DQN (Deep Q-Network) learned to play games; AlphaGo and OpenAI Five reached superhuman level at Go and Dota 2. But each of these agents was trained for one specific task.

4 · What LLMs added: reasoning and tools ▶ 11:34

  1. In-context learning (GPT-3). Describe a task and give a few demonstrations (e.g. few-shot translation) and the model follows them, with no gradient update, unlike every system above. (More in in-context learning.)
  2. Reasoning: chain-of-thought prompting ▶ 12:39. Asking the model to work through a problem step by step generally gives more accurate answers.
  3. Tools: WebGPT (OpenAI). The model uses a web browser to look things up instead of relying only on what's in its parameters, which led to much follow-up work on web-browsing agents.
  4. MRKL (Modular Reasoning, Knowledge and Language) ▶ 13:17, one of the first systems built around tool use: a router decides which outside expert gets the request. A weather question goes to a weather API; a coding task to a Python tool.
Two prerequisites for agents were now in place: models that reason step by step and models that use tools.

5 · The agent boom: ReAct, BabyAGI, AutoGPT ▶ 14:00

  • ReAct (2022), Reason + Act. Instead of thinking once and answering, the model runs a loop: think about what to do next, take an action, look at what happened, use that to think again.
  • ChatGPT launched, and millions of people started talking to LLMs every day.
  • GPT-4. Agents rely on the base model, and GPT-4 could follow long instructions and plan more reliably.
  • BabyAGI ▶ 15:26, within two weeks of GPT-4: give the model a goal and let it write its own task list, work through it, and add new tasks as it goes.
  • AutoGPT, the same month, days apart: connect GPT-4 to tools (web search, the file system…) and let it keep going on its own without a person typing each next prompt.

Both were ahead of their time and neither really worked well. But they caught people's attention, opened up the imagination about what was possible, and are probably when the word "agent" entered everyday use.

Going beyond the lecture. The lecture dates only the actor model (1973) and ReAct (2022); the other years in the timeline are mine, from the usual references (Contract Net: Smith 1980; Society of Mind: 1986; Maes, Designing Autonomous Agents: 1990; the four properties: Wooldridge and Jennings 1995; Russell and Norvig's first edition: 1995; Sutton and Barto's book: 1998; DQN 2013–15; AlphaGo 2016; OpenAI Five 2019; GPT-3 2020; WebGPT 2021; MRKL and CoT 2022; ChatGPT Nov 2022; GPT-4, AutoGPT and BabyAGI spring 2023).

6 · Better base models, better agents ▶ 16:30

The models. Comparing GPT-3, GPT-4 and GPT-5 on standard benchmarks, each generation makes a big jump: GPT-3 → GPT-4 is a 43% increase on MMLU, and similar on every dataset shown. By GPT-5 some of those datasets are missing from the chart because they're solved (saturated) and no longer give a reliable signal, so harder ones replace them, such as GPQA (very challenging QA) and harder maths. A stronger model means an agent that reasons better, makes fewer mistakes, hallucinates less, and handles longer tasks.

The agents ▶ 17:58. On SWE-bench Verified (fixing real GitHub issues) the best score was about 30% in January 2025; in the year and a half since, the numbers have climbed very high. Recent release charts on OSWorld (computer use) and Terminal-Bench (command-line tasks) are equally impressive. A newer trend: charts plot accuracy against price, because people care about the efficiency of the process too.

7 · Q&A: will capabilities saturate? ▶ 19:06

Will model capability saturate in a few years, or keep improving for a decade? Hard to forecast: a lot happens in two or three months these days. One interesting thread is RSI, recursive self-improvement, which has shown promising results on open maths problems and other tasks; with RSI the pattern may accelerate a lot.

Why do the better results on the Terminal-Bench chart cost more, and why are the newest models better? ▶ 20:02 API cost tracks the number of tokens processed, and the general pattern is that thinking longer improves performance. Why each model beats its predecessor has many possible reasons (better data? better distillation?), and for proprietary models we mostly don't know; open-weight vs proprietary models comes up later in the course.

Key takeaways

  1. "Agent" = one who acts. Software agents date to the 1973 actor model; the Contract Net and Society of Mind already had task division, negotiation and sub-agent orchestration.
  2. Russell and Norvig: an agent perceives through sensors and acts through actuators. RL formalised the agent–environment loop and produced superhuman agents, each for a single task.
  3. LLMs supplied in-context learning, step-by-step reasoning (CoT) and tool use (WebGPT, MRKL): the two prerequisites for general agents.
  4. ReAct (2022) turned them into a think–act–observe loop; after GPT-4, BabyAGI and AutoGPT showed the dream, though neither worked well.
  5. Agents ride on base models: each model generation and each year of agent work has brought big benchmark jumps, now reported as accuracy versus cost.
