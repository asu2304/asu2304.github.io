A Brief History of Deep Learning: Biology, Spring and Winter

Deep learning did not start with GPUs. It starts with a 19th-century argument about whether the nervous system is one continuous web or a network of separate cells, runs through the hype of the perceptron, crashes into the “winter of AI” after Minsky and Papert, and keeps going quietly with backpropagation and the universal approximation theorem. A recurring theme: ideas arrive long before the conditions to make them work.

    2 · Biology first: one web, or many cells?

    Around 1871, Joseph von Gerlach proposed that the nervous system is a single continuous network, not a set of discrete cells. This was the reticular theory.

    • In the 1870s Camillo Golgi developed a staining technique, a chemical reaction that made nervous tissue easier to examine. Looking at stained slices, he too concluded the nervous system was one continuous network.
    • Using the same technique, Santiago Ramón y Cajal reached the opposite conclusion (around 1888–1891): the nervous system is made of discrete individual cells forming a network. This is the neuron doctrine.
    • Around 1891 the term neuron was coined, and the neuron doctrine was consolidated. (The same scientist also coined the word chromosome.)
    • The Nobel Prize in Medicine (1906) went to both Golgi and Cajal, so both camps kept their champions and the conflict lasted.
    • It was settled in the 1950s by a different field: electron microscopy confirmed the neuron doctrine. Neurons are separate cells connected through synapses, and this structure is the foundation of artificial neural networks.

    A second debate asked whether brain processing is localised (specific regions for speech, vision, …) or distributed. Both views still matter: modern networks are largely distributed, but multilingual models, for instance, are sometimes pushed to keep parts of the network for particular languages or inputs.

    Going beyond the video. The lecture doesn't say the name aloud; the scientist who coined "neuron" (1891) and "chromosome" (1888) is Heinrich Wilhelm Gottfried von Waldeyer-Hartz.

    3 · The spring of AI (1943–1969)

    1. 1943, McCulloch and Pitts (a neuroscientist and a logician) proposed a simplified model of a neuron: binary inputs, a binary decision. Example: is it raining, do I have money, do I have time, so do I go to a movie? This is the subject of L7.
    2. 1957–58, Frank Rosenblatt's perceptron added weights: some inputs matter more than others (having money matters more than being in the mood). Covered in L8.
    3. 1965–68, Ivakhnenko and his group already proposed networks that look like a modern multi-layer deep network.

    The claims were huge. Rosenblatt said the perceptron may eventually be able to learn, make decisions and translate languages. Why translation? It was the post-war era, and translating messages between languages such as Russian and English was a pressing problem. Seventy years later we are still working on it: a recent Meta (Facebook) paper covers about 40,000 translation directions across 200 languages, at very uneven quality. A 1958 newspaper article even called it the embryo of a computer that would one day walk, talk, see, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence. That is still the stuff of science fiction.

    A pattern that repeats through the whole history: tall claims are made early, and it takes decades for them to be realised, which leads to disappointment in between.

    4 · The winter of AI (from 1969)

    In 1969, Minsky and Papert's book Perceptrons laid out the limits of what perceptrons can do. The claim had been: give me enough input–output pairs $(x_1,y_1),(x_2,y_2),\dots$ of some unknown function and I can train a model whose output is close to the true $y$. Minsky and Papert showed that a single perceptron cannot even learn the two-input XOR function. Try it below.

    The disappointment was big: if a perceptron can't learn XOR, how could it handle translation or predicting heart disease from blood sugar and cholesterol? But Minsky and Papert's point was about a single perceptron. A network of perceptrons can represent such functions. That second half often got lost.

    What followed was roughly two decades of declining interest and funding for connectionist AI (networks of simple units), while symbolic AI continued. Today's AI is dominated by connectionist methods, and people are again looking at hybrids because deep learning has limits too.

    5 · Work that continued through the winter

    Backpropagation was discovered and rediscovered several times in the 1960s and 70s (without the internet, people didn't know others had found it). In 1986, Rumelhart, Hinton and others popularised it for training neural networks, and almost every modern network is still trained with it. At its heart is gradient descent, which is much older: Cauchy proposed it to compute the orbits of heavenly bodies.

    In 1989 came the universal approximation theorem: a network with one hidden layer and enough neurons can approximate any function $f$ to any desired precision, with the precision controlled by the number of neurons. It is almost the reverse of Minsky and Papert: not "even simple functions are impossible" but "any function is possible". The lecture's picture is a curve approximated by rectangular bars:

    The orange curve is the unknown $f$. Each bar is what a pair of neurons can produce; their sum is the network's output. Add neurons and watch the error fall.

    Going beyond the video. Each bar needs two very steep sigmoid neurons: one that switches on at the bar's left edge and one, subtracted, that switches on at its right edge. That is why the counter shows two neurons per bar. The 1989 theorem is usually credited to George Cybenko (for sigmoid networks), with related results by Hornik, Stinchcombe and White in the same year.

    So by 1989 two things were known: backpropagation can train deep networks, and deep networks are worth training. Yet for nearly two more decades, training deep networks with backpropagation in practice was unstable and often didn't converge, apart from some success with convolutional networks. That changes in L2.

    Key takeaways

    1. The neuron doctrine (discrete cells joined by synapses) beat the reticular theory, settled only by 1950s electron microscopy. It is the blueprint for artificial networks.
    2. Spring: McCulloch–Pitts (1943), Rosenblatt's perceptron (1957–58) and early multi-layer networks (1965–68), with claims far ahead of reality.
    3. Winter: Minsky and Papert (1969) showed a single perceptron can't learn XOR. That a network can was often forgotten, and funding for connectionist AI fell for about two decades.
    4. Backpropagation (popularised 1986) and the universal approximation theorem (1989) showed deep networks are trainable in principle and powerful, but practical training stayed unstable until about 2006.
    5. Ideas often come before the data, compute and know-how to make them work.
