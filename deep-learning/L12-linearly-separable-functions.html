Linearly Separable Boolean Functions, and XOR

The perceptron, and the guarantee of its learning algorithm, only work for linearly separable data. XOR is the smallest function that isn't: its four inequalities contradict each other. Of the 16 boolean functions of two inputs, 14 are linearly separable; in general there are $2^{2^n}$ functions of $n$ inputs, and real-world data are rarely separable. So we need something beyond a single perceptron.

1 · XOR: four inequalities that can't all hold

The convergence proof (L11) needs linearly separable data. So which boolean functions are not linearly separable? The famous example is XOR. A perceptron fires when $w_0+w_1x_1+w_2x_2\ge0$ (L8), so implementing XOR means satisfying one inequality per row:

  1. Rows 2 and 3 say $w_1\ge-w_0$ and $w_2\ge-w_0$.
  2. Add them: $w_1+w_2\ge-2w_0$.
  3. Row 1 says $w_0<0$, so $-w_0>0$ and therefore $-2w_0>-w_0$. Hence $w_1+w_2>-w_0$.
  4. Row 4 demands $w_1+w_2<-w_0$. Contradiction: no choice of $w_0,w_1,w_2$ satisfies all four.

The same thing is clear from the picture: the points with output 1, $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$, sit on one diagonal; the points with output 0, $(0,0)$ and $(1,1)$, sit on the other. However you turn and shift a line, you cannot put the two 1s on one side and the two 0s on the other. (The demo in section 3 lets you check this for every two-input function.)

2 · Real data are rarely linearly separable

XOR isn't a curiosity. Most real-world data won't be linearly separable:

  • Outliers. Split people into those who like machine learning and those who don't. Some people have all the characteristics of the first group but still don't like it. Or: you'd expect everyone in a certain locality to speak one language, but a few speak another.
  • Structure, not noise. Even with no outliers at all, the classes can sit in an inner circle and an outer ring. Each group behaves consistently, but no straight line separates them.

A model that can only handle linearly separable data is not acceptable in the long run. A single perceptron can't find a line separating the positives from the negatives in these cases. The plan: show that a network of perceptrons can, even when the data are not linearly separable to begin with (L13).

3 · How many boolean functions of two inputs?

Two boolean inputs give four possible input rows. A function assigns 0 or 1 to each row, so there are $2^4=16$ functions, that is, $2^{2^2}$. They include always-off ($f_1$), always-on ($f_{16}$), AND, OR and so on. Work through them and you'll find that all except XOR and its negation (XNOR) are linearly separable: 14 of the 16.

4 · And with $n$ inputs?

With $n$ boolean inputs there are $2^n$ input rows, each of which can map to 0 or 1, so there are $2^{2^n}$ boolean functions. For $n=2$ that's the 16 above.

How many of them are not linearly separable? A tempting guess is $n$ (since two of the 16 fail for $n=2$). That's wrong. In fact there's no known formula: Prof. Khapra calls it an unsolved problem. The point is not the exact count. It's that some functions are always not linearly separable, even for simple boolean inputs, and real-world data are messier still. So we need a way to deal with functions that are not linearly separable, first for boolean inputs and outputs (next lecture), later in general.

Going beyond the video: counting them for small $n$. There is no closed form, but for small $n$ we can simply count by brute force: try integer weights and thresholds and record every function a perceptron produces. Press the button (it takes a fraction of a second). The known values are 4, 14, 104, 1882, then 94,572 for $n=5$; the separable fraction collapses towards zero, because the number of separable functions grows roughly like $2^{n^2}$ while the total is $2^{2^n}$.

Key takeaways

  1. XOR is not linearly separable: its four perceptron inequalities contradict each other ($w_1+w_2$ must be both $\ge-2w_0>-w_0$ and $<-w_0$).
  2. Real data are often not linearly separable, because of outliers or because of structure such as concentric classes.
  3. There are $2^{2^n}$ boolean functions of $n$ inputs; for $n=2$, 14 of the 16 are linearly separable (all but XOR and XNOR).
  4. There is no known formula for how many are not separable, but some always aren't, so a single perceptron is not enough: we need networks of perceptrons.
