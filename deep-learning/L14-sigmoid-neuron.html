The Sigmoid Neuron

Real problems have real-valued inputs and outputs, and we'll settle for approximating them. The first step is to fix the perceptron's harshness: a critics' rating of 0.49 means "dislike" and 0.51 means "like". That jump is built into the step function itself. Replace it with the logistic function $\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ and the output changes smoothly from 0 to 1, can be read as a probability, and is differentiable, which is what training with calculus will need.

1 · Enough about boolean functions

This lecture opens a new chapter: sigmoid neurons, gradient descent, feedforward networks and their representation power. So far (L7–L13) we studied $y=f(x)$ with $x$ and $y$ boolean; perceptrons allowed real inputs, but the discussion stayed boolean. Now we want arbitrary functions

2 · The quest: approximate, not exact

For boolean functions, "the network represents $f$" had a crisp meaning: for each input in the truth table, the network outputs exactly the truth-table value. With data, the same idea: for every training pair $(x,y)$ the prediction $\hat y$ should equal $y$.

Now we relax it: can we build a network whose prediction is approximately right, $\hat y\approx y$? In the oil example, we already know the characteristics $x$ of many existing drilling stations and how much oil $y$ each has produced. We want a network that, fed any of those $x$'s, predicts something very close to the actual $y$. (What "approximately" means precisely comes later.) That's only the problem statement; the first step towards a solution is to graduate from perceptrons to sigmoid neurons.

3 · The perceptron is harsh

Recall: a perceptron fires when the weighted sum of its inputs crosses the threshold, $\theta=-w_0$ (L8). Take a single input, the critics' rating of a movie on a 0–1 scale, with weight $w_1=1$ and threshold $0.5$ (so $w_0=-0.5$). Then:

  • Rating 0.49: $0.49<0.5$, so the output is dislike.
  • Rating 0.51: $0.51\ge0.5$, so the output is like.

Nobody decides like that. 0.49 and 0.51 are nearly the same; we want something smoother that grades them: 0.49 is fairly good, 0.51 slightly better.

And this isn't a quirk of the example, the threshold or the weights: it's the nature of the function. Let $z=\sum_{i=0}^n w_ix_i$ be the weighted sum (bias included). The perceptron outputs 1 when $z$ crosses the threshold and 0 otherwise: a step function of $z$. This if-else form makes the output jump suddenly from 0 to 1, whatever problem you apply it to.

4 · The sigmoid neuron

Sigmoid functions are a family of smooth S-shaped functions. The member used here is the logistic function (later in the course we'll meet tanh, another member):

$$y=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\left(w_0+\sum_{i=1}^n w_ix_i\right)}}=\frac{1}{1+e^{-w^\top x}}=\sigma(z),\qquad z=w^\top x$$ with $x=(x_0,\dots,x_n)$, $x_0=1$, and $w=(w_0,\dots,w_n)$, exactly the quantity the perceptron thresholds.

Why the S shape (take $w_0=0$ here so the picture is centred; with $w_0\ne0$ the curve just shifts):

  1. $w^\top x\to\infty$: $e^{-w^\top x}\to0$, so $y\to\dfrac{1}{1+0}=1$. Large inputs saturate at 1.
  2. $w^\top x\to-\infty$: $e^{-w^\top x}\to\infty$, so $y\to\dfrac{1}{\infty}=0$. Very negative inputs saturate at 0.
  3. $w^\top x=0$: $y=\dfrac{1}{1+e^0}=\dfrac12$.
  4. In between, $y$ moves smoothly from 0 to 1. There is no sharp transition, and the output is no longer binary: any value in $(0,1)$ is possible.

Two things to notice. First, with the lecture's numbers ($w_1=1$) the sigmoid is very gentle: over the whole 0–1 rating range $z$ only runs from $-0.5$ to $0.5$, so the output stays between about 0.38 and 0.62. Second, as you make $w_1$ large (keeping $-w_0/w_1$ fixed) the sigmoid gets steeper and steeper and approaches the step function. So the weights control how smooth the decision is, and the step function is a limiting case.

5 · The output as a probability

The output lies between 0 and 1, the same range as a probability. So we can read it as one: an output of 0.49 means "there is a 49% chance I like this movie", and 0.51 means 51%. The difference between 49% and 51% makes sense, unlike the jump from "0" to "1". The harshness has disappeared.

This matters in practice: in most modelling problems where the answer is binary, you'd rather predict a probability than a hard 0/1. "There's a 49% chance of finding oil here", or "a 49% chance this person won't repay the loan", and then a domain expert decides whether to invest.

Smooth / differentiable?no: not differentiable at the jumpyes: smooth, continuous, differentiable

Why do continuity and differentiability matter? Because for a large part of this course, calculus is the hero. The algorithms coming next all take derivatives, and you can't take derivatives of functions that aren't differentiable. That's why it's good to have the sigmoid neuron.

Going beyond the video: the derivative. The logistic function has a famously tidy derivative, $$\frac{d\sigma}{dz}=\sigma(z)\bigl(1-\sigma(z)\bigr),$$ largest ($\tfrac14$) at $z=0$ and nearly 0 where the curve saturates. Tick "show the slope" in the demo. The step function's slope is 0 everywhere except at the jump, where it doesn't exist: a derivative that is zero almost everywhere tells a learning algorithm nothing about which way to move the weights. Notice also that the sigmoid's slope dies out in the flat tails, which will matter for training. My ML notes cover the same function in L55 and its use as a classifier in L56 (logistic regression).

Key takeaways

  1. The goal shifts to real functions $y=f(x)$, $x\in\mathbb{R}^n$, $y\in\mathbb{R}$, represented approximately: $\hat y\approx y$ on the training data.
  2. The perceptron is harsh: with threshold 0.5, ratings 0.49 and 0.51 give opposite decisions. The cause is the step function itself.
  3. The sigmoid neuron outputs $\sigma(w^\top x)=1/(1+e^{-w^\top x})$: 0 as $w^\top x\to-\infty$, $\tfrac12$ at 0, 1 as $w^\top x\to\infty$, smooth in between.
  4. Its output lies in $(0,1)$ and can be read as a probability.
  5. Unlike the step function it is smooth and differentiable, which the calculus-based training algorithms ahead require.
