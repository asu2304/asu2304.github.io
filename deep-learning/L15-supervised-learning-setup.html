A Typical Supervised Machine Learning Setup

Every supervised problem has the same five parts: data of $(x,y)$ pairs, a model $\hat f(x;\theta)$ that guesses how $y$ depends on $x$, parameters $\theta$ that let the data shape that guess, an objective (loss) that scores the guess, and a learning algorithm that finds the $\theta$ minimising it. With real data we no longer ask for zero error, only for the smallest error we can get.

1 · From "zero error" to "minimum error"

Run the perceptron algorithm on data that are not linearly separable. It never converges, because some points keep toggling. Stop it after, say, a thousand iterations anyway: it still ends with some line through the middle of the data. On the lecture's second example, that line misclassifies about two or three of ten orange points and a couple of the ten blue ones, roughly 20% error.

For most real applications that is acceptable. Predicting which party someone will vote for, being wrong on 20% of people still gives a rough idea of how the election is going (unless the race is very close, or the application is mission-critical). Getting to zero error here would need a wildly complex boundary, with islands around stray points.

From now on the goal is to minimise the error, not to make it zero.

2 · The data

We are always given $n$ training pairs

$$\{(x_i,\,y_i)\}_{i=1}^{n},\qquad x_i\in\mathbb R^m,\quad y_i\in\mathbb R .$$

($m$ for the input dimension, because $n$ is already the number of points.) $y$ could also be a vector, for example predicting both a movie's box-office collection and its IMDb rating, but for now it is one real number. The lecture's examples all fit this table:

ProblemEach $x_i$ (an $m$-dim vector)$y_i$
Oil miningsalinity, pressure, density, … at an existing drilling stationamount of oil mined
Bank interest ratesalary, education, family size, past loans and their rates, how fast they were repaidthe rate that suited this customer
Box officebudget, number of superstars, music director, cinematographer, genrecollection

Every supervised problem can be cast as this matrix of $x$'s with a column of $y$'s.

3 · The model: assume a form, learn its parameters

We believe $y$ depends on $x$, so $y=f(x)$ for some function $f$. If we knew $f$, we would just plug in a new location's salinity, pressure, … and drill wherever it predicts the most oil: "all of us would be billionaires". We know a function exists (if you think an input is missing, add it to $x$), but not what it is.

The crux of machine learning: assume a form $\hat y=\hat f(x;\theta)$ with parameters $\theta$ (or $w$), and learn $\theta$ from the data. The assumed relation is called the model.

School version: assume $y=mx+c$. Given two $(x,y)$ pairs you get two simultaneous equations and solve for $m$ and $c$. Real problems have many points and many parameters, but the idea extrapolates: known $x$'s and $y$'s pin down unknown parameters.

Assumed modelName
$\hat y=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-w^\top x}}$logistic (the sigmoid neuron of L14)
$\hat y=w^\top x$linear regression
$\hat y=x^\top W x$a quadratic relation
a deep composite functiondeep learning: just another, very complex, choice of $\hat f$

None of these is right or wrong in advance. Each is an assumption.

4 · Why parameters at all?

Why not simply assume $\hat y=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}}$? It is a function of $x$ too. But it is the same as declaring "$y=3x+2$": you cooked the numbers up without looking at the data, so you can throw the data away and live with whatever it predicts. Nothing is learned.

Parameters keep the form fixed (an S-shape) but let the data choose its shape: $w$ and $b$ decide how steep the S is and where it sits. In the demo below, untick "use parameters" and the sliders stop mattering: whatever the data, the curve can't move.

5 · The learning algorithm and its objective

For $y=mx+c$ the algorithm was "solve two simultaneous equations". With many points and parameters we need something more principled. We have seen one, the perceptron learning algorithm; next comes gradient descent.

What should guide it? The perceptron looped "while not converged", i.e. until zero error, which only made sense for separable data. Now the objective must be to minimise a loss. For the movie example ($x_i$ = everything about movie $i$, $y_i\in\{0,1\}$, model $\hat y=\sigma(w^\top x)$) we want $\hat y_i$ close to $y_i$ on every training point:

$$\min_{w}\ \sum_{i=1}^{n} d(\hat y_i,\,y_i)\qquad\text{e.g. the squared error}\qquad \mathcal L(w)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat y_i-y_i)^2 .$$
  1. Why square? So positive and negative errors don't cancel. Want $y=1$, predict $0.5$: error $-0.5$. Another point: want $1$, predict $1.5$: error $+0.5$. The plain sum is $0$, which looks perfect, though both predictions are off by $0.5$. Squaring removes the sign: $0.25+0.25=0.5$.
  2. Why not the absolute value? $|\hat y-y|$ also removes the sign, but it is not smooth: it has a kink at zero. The square is smooth and differentiable, and "the hero of the course is going to be calculus".
  3. What "learning" now means. $w$ has $m+1$ components (with $w_0$), each anywhere in $(-\infty,\infty)$: infinitely many candidates. We want the one that, plugged into $\mathcal L$, gives the smallest value.

6 · Training, validation and test: the textbook analogy

Learning is always driven by an objective. Think of learning a trigonometry chapter in high school:

the examtest setno more training once you are there

Key takeaways

  1. Real data are rarely separable, so the aim becomes minimum error, not zero error.
  2. Setup: data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $x_i\in\mathbb R^m$; model $\hat y=\hat f(x;\theta)$; a loss; an algorithm that finds $\theta$.
  3. The model is an assumption (logistic, linear, quadratic, or a deep composite function). Its parameters are what let the data shape it; with none, there is nothing to learn.
  4. Squared error: the square stops positive and negative errors cancelling, and unlike $|\cdot|$ it is smooth, which gradient-based learning needs.
  5. Training, validation, test ≈ chapter, exercises, exam. Training minimises the training error.
