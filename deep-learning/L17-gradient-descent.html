Gradient Descent: The Update Rule in Action

Move opposite to the gradient, by a small step: $w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\nabla w_t$. This lecture plays with the rule on a 1-D function with several minima, where the start point and the learning rate $\eta$ decide what happens. It then works out $\nabla w$ and $\nabla b$ for the sigmoid neuron with squared error and runs the resulting algorithm on the two-point toy problem from L16. The loss goes down at every step, slowly on flat stretches and quickly on steep ones.

1 · Playing the update rule by hand

A 1-D loss $\mathcal L(w)$ with three minima. Wherever you are, compute the derivative there, multiply by $\eta$, and step the other way:

$$w_{\text{new}}=w-\eta\,\frac{d\mathcal L}{dw}\Big|_{w}.$$

"Derivative at $w=1.2$" means: take the formula (for $w^2$, it would be $2w$) and plug in $1.2$, giving a plain number. The lecture's run, with $\eta=1$:

  1. $w=1.2$: $\eta\cdot\frac{d\mathcal L}{dw}=0.31$, so $w\leftarrow1.2-0.31=0.89$. Good: heading towards a minimum.
  2. $w=0.89$: $\eta\cdot\frac{d\mathcal L}{dw}=1.25$, so $w\leftarrow0.89-1.25=-0.36$. It jumped across the bottom of the valley and landed on the other slope.

Why? $\eta$ is too large: the step overshoots. Prof. Khapra leaves it as a game, with questions to break your head on:

  • Initialisation: start on a different slope and you roll into a different minimum. Starting at 1.2 you may never reach the deepest one.
  • Learning rate: what happens when $\eta$ is very large? Very small?

2 · The rule, in vector form

From the derivation (L19): the direction $u$ we move in should be at 180° to the gradient. Move opposite to the gradient, and by a small, conservative step $\eta$, because the Taylor approximation behind the derivation is only trustworthy in a small neighbourhood (L18).

$$w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\nabla w_t,\qquad b_{t+1}=b_t-\eta\,\nabla b_t,\qquad\text{i.e.}\quad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta_t)$$ $$\text{where}\quad \nabla w_t=\frac{\partial\mathcal L(w,b)}{\partial w}\Big|_{w=w_t,\;b=b_t},\qquad \nabla b_t=\frac{\partial\mathcal L(w,b)}{\partial b}\Big|_{w=w_t,\;b=b_t}.$$

Again: a formula first, then evaluated at the current point. For $\mathcal L=w^2+b^2$ the gradient formula is $(2w,\,2b)$; at $(w_t,b_t)=(1,3)$ it is the real vector $(2,6)$, which we can add and subtract.

This replaces guesswork with a principled move on the $(w,b)$ plane: at each step the loss is guaranteed to decrease, and by the largest amount available to a small step. That guarantee assumes $\eta$ is small. In the game above the last step increased the loss because $\eta$ was too large.

3 · The algorithm

t ← 0; max_iterations ← 1000 initialise w₀, b₀ randomly while t < max_iterations: w_{t+1} ← w_t − η ∇w_t b_{t+1} ← b_t − η ∇b_t t ← t + 1

Everything is known except how to compute $\nabla w$ and $\nabla b$, which needs the actual loss.

4 · Computing ∇w and ∇b for the sigmoid neuron

Take one data point $(x,y)$, the model $f(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(wx+b)}}$, and the loss

$$\mathcal L(w,b)=\tfrac12\big(f(x)-y\big)^2 .$$

The $\tfrac12$ is for convenience: scaling the loss doesn't move the $(w,b)$ where it is smallest, and it will cancel a 2.

  1. Chain rule on the square. $\displaystyle \nabla w=\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\tfrac12(f(x)-y)^2=\tfrac12\cdot2\,(f(x)-y)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial w}\big(f(x)-y\big)$.
  2. $y$ is a constant (the true value doesn't depend on $w,b$), so its derivative is 0: $\displaystyle \nabla w=(f(x)-y)\,\frac{\partial f(x)}{\partial w}$.
  3. Derivative of $1/g$: with $g=1+e^{-(wx+b)}$, $$\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}=\frac{-1}{g^2}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial w}e^{-(wx+b)}=\frac{-1}{g^2}\cdot e^{-(wx+b)}\cdot(-x)=\frac{1}{g}\cdot\frac{e^{-(wx+b)}}{g}\cdot x .$$
  4. Recognise the pieces. $\frac1g=f(x)$, and $\frac{e^{-(wx+b)}}{1+e^{-(wx+b)}}=1-f(x)$. So $\frac{\partial f}{\partial w}=f(x)\,(1-f(x))\,x$.
  5. For $b$ the same steps give a factor 1 instead of $x$, because $\frac{\partial}{\partial b}(wx+b)=1$.

With $N$ points the loss is a sum, and the derivative of a sum is the sum of the derivatives:

$$\nabla w=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(f(x_i)-y_i\big)\,f(x_i)\big(1-f(x_i)\big)\,x_i,\qquad \nabla b=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(f(x_i)-y_i\big)\,f(x_i)\big(1-f(x_i)\big).$$

5 · Running it on the toy problem

Same two points as L16, $(0.5,0.2)$ and $(2.5,0.9)$. Initialise $w=-2$, $b=-2$, $\eta=1$, max_epochs $=1000$. Each epoch: set dw = db = 0, loop over the points adding each one's grad_w and grad_b (computing $f(x)$ with the current $w,b$), then update w ← w − η·dw, b ← b − η·db.

What the lecture's animation shows: it starts very slowly. After 27 iterations the loss is still high. Then it falls quickly. By 100 iterations it is around 0.02, and it keeps creeping down after that. At no step does the loss go up. Why it is slow in some regions and fast in others is left for later in the course. Try it:

Things to test. With $\eta=1$ from $(-2,-2)$: the long flat start, then the plunge, and zero uphill steps. Start from $(4,4)$ with $\eta=0.3$: 1000 iterations barely move it off the plateau. Push $\eta$ to 30 or more: now the "steps where the loss rose" counter starts climbing. The guarantee needs a small $\eta$.

Going beyond the video: why the plateau. Every term in $\nabla w$ carries $f(1-f)$, which is at most $0.25$ and nearly 0 when the sigmoid is saturated ($f\approx0$ or $1$). At $(-2,-2)$ both points give $f<0.05$, so the gradient is tiny even though the loss is large. This saturation problem returns later in the course. Key takeaways

  1. Gradient descent: $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta_t)$. Opposite to the gradient, a small step $\eta$.
  2. $\nabla w_t$ is the partial derivative's formula evaluated at the current $(w_t,b_t)$: a number, not a formula.
  3. For $f=\sigma(wx+b)$ and $\tfrac12(f-y)^2$: $\nabla w=\sum_i(f_i-y_i)f_i(1-f_i)x_i$, and $\nabla b$ is the same without $x_i$.
  4. With a small enough $\eta$ the loss falls at every step. Too large an $\eta$ overshoots; the start point decides which minimum you reach.
  5. On the toy problem, progress is slow on flat regions and fast on steep ones, but always downhill.
