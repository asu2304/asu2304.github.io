The Taylor Series: Seeing the Loss Up Close

To walk down the error surface we update $\theta_{\text{new}}=\theta+\eta\,\Delta\theta$. The open question is which $\Delta\theta$ to use. The tool for answering it is the Taylor series: near a point we know, any smooth loss is well approximated by a polynomial, a tangent line (or plane) at first order, a parabola at second. The catch, and the reason $\eta$ must be small: the approximation is only good in a small neighbourhood.

1 · The goal: a principled step on the error surface

The guesswork of L16 was navigating the error surface by random jumps, sometimes uphill. Brute force (try every value) is infeasible. We want a way to go down at every step and settle where the loss is minimal.

Put the parameters in a vector, $\theta=[w,\,b]\in\mathbb R^2$. Start from a random $\theta$ and look for a change $\Delta\theta=[\Delta w,\,\Delta b]$ that lowers the loss, one step at a time:

$$\theta_{\text{new}}=\theta+\eta\cdot\Delta\theta .$$
  • Adding $\Delta\theta$ moves $\theta$ towards the direction of $\Delta\theta$ (as adding $x$ turned $w$ towards $x$ in the perceptron, L10).
  • Multiplying by a small $\eta$ shrinks the step but keeps its direction: be conservative, take one small step at a time.

Random guessing picked $\Delta\theta$ arbitrarily and checked afterwards. We want someone to tell us the right $\Delta\theta$. The answer comes from the Taylor series.

2 · The Taylor series

It approximates any continuously differentiable function by polynomials of degree $n$; the higher the degree, the better. Our loss qualifies: the sigmoid is differentiable and so is the squared error. In one variable, around a point $w_0$ where we know the loss:

$$\mathcal L(w)=\mathcal L(w_0)+\frac{\mathcal L'(w_0)}{1!}(w-w_0)+\frac{\mathcal L''(w_0)}{2!}(w-w_0)^2+\frac{\mathcal L'''(w_0)}{3!}(w-w_0)^3+\cdots$$

Why we care: at step 0 we sit at $w_0$ and can compute $\mathcal L(w_0)$, but we don't know the whole curve. We must decide whether a nearby $w=w_0+\delta$ (left or right) has lower loss. Taylor estimates $\mathcal L$ there from information at $w_0$ alone. Cut the series after the first-order term for a linear approximation, after the second for a quadratic one.

3 · First order is just the tangent line

  1. Pretend that near $x_0$ the function is a line, $y=mx+c$. We know the point on it: $y_0=mx_0+c$.
  2. The new point: $y_1=mx_1+c$. Subtract: $y_1-y_0=m(x_1-x_0)$, so $y_1=y_0+m(x_1-x_0)$.
  3. The slope $m$ is the derivative at that point. Rename $x\to w$, $y\to\mathcal L$: $$\mathcal L(w)\approx\mathcal L(w_0)+\mathcal L'(w_0)\,(w-w_0),$$ exactly the series cut at first order.

So the linear approximation draws the tangent at $w_0$ and moves along it instead of along the curve. Because the curve bends, the value on the line is a little off from the true value: some error.

The quadratic cut has the form $y=ax^2+bx+c$, with coefficients again coming from the derivatives (first and second). In a small neighbourhood it hugs the curve more closely than the tangent line does.

4 · How good is the approximation? It depends on the neighbourhood

Let $\varepsilon$ be the size of the neighbourhood around $w_0$. The lecture's demo shows three things, all of which you can check below:

  1. Small $\varepsilon$: good. Near $w_0$ the tangent is very close to the curve. Go far away and the line's prediction is way off.
  2. Same $\varepsilon$, different places. Where the slope is low, even a wider window is well approximated; where the slope is high, the same window gives a large gap.
  3. Small enough $\varepsilon$: good everywhere. Even where the gradient is large, a small window keeps the approximation good.
Taylor approximations are to be used only in small windows. That is where the small $\eta$ in $\theta+\eta\,\Delta\theta$ comes from.

Then raise the degree, on $\sin w$: degree 2 is better than 1, degree 3 better again, and by degree 7 or 8 the polynomial matches $\sin$ almost perfectly over a large range. Keep all the terms and you get the function back exactly.

Try: degree 1, $\varepsilon=0.4$, $w_0=-2$ (flat region): the gap is tiny. Slide $w_0$ to $2$ (steep): same window, much bigger gap. Shrink $\varepsilon$ to 0.05: small again. Then switch to $\sin w$, open the window to 3 and step the degree from 1 to 9.

Going beyond the video: it's the bend, not the slope. The gap left by the tangent line is about $\tfrac12\mathcal L''(w_0)\,\varepsilon^2$: it is set by the curvature and grows with the square of the window. On the loss-like curve slope and curvature rise together, so "steep region, worse approximation" holds there. But a perfectly straight steep line would be approximated exactly. Compare the slope and curvature readouts in the demo, and check that halving $\varepsilon$ roughly quarters the degree-1 gap.

5 · Two variables

The same works for $\mathcal L(w,b)$, which is what we actually have. The lecture uses $0.5\sin x+0.25\sin y$ (axes called $x,y$ for convenience). The linear approximation is now a plane touching the surface. In a small neighbourhood the plane is very close to the surface; widen the neighbourhood and it drifts away, more so where the surface curves strongly. The quadratic approximation stays good over a larger window; here partly because the surface itself looks quadratic in places.

Either way the lesson is the one we need for the next video: around the current $\theta$, the loss is well described by its value, its gradient (first order) and, if wanted, its second derivatives, as long as we stay close.

Going beyond the video. For the 2-D quadratic used above: $f(\theta_0+\delta)\approx f(\theta_0)+\nabla f(\theta_0)^\top\delta+\tfrac12\,\delta^\top H\,\delta$, where $H$ is the matrix of second partial derivatives (the Hessian, introduced in L19). Here $\nabla f=(0.5\cos x,\,0.25\cos y)$ and $H=\mathrm{diag}(-0.5\sin x,\,-0.25\sin y)$.

Key takeaways

  1. Update: $\theta_{\text{new}}=\theta+\eta\,\Delta\theta$. $\Delta\theta$ gives the direction, a small $\eta$ a conservative step. Which $\Delta\theta$? Taylor will tell us.
  2. Taylor: $$\mathcal L(w)=\mathcal L(w_0)+\mathcal L'(w_0)(w-w_0)+\tfrac{1}{2!}\mathcal L''(w_0)(w-w_0)^2+\cdots$$ valid for continuously differentiable losses (sigmoid + squared error is one).
  3. First order = the tangent line ($y_1=y_0+m(x_1-x_0)$), or a tangent plane in 2-D. Second order = a parabola, closer in a small neighbourhood.
  4. Approximations are good only in a small neighbourhood: far away they fail, and faster where the curve is steep or bends sharply. Hence a small $\eta$.
  5. More terms give a better fit over wider ranges; the full series gives back the function.
