Deriving Gradient Descent from the Taylor Series

Write the step as $\eta u$ and expand the loss: $\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)\approx\mathcal L(\theta)+\eta\,u^\top\nabla\mathcal L$ for small $\eta$. A move is favourable only if it lowers the loss, i.e. if $u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L<0$. That dot product is $\lVert u\rVert\lVert\nabla\rVert\cos\beta$, most negative when $\beta=180°$. So the best direction is opposite to the gradient, and that is where the name gradient descent comes from.

1 · The Taylor expansion of a step

From L18: we are at $\theta=[w,b]$ and will move to $\theta+\eta\,\Delta\theta$. Write $u=\Delta\theta$ to save ink. Taylor's series around $\theta$:

$$\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)=\mathcal L(\theta)+\eta\,u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)+\frac{\eta^2}{2!}\,u^\top\nabla^2_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)\,u+\frac{\eta^3}{3!}\cdots+\frac{\eta^4}{4!}\cdots$$

The new point is only a small distance from $\theta$, and $\eta$ is what keeps it small, which is exactly where Taylor approximations are good. $\eta$ multiplies every term. The second term is a dot product between $u$ and a new object, $\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)$.

2 · Derivative, partial derivative, gradient

  • Derivative (one variable): $y=x^2\Rightarrow \frac{dy}{dx}=2x$.
  • Partial derivatives (several variables): $y=w^2+b^2\Rightarrow \frac{\partial y}{\partial w}=2w,\ \frac{\partial y}{\partial b}=2b$. Differentiate with respect to one variable and treat the others as constants; that's why $b^2$ vanishes when differentiating with respect to $w$. Note the change of symbol from $d$ to $\partial$.
  • Gradient: the collection of all partial derivatives, a vector: $$\nabla_\theta\,y=\begin{bmatrix}\partial y/\partial w\\[2pt]\partial y/\partial b\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2w\\ 2b\end{bmatrix}.$$

So $\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)$, read "the gradient of the loss with respect to $\theta$", is just $\big[\partial\mathcal L/\partial w,\ \partial\mathcal L/\partial b\big]^\top$, a vector in $\mathbb R^2$ like $u$.

3 · The next term: the Hessian

For $y=x^2$ the second derivative is $2$. With several variables, the second-order object is the gradient of the gradient: take each entry of the gradient vector and differentiate it with respect to each parameter.

$$\nabla^2_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\dfrac{\partial^2\mathcal L}{\partial w^2}&\dfrac{\partial^2\mathcal L}{\partial w\,\partial b}\\[8pt]\dfrac{\partial^2\mathcal L}{\partial b\,\partial w}&\dfrac{\partial^2\mathcal L}{\partial b^2}\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{(the Hessian)}$$

The general rule: the derivative of a vector with respect to a vector asks, "if this input changes, how much does each output entry change?" for every input, so the answer is a matrix. A 3-entry vector differentiated with respect to 2 parameters gives 6 numbers, a 3×2 (or 2×3) matrix.

The third-order term would differentiate a matrix with respect to a vector. We won't go there, and in fact we don't need even the Hessian for this lecture.

4 · Cut at first order

$\eta$ is small, so $\eta^2$ is smaller still, and $\eta^3,\eta^4$ even more so. Drop everything after the linear term. L18 showed geometrically that the linear approximation is good enough when $\eta$ is small:

$$\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)\approx\mathcal L(\theta)+\eta\,u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta).$$

5 · When is a move favourable?

  1. We were at loss $\mathcal L(\theta)$ and moved to $\theta+\eta u$. The move is good only if the loss went down: $\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)-\mathcal L(\theta)<0$.
  2. Move $\mathcal L(\theta)$ to the left in the approximation: $\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)-\mathcal L(\theta)\approx\eta\,u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)$.
  3. $\eta$ is a positive scalar, so it doesn't change the sign. The condition is on $u$ alone: $$u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)<0 .$$

Any $u$ satisfying this is a good move. But we want the best one: since this quantity is the change in loss, the more negative, the better.

6 · The best direction: 180° from the gradient

  1. Let $\beta$ be the angle between $u$ and the gradient. Then $$\cos\beta=\frac{u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)}{\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)\rVert}.$$ Our quantity of interest sits in the numerator.
  2. $\cos\beta$ is bounded: $-1\le\cos\beta\le1$. Multiply through by $k=\lVert u\rVert\,\lVert\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)\rVert$: $$-k\ \le\ u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)\ \le\ k .$$
  3. Only negative values interest us, and the most negative is $-k$, reached when $\cos\beta=-1$, i.e. $\beta=180°$.
Move in the direction opposite to the gradient. $u=-\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta)$ (up to a positive scale), giving the update $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\,\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta_t)$. "This is something you would have heard a hundred times"; now you know where it comes from.

What to check. For small $\eta$ the two curves coincide: the loss drops exactly for $90°<\beta<270°$ (the half of directions with $u^\top\nabla<0$), and most at $180°$. Raise $\eta$ and the true curve peels away from the cosine: the first-order story is only trustworthy for small steps.

Going beyond the video. "Steepest" depends on how step length is measured: here every $u$ has the same Euclidean length. Methods that also use the Hessian (dropped in §4) choose a different direction. That's Newton's method, not needed for this course's story. Key takeaways

  1. With $u=\Delta\theta$: $$\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)=\mathcal L(\theta)+\eta\,u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L+\tfrac{\eta^2}{2!}u^\top\nabla^2_\theta\mathcal L\,u+\cdots$$ For small $\eta$ keep only the linear term.
  2. Derivative → partial derivatives → gradient (their vector) → Hessian (gradient of the gradient, a matrix).
  3. A move is favourable iff $\mathcal L(\theta+\eta u)-\mathcal L(\theta)<0$, i.e. $u^\top\nabla_\theta\mathcal L<0$ ($\eta>0$ drops out).
  4. $u^\top\nabla=k\cos\beta\in[-k,k]$: most negative at $\beta=180°$. Move opposite to the gradient: $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\nabla_\theta\mathcal L(\theta_t)$.
