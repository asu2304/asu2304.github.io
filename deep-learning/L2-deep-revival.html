The Deep Revival: From Cats to Convolutional Networks

Around 2006, Hinton’s group showed that a deep network trains well with backpropagation if its weights are initialised sensibly (unsupervised pre-training). That spark led to better optimisers and regularisers, competition wins, and in 2012 AlexNet’s ImageNet result. The convolutional networks behind it trace back to a 1959 experiment on a cat’s visual cortex.

1 · 2006: deep networks become trainable

Until then, a deep network initialised with random weights and trained with backpropagation often failed. Hinton's group proposed a simple fix: initialise the weights properly, using unsupervised pre-training, and then training with backpropagation becomes stable, even for a network that was deep by the standards of the time.

  • The idea wasn't completely new: Schmidhuber had trained very deep learners around 1991–1993.
  • It didn't take off then because the conditions weren't right: the internet was young, data and storage were small, and compute was limited.
  • By 2006 compute was better (GPUs had not yet arrived in deep learning) and there was more data to train on.
Going beyond the video. The lecture says "around 2016" at one point, but it means 2006, as it says elsewhere: the deep belief network paper by Hinton, Osindero and Teh (2006). Weight initialisation comes back properly later in the course.

2 · 2007–2009: understanding why it works

Once the spark was there (we always knew deep networks were powerful, from the universal approximation theorem in L1; now we could train them), people asked why unsupervised pre-training helps. Two answers emerged:

HypothesisWhat it saysWhat it led to
Better optimisationPre-training starts the network in a place where the optimisation problem is easier.Better optimisation algorithms (see L3).
Better regularisationPre-training helps the network generalise, acting like a regulariser.Better regularisation methods.

With these, deep learning started winning competitions: handwriting recognition on MNIST, speech recognition, and visual pattern recognition on traffic-sign data.

3 · ImageNet: deep learning arrives

The ImageNet challenge: about 1,000 classes (cats, dogs, aeroplanes, trucks, …), about a million training images, and a test set to classify. In 2012 AlexNet won with an error rate of about 16%. ZFNet cut it to 11.2%, and within a few years it reached 3.6%, better than humans, who get about 4–5% wrong because some images are unclear (a dog's face hidden, two similar breeds). Depth grew from 8 layers in AlexNet to 152 in ResNet.

From here it was accepted that for image problems you migrate to convolutional neural networks. The same shift soon happened in NLP and speech: deep networks replaced older methods. Prof. Khapra calls 2012–2016 the transition period.

4 · From cats to convolutional networks

  1. 1959, Hubel and Wiesel. Electrodes on different parts of a cat's brain, and a stick pattern shown at different positions on a screen. Depending on where the stick is, a different part of the brain fires: each neuron has a receptive field. Different parts of the brain look at different parts of the scene.
  2. 1980, the Neocognitron. A network inspired by this: different parts of the image are handled by different parts of the network, rather than everything being connected to everything.
  3. 1989, Yann LeCun (one of the founding fathers of deep learning) used convolutional networks to recognise handwritten digits, motivated by postal services reading PIN codes to sort letters.
  4. 1998, LeNet-5 improved the 1989 model, and LeCun introduced the MNIST dataset, still among the first datasets every newcomer tries.

Try the cat experiment. Each "neuron" below watches one patch of the screen and, as Hubel and Wiesel also found, prefers one orientation. Drag the stick around and rotate it.

Going beyond the video. The lecture talks about position (receptive fields). Orientation preference is also part of Hubel and Wiesel's Nobel-winning findings, and it's exactly what early convolutional filters learn: edge detectors at different angles. The Neocognitron is Kunihiko Fukushima's model. Convolutions themselves are covered later in the course.

So the CNNs that won ImageNet in 2012 were not new. What changed by 2012 was everything around them: better optimisation, initialisation and activation functions, and an understanding of how to train deep networks on large-scale problems.

Key takeaways

  1. 2006: good initialisation (unsupervised pre-training) made deep networks trainable with backpropagation. Data and compute finally allowed it.
  2. 2007–2009: studying why it worked (optimisation vs regularisation) gave better optimisers and regularisers, and deep learning began winning competitions.
  3. ImageNet: about 16% error with AlexNet (2012) down to 3.6% with ResNet, below human error, as depth grew from 8 to 152 layers.
  4. CNNs come from receptive fields in a cat's visual cortex (1959), via the Neocognitron (1980) and LeCun's digit recognisers (1989, LeNet-5 and MNIST in 1998).
