Lecture 2 · A brief history of deep learning

From Spring to Winter of AI

The story runs from a nineteenth-century argument about whether the brain is one continuous web or many separate cells, to the first mathematical neuron (1943), to the perceptron and a wave of hype (1958), and then to a sharp critique (1969) that froze funding for years. Backpropagation (1986) and the universal approximation theorem (1989) set up the thaw.

1 · Is the brain one network or many cells?

  • 1871–73Reticular theory (Joseph von Gerlach): the nervous system is a single continuous network, not a collection of discrete cells.
  • 1871–73Staining technique (Camillo Golgi): a chemical reaction that makes nervous tissue visible under the microscope. Golgi used it to argue for reticular theory.
  • 1888–91Neuron doctrine (Santiago Ramón y Cajal): with the same stain, Cajal concluded the system is made of discrete individual cells. Wilhelm Waldeyer coined the word neuron (1891) to consolidate the idea.
  • 1906Nobel Prize shared by Golgi and Cajal, who still disagreed with each other.
  • 1950sElectron microscopy settles it: neurons are separate cells, joined at synapses. The neuron doctrine wins.

2 · Spring: from artificial neurons to perceptrons

  • 1943McCulloch–Pitts neuron: Warren McCulloch (neuroscientist) and Walter Pitts (logician) propose a highly simplified computational model of a neuron. Lecture 8 is about it.
  • 1957–58Perceptron (Frank Rosenblatt): a neuron with learnable weights. The press went further than the maths: the New York Times reported the Navy expected it to become a machine that could "walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself and be conscious of its existence".
  • 1965–68Group Method of Data Handling (Ivakhnenko and Lapa): arguably the first trained deep networks, with as many as eight layers.

3 · Winter: the limits of a single perceptron

  • 1969Perceptrons (Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert) lists what a single perceptron cannot do. The famous example: it cannot compute XOR. Read as a verdict on neural networks in general, it helped cut funding. The AI winter follows.

Why XOR? A single perceptron draws one straight line and says "1" on one side. XOR wants the two corners $(0,1)$ and $(1,0)$ on one side and $(0,0)$, $(1,1)$ on the other. Try to find such a line.

4 · The thaw begins

  • 1986Backpropagation popularised by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams for training multi-layer networks. (Versions of the idea appeared earlier, for example in Werbos's 1974 thesis.)
  • 1989Universal approximation theorem (Cybenko; Hornik et al.): a feed-forward network with a single hidden layer and enough neurons can approximate any continuous function to any desired precision.
Minsky and Papert were right about one perceptron. A network of perceptrons can compute XOR easily, and the theorem says networks can, in principle, approximate anything. The catch in 1989: actually training deep networks was still hard. That is where L3 picks up.
Going beyond the video. A two-layer fix for XOR: $h_1=\text{OR}(x_1,x_2)$, $h_2=\text{AND}(x_1,x_2)$, output $=h_1\wedge\neg h_2$. Every unit is a single perceptron; stacked, they do what one cannot.

Key takeaways

  1. Reticular theory (one web) lost to the neuron doctrine (discrete cells), settled by electron microscopy.
  2. 1943 McCulloch–Pitts neuron → 1958 Rosenblatt's perceptron, amid heavy hype.
  3. 1969 Minsky–Papert: a single perceptron can't do XOR → AI winter.
  4. 1986 backprop and 1989 universal approximation reopened the door for multi-layer networks.
