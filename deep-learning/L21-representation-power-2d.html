Building 2-D Towers from Sigmoid Neurons

With two inputs, a sigmoid neuron is a step wall whose orientation is set by $w_1, w_2$. Two walls subtracted give a tower open on two sides; a second pair at right angles closes it, except for some leftover "scaffolding". Adding the two and passing the sum through one more sigmoid that keeps only the top level gives a closed 2-D tower, and many such towers approximate any function of two inputs.

1 · A function of two inputs

Back to oil mining, now with two inputs: $x_1$ = salinity, $x_2$ = pressure. Each location is a point $(x_1,x_2)$ in the plane. At some points we found oil (orange), at others not (blue). The oil points sit in a patch surrounded by no-oil points, so no line separates them: the data are not linearly separable.

The function we want is 1 on the oil patch and 0 everywhere else: a 2-D tower. More generally, an arbitrary surface over the $(x_1,x_2)$ plane can be approximated by many small 2-D towers placed side by side, exactly as in 1-D (L20). So the question again is: how do we make one 2-D tower from sigmoid neurons? Once 2-D is clear, the same idea extends to 3, 4, … $n$ dimensions.

2 · One sigmoid neuron with two inputs

$$h=\frac{1}{1+e^{-(w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b)}}$$
  • $w_1$ controls the slope along the $x_1$ direction: zero gives a flat surface at 0.5, larger values give a gentle S, then a steep one, then almost a step.
  • $w_2$ does the same along $x_2$ (set $w_1=0$ to see it alone).
  • $b$ moves the step: it sets where the crossover from 0 to 1 happens.
  • Both $w_1$ and $w_2$ non-zero: the wall runs diagonally. The orientation is yours to choose.

So, as in 1-D, large weights give a step, now a step wall across the plane.

3 · An open tower, then four walls

  1. Two walls along $x_1$. $h_{11}$ and $h_{12}$ are steep sigmoids that use only $x_1$ ($w=60$ in the lecture's figure, $x_2$ weights zero), with different crossover points. Their difference $h_{11}-h_{12}$ is $0-0$ on one side, $1-1$ on the other and $1-0$ in between: a tower, but open on two sides, a long ridge running along $x_2$.
  2. Two walls along $x_2$. $h_{13}$ and $h_{14}$ use only $x_2$ (the lecture sets $w_{31}=w_{41}=0$, $w_{32}=w_{42}=200$). $h_{13}-h_{14}$ is a second ridge, at right angles to the first.
  3. Add the two ridges. Where both are 0 the sum is 0. Where exactly one ridge is 1 (the four arms) it is 1. In the middle, where they cross, it is $1+1=2$. The walls have now closed, and our tower sits on top at level 2, but with extra scaffolding at level 1 sticking out.
  4. Keep only what is above level 1. Pass the sum through one more sigmoid whose switch-over point sits just above 1 (between levels 1 and 2). Level 0 and level 1 go to 0, level 2 goes to 1. What remains is a tower closed on all four sides.
2-D tower maker: four sigmoid neurons on $(x_1,x_2)$ → two differences, added → one thresholding sigmoid. $$\text{tower}(x_1,x_2)=\sigma\!\Big(w\big[(h_{11}-h_{12})+(h_{13}-h_{14})\big]+b\Big),\quad -b/w \text{ between 1 and 2}.$$

4 · From one tower to any 2-D function

The resulting network: $x_1,x_2$ → four sigmoids (two oriented along each axis) → pairwise differences added → a sigmoid that keeps only levels above 1 → the tower. Make many such towers (adjustable thickness), scale each to the height of the function at its location, and add them: the last stage of the demo does exactly that. Thinner towers give a better approximation, to any desired precision.

Note the honest caveat from the lecture: this construction uses more than one hidden layer (the thresholding sigmoid is a second one), whereas the theorem says one hidden layer suffices. Proving the one-layer version is more complicated; the illustration shows that a small, fixed number of layers can approximate an arbitrary 2-D function.

Why does this matter? In machine learning there is always an $x$, a true $f(x)$ and an approximation $\hat f(x)$ we pick from some family. A neural network is just one more family: a composite function of $x$ (sigmoids, then additions, then sigmoids again). The theorem says this family can approximate any real function, provided we add enough neurons. Reducing that "enough" is a separate story.

A closing question from the lecture: 1-D input needed two neurons per tower, 2-D input needed four. How many for $n$-dimensional input?

Going beyond the video: the answer, and the cost. Two walls per input dimension, so $2n$ first-layer neurons per tower (plus one thresholding neuron whose switch-over sits between levels $n-1$ and $n$). The number of towers is the real problem: with $k$ towers per axis you need $k^n$ of them. This is why, in practice, we rely on depth and learned features rather than on this brute-force construction.

Key takeaways

  1. With two inputs, a steep sigmoid neuron is a step wall; $w_1,w_2$ set its orientation and steepness, $b$ its position.
  2. Two parallel walls subtracted make an open tower (a ridge); a second pair at right angles makes a crossing ridge.
  3. Their sum has levels 0, 1 and 2; a final sigmoid switching between 1 and 2 keeps only the closed tower.
  4. Many scaled towers added together approximate any function of two inputs; thinner towers, better approximation, more neurons.
  5. A neural network is just a (very flexible) composite function $\hat f(x)$ within the usual machine-learning setup.
