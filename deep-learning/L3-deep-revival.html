Lecture 3 · A brief history of deep learning

The Deep Revival

By the 2000s we knew deep networks could represent almost anything, but training them was unstable. In 2006 Hinton and Salakhutdinov showed a trick (pre-train each layer without labels, then fine-tune) that made deep networks train well. Within six years deep learning was winning competitions in handwriting, speech and images.

1 · 2006: unsupervised pre-training

Hinton and Salakhutdinov (Science, 2006) trained a deep network one layer at a time. Each layer was first trained, without labels, to model the layer below it (using restricted Boltzmann machines). The stacked layers then gave a good starting point, and ordinary supervised training fine-tuned the whole network.

  1. Train layer 1 to reconstruct / model the raw input.
  2. Freeze it; train layer 2 on layer 1's outputs. Repeat for every layer.
  3. Put a classifier on top and fine-tune all the weights with labelled data.

Follow-up work (Bengio et al., 2007; Erhan et al., 2009–10) asked why it worked. Two answers: pre-training acts as a better optimiser (it starts the weights in a good region) and as a regulariser (it constrains the weights to explain the input, not just the labels).

That question (why does this help?) pushed people to study optimisation, regularisation, initialisation and activation functions. Better answers there eventually made pre-training largely unnecessary. You will meet each of them later in the course.

2 · Wins in other domains

  • 2009Handwriting: Graves et al. use LSTM networks to win international handwriting-recognition competitions (ICDAR).
  • 2010Speech: deep neural networks replace Gaussian mixtures in speech recognisers (Dahl, Hinton, Deng, Mohamed and others), with large error reductions; Microsoft and Google adopt them.
  • 2010Handwritten digits: Ciresan et al. reach 0.35% error on MNIST with a plain deep MLP trained on GPUs.
  • 2011Traffic signs: a GPU-trained deep network beats human accuracy on the German traffic-sign benchmark.
  • 2012–16ImageNet: deep CNNs win year after year (chart below).

Key takeaways

  1. 2006: layer-wise unsupervised pre-training made deep networks trainable.
  2. It helps as better initialisation (optimisation) and as a regulariser; studying why led to better optimisers, initialisers, activations and regularisers.
  3. 2009–2011: deep nets win at handwriting, speech, digits and traffic signs.
  4. 2012 onward: CNNs dominate ImageNet, with error falling from ≈26% to ≈3.6% in four years while networks went from 8 to 152 layers.
