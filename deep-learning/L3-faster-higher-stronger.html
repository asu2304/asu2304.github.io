Faster, Higher, Stronger: Optimisers, Activations, Sequences and Games

Once deep networks could be trained, the race was to train them faster and more stably: momentum and Nesterov, then Adagrad, RMSProp and Adam; ReLU and its cousins instead of the logistic function; dropout and batch normalisation. The same years saw recurrent networks and LSTMs finally work on language and speech, and deep reinforcement learning beat humans at Atari, Go, poker and Dota.

Where the story stands: enthusiasm in the 1950s, the spring, the winter, some 1990s success with CNNs, the 2006 breakthrough, and by 2012 deep networks winning ImageNet. From about 2016 the pace accelerated further.

1 · Faster: better optimisation

Now that deep networks could be trained, the question became: can we reach a good solution faster, and a better one (deep networks have many minima)? Faster convergence was always a goal in machine learning. Nesterov proposed a method in 1983 that converges faster than plain gradient descent, and the idea grew into a series of optimisers: Adagrad, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW and more, along with increasingly sophisticated learning-rate schedules. All of these come later in the course.

A race on a long, narrow valley, the shape that makes plain gradient descent slow. All four start at the same point with the same learning rate. Press Play.

Going beyond the video. The lecture only names these methods. Rough intuition until we cover them: momentum remembers past gradients so it builds speed along the valley floor; Nesterov looks one step ahead before correcting; Adam scales each coordinate's step by a running average of its gradient size, so the steep and the shallow directions get comparable steps.

2 · Higher: better activation functions

In the 1980s–90s the only activation was the logistic (sigmoid) function. Since then a whole "industry" of activation functions has appeared, each meant to stabilise training or give faster convergence and better performance: tanh, ReLU, leaky ReLU, parametric ReLU, GELU and others. Regularisers such as dropout and batch normalisation (which can also be seen as a regulariser) added further stability.

3 · Stronger: sequences, RNNs and LSTMs

Speech is a sequence of phonemes, video a sequence of frames, text a sequence of words. You can't analyse each millisecond of speech independently: it depends on what came before. So we need networks in which inputs interact over time.

  • 1982: Hopfield networks did some sequence processing (not covered in the course).
  • Recurrent neural networks (around 1986–1990): the Jordan network feeds each time step's output back in as input for the next step.
  • They weren't used for real problems because they were very hard to train: exploding and vanishing gradients (covered later in the course).
  • 1997: LSTMs (long short-term memory) eased the vanishing-gradient problem, but compute and data were still lacking.
  • 2014: with better training know-how, GPUs and large datasets, RNNs and LSTMs entered NLP and replaced statistical machine translation. They dominated roughly 2014–2019.
  • 2017: transformers arrived and slowly replaced them (L4).

Why do gradients vanish? Backpropagating through $T$ time steps multiplies $T$ derivatives together. The sigmoid's slope is at most $0.25$, so the product shrinks like $0.25^T$:

Size of the gradient that reaches step 1 after flowing back through $T$ steps, if each step multiplies it by the activation's slope times a weight $w$ (log scale).

Going beyond the video. This is the one-number version of the real story, where the product is of Jacobian matrices. The lesson is the same: per-step factors below 1 make gradients vanish, above 1 make them explode. LSTMs are built to keep a path where the factor stays close to 1.

Key takeaways

  1. After 2006 the goal became training faster and better: Nesterov (1983), then Adagrad, RMSProp, Adam, AdamW and learning-rate schedules.
  2. Activations moved beyond the logistic function (tanh, ReLU and variants, GELU); dropout and batch norm stabilised training.
  3. RNNs (1986–1990) and LSTMs (1997) were held back by vanishing/exploding gradients, compute and data; they took over NLP in 2014 until transformers (2017).
  4. Deep RL beat humans at Atari, Go, poker and Dota, and MuZero played several games with one agent.
