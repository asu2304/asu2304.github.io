Gradient Descent Recap: Why It Crawls on Flat Ground

The step gradient descent takes is $\eta$ times the derivative, and the derivative is the slope. So on steep parts of the error surface it moves fast, and on gentle plateaus, including the region near the minimum, it crawls. Starting on a plateau can waste hundreds of iterations with almost no change in the loss. This recap sets up the problem that the next few lectures (momentum, NAG, adaptive rates) fix.

1 · Where we are, and today's plan

We began with one sigmoid neuron with two parameters, $w$ and $b$, and learned them with gradient descent. Then we moved to deep networks with any number of layers and neurons, where the same gradient descent works because backpropagation computes all the partial derivatives efficiently, one layer at a time (L29).

This part of the course is about variants of gradient descent. The pattern: spot a peculiarity of plain gradient descent, propose a fix, find the fix's own problem, fix that, and so on. First comes a brisk recap of gradient descent (from L16–L19) and the first problem it has. 2 · The toy problem, and why not guess or brute-force

One sigmoid neuron $f(x)=\sigma(wx+b)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-(wx+b)}}$, two training points $(0.5,\,0.2)$ and $(2.5,\,0.9)$, and the squared-error loss

$$L(w,b)=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^{2}\big(f(x_i)-y_i\big)^2 .$$

"Training" means finding $w,b$ so that the S-shaped curve passes through both points.

approachwhat it doeswhy it fails
Guessworknudge $w$ or $b$, keep the change if the loss drops (a guess gave loss 0.099, say)no principled direction; some guesses climb the surface instead of descending
Brute forceevaluate the loss on a grid (e.g. $w,b\in[-6,6]$) and pick the lowestparameters really range over $(-\infty,\infty)$, and a real network has far too many of them

While guessing, you were in fact walking on the loss surface without seeing it. We want a principled way to walk downhill on it.

3 · The gradient descent rule

From the current $\theta=(w,b)$, the Taylor series tells us the loss in a small neighbourhood. Requiring the new loss to be lower than the current one, $L(\theta+\eta u)<L(\theta)$, leads to: move opposite to the gradient.

$$w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\nabla w_t,\qquad b_{t+1}=b_t-\eta\,\nabla b_t,\qquad \nabla w_t=\left.\frac{\partial L(w,b)}{\partial w}\right|_{w=w_t,\ b=b_t}$$

$\nabla w_t$ means the partial derivative evaluated at the current point. For example, if $L=w^2$ then $\partial L/\partial w=2w$, and at $w_t=1$ the gradient is 2. For our neuron the derivative is (from L17):

$$\nabla w=\sum_i\big(f(x_i)-y_i\big)\,f(x_i)\big(1-f(x_i)\big)\,x_i,\qquad \nabla b=\sum_i\big(f(x_i)-y_i\big)\,f(x_i)\big(1-f(x_i)\big).$$

The algorithm: initialise randomly, then repeat the update for a fixed number of iterations (say max_iterations = 1000).

4 · Watching it run: slow, fast, slow

In the lecture's animation, gradient descent starts near $w\approx-5,\ b\approx-5$, where the loss is high (≈0.4–0.5) and the surface is a flat plateau. The trail of $(w,b)$ points on the plane below is so dense it looks like a snake's body: each update barely moves. When the trail reaches the edge of the valley, consecutive points jump far apart. Inside the valley, on gentle ground again, the steps shrink.

A second start, $w=-2,\ b=2$, is on a slope. It drops into the valley at once, with widely spaced points, then slows down on the gentle approach to the minimum. 100 iterations run out before reaching it, even though it is almost there.

5 · Why: the derivative is the slope

The update is $w\leftarrow w-\eta\,\nabla w$. If $\nabla w\approx0$, the new $w$ is the old $w$. If $\nabla w$ is large, $w$ (and likewise $b$) jumps far. So the magnitude of the gradient sets the size of the move.

The derivative measures how sensitive the function is to a change in its input, $\Delta y/\Delta x$ for a small $\Delta x$: the slope. Take $y=x^2$ and keep $\Delta x=0.5$ fixed:

Same $\Delta x$, bigger $\Delta y$ where the curve is steeper. Make the function $2x^2$ and it's steeper still: its derivative is $4x$ instead of $2x$.

$f(x)=c\,x^2$. The secant from $x$ to $x+\Delta x$ has slope $\Delta y/\Delta x$; the tangent at $x$ has slope $f'(x)=2cx$. Move $x$ from a gentle region to a steep one and watch the slope, and hence the gradient descent step, grow.

6 · The problem to solve

Steep region, large gradient, big move. Flat region, small gradient, tiny move. The repercussions:

  • Unlucky start on a plateau. If the flat region is wide in $(w,b)$, you can spend a very long time there. You might run 1000 iterations and see the loss go from 0.9 to 0.8999…, conclude that "nothing is happening", and give up. 10,000 iterations might get out, but you might not have that patience.
  • Near convergence. Close to a minimum the surface is gentle, so the last stretch is a slow crawl even though you're "almost there". This happens every time, whatever the start.
Goal for the coming lectures: move faster on gentle slopes. First a detour through contour plots (L31), the usual way of visualising these surfaces, then momentum-based gradient descent (L32).
Going beyond the video. Two plateaus show up in this toy problem. Far from the data, $\sigma(wx+b)$ saturates, so the factor $f(1-f)$ in the gradient is nearly 0 even though the loss is large. Near the minimum, the gradient is small simply because we're near a minimum. You can't fix both by raising $\eta$: a big enough $\eta$ to cross the plateau quickly can overshoot in the valley (try $\eta=100$ from $(-2,2)$: it reaches the valley in one step but never settles). That tension is what motivates momentum and adaptive learning rates. Same rule, other problems: my ML notes, ML L31.

Key takeaways

  1. Guesswork and brute-force search over the loss surface don't scale; gradient descent gives a principled downhill direction from the Taylor series.
  2. Update: $\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\nabla\theta_t$, with the gradient evaluated at the current point.
  3. The step size is proportional to the gradient's magnitude, i.e. the slope: fast on steep regions, slow on gentle ones.
  4. A start on a plateau can stall training for many iterations, and the approach to a minimum is always slow.
  5. Next: visualise surfaces with contours, then modify gradient descent to move faster through gentle regions.
