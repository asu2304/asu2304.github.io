Contour Maps: Reading an Error Surface from Above

A 3-D loss surface is hard to look at. Slice it at regular heights, look down from the top, and you get a contour map: every ring is one loss value, and how close the rings sit tells you how steep the surface is. From here on, every optimiser in the course is drawn as a path on such a map.

1 · Why bother with contours?

The previous lecture (L30) ended with a problem: gradient descent crawls on gentle slopes. Before fixing it, we need a better way to draw what the optimiser is doing. A 3-D plot of the loss over $(w,b)$ is cumbersome: a static image only shows one viewing angle, and paths hide behind hills. A contour map shows the same information in 2-D. It is also a standard tool you will meet everywhere in machine learning.

2 · What a contour map is

  1. Take the 3-D surface $z=f(x,y)$.
  2. Slice it with horizontal planes at regular intervals along the $z$-axis. In the lecture's first example the slices are at $z=-1,-0.8,-0.6,-0.4,-0.2,\dots$, one every $0.2$, and each slice is labelled with its level.
  3. Look at the sliced surface from the top. Each slice appears as a ring (not necessarily a circle). That top view is the contour map.

Every point on one ring has the same $z$, because the ring came from a plane parallel to the $xy$-plane. For us the $xy$-plane is the $(w,b)$-plane and $z$ is the loss, so each ring is a set of $(w,b)$ with equal loss.

Lower the viewing angle to see the surface in 3-D, then raise it to 90° and watch it become the contour map on the right:

Left: the surface with its slices drawn at their heights. Right: the contour map. The thick ring is the same slice in both views. Colour: blue = low, red = high.

3 · Ring spacing is slope

Look at two neighbouring rings. In some places they are far apart, in others the same two rings almost touch. Why? Turn the 3-D view sideways: where the rings are far apart the surface is gentle, where they are close it is steep.

The reason is the definition of slope from the last lecture: change in height divided by change in position. Neighbouring rings are always the same height apart, say $\Delta z$. If you must walk a horizontal distance $d$ to climb from one ring to the next, the slope along your walk is about

$$\text{slope}\approx\frac{\Delta z}{d}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad d\approx\frac{\Delta z}{\text{slope}} .$$
Reading a contour map. (1) Rings are levels of the loss, taken at equal intervals. (2) Rings far apart ⇒ gentle slope; rings close together ⇒ steep slope. So the map carries everything gradient descent cares about: where the loss is high or low, and where the slope is steep or gentle.

Test it on the lecture's error surface. Slide the cut $b=b_0$ across the map; the lower plot is the loss along that cut, with the ring levels as dashed lines.

The lecture also draws several other functions and their contour maps and encourages you to try your own: in every case, steep regions give crowded rings and gentle regions give sparse ones. The surface selector in the first demo has two extra shapes to practise on.

4 · Gradient descent on the contour map

Now revisit the gradient descent run from L30 on the lecture's toy problem: one sigmoid neuron $f(x)=\sigma(wx+b)$ that should pass through the two points $(0.5,\,0.2)$ and $(2.5,\,0.9)$, with squared-error loss

$$\mathscr{L}(w,b)=\tfrac12\sum_{i=1}^{2}\big(f(x_i)-y_i\big)^2,\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\nabla w_t,\quad b_{t+1}=b_t-\eta\,\nabla b_t .$$

On the map, the region where the rings are sparse is the flat plateau, and the crowded rings lead down into the valley. Exactly as on the 3-D surface:

  • On the plateau the point barely moves; consecutive $(w,b)$ points are so close they look like a continuous snake.
  • Where the rings crowd together, the slope is steep and $w,b$ change suddenly by large amounts. The whole shape of the sigmoid changes in a few steps.
  • Inside the valley the slope is gentle again, so it slowly creeps towards convergence, with the two points almost, but not quite, on the curve.

The colour coding replaces the level labels: dark red is high loss (bad), dark blue is low loss (good). Run it, or click anywhere on the map to start from there.

From now on the lecture mostly drops the 3-D view: the contour map, with colour for the loss level and ring spacing for the slope, tells the whole story. The next lecture uses it to show how momentum escapes the plateau.

Going beyond the video: rings and the gradient. The gradient at a point is always perpendicular to the ring through that point (moving along a ring doesn't change the loss), and its length is the slope, roughly $\Delta z/d$. So gradient descent steps cut straight across the rings, and the step $\eta\,\lVert\nabla\rVert$ is long where rings are crowded and short where they are sparse. That's the whole behaviour of the demo above in one sentence. See also the gradient-descent notes in my ML series, ML L31.

Key takeaways

  1. A contour map is the top view of a 3-D surface sliced at regular intervals along the vertical (loss) axis.
  2. Every ring joins points of equal loss; colour (red high, blue low) can replace the level labels.
  3. Rings far apart mean a gentle slope; rings close together mean a steep slope, since $d\approx\Delta z/\text{slope}$.
  4. Gradient descent's behaviour is visible on the map: tiny steps where rings are sparse (plateau, near the minimum), big jumps where they are crowded.
  5. Given a contour map you can picture the 3-D surface, and vice versa.
