Stochastic, Mini-Batch and Batch Gradient Descent

Batch gradient descent looks at every training point before making a single update: exact gradient, theoretical guarantees, but painfully slow on a million points. Stochastic gradient descent updates after every point: many cheap updates, but each is a noisy estimate, so the path oscillates. Mini-batch sits in between, and it is what everyone uses in practice, for gradient descent, momentum and NAG alike.

1 · What our code has been doing: batch gradient descent

Look again at the gradient descent code (and its variants for momentum and NAG). There are two loops. The inner loop goes over all training points, computing each one's gradient and accumulating it in dw, db; only after the loop is there one update.

Why? Because that is the true gradient. The loss is a sum (or average) over all $N$ training examples, so its derivative is too:

$$\mathscr{L}(w,b)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathscr{L}_i(w,b)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\nabla w=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_i}{\partial w}.$$

No approximation is made, so all the theoretical guarantees hold: the Taylor-series argument of gradient descent promised that the loss decreases at every step (for a small enough $\eta$), and that promise is about this exact gradient.

The flip side. With a million training points you compute a million derivatives to make one update. Running for 100 steps (tiny by modern standards) costs 100 million gradient computations, and after 100 steps the weights have hardly moved: nowhere near convergence.

2 · Stochastic gradient descent

A small change to the code: move the update inside the loop over points. Now for every point we compute its derivative and immediately update the weights, a greedy update.

for epoch in 1 … max_epochs: for (x, y) in data: # shuffled dw, db = grad_w(w, b, x, y), grad_b(w, b, x, y) w = w − η·dw; b = b − η·db # update after every point

With a million points that is a million updates per epoch. (One epoch is one pass over the entire data; one step is one update.)

The cost: the true derivative is an average over all points, and we are pretending a single point's derivative is as good as that average. It is stochastic because we estimate the total gradient from one data point, so the guarantees no longer hold exactly.

The coin analogy. To estimate the probability of heads of a biased coin you wouldn't toss it once ("heads, so P = 1"). You also don't need a million tosses. A hundred or a thousand give a fair estimate. Likewise: waiting for all $N$ points is wasteful, one point is too noisy, and something in between is the sweet spot.

3 · Why SGD oscillates

Run SGD and batch gradient descent on the same loss surface. Batch descent (green) goes smoothly downhill. SGD (black) often goes off course and comes back. Each point acts independently, pushing the parameters in the direction that is most convenient for itself. A move that's good for one point may be bad for the next, which pushes back. They're not working together, while in batch descent the update follows the consensus (the average) of all the points.

4 · Mini-batch gradient descent

Better estimates mean fewer oscillations. Keep a count of the points seen, and update only after every $k$ points (say $k=25$, 100 or 200), averaging their gradients. This is like estimating the coin's bias from 25 tosses instead of one. The larger $k$, the more accurate the estimate and the smoother the path; at $k=N$ it is batch gradient descent. But we want $k$ much smaller than $N$.

5 · Stochastic versions of momentum and NAG

The same idea applies to every algorithm: indent the update lines into the loop (stochastic), or update after every batch (mini-batch). Switch the demo to momentum or NAG. You now see two kinds of oscillation:

  • the full-batch path still oscillates in the steep valley, because of momentum: it moves fast and overshoots;
  • the stochastic path adds oscillations from the noisy estimates. It can even head off in a different direction at the start, because the first few points it saw weren't representative of the total gradient, and then must course-correct.

The relative strengths survive: stochastic NAG still makes shorter U-turns than stochastic momentum, and both still converge much faster than stochastic gradient descent (in the lecture, after 500 steps stochastic GD had not even left the plateau while both had converged). The mini-batch versions behave the same, with the stochastic oscillations reduced.

6 · In practice

  • You use the mini-batch version of every optimisation algorithm: with lots of training data you can't wait for all of it before each update, and single points are too noisy.
  • Batch sizes are usually powers of two. In NLP with Transformers, sizes around 4096 are popular, and results can be quite sensitive to the batch size (1024 vs 4096 can give very different results). The common wisdom: larger batches are better (short of the full data).
  • There is nothing to memorise: you'll pick up sensible batch sizes for your application from papers and existing implementations.
Going beyond the video: how much does a batch help? If per-point gradients have variance $\sigma^2$ around the true gradient, the average of $k$ independent ones has variance $\sigma^2/k$: the noise (standard deviation) shrinks only like $1/\sqrt{k}$. Going from $k=1$ to $25$ cuts the noise 5×; going from 25 to 100 only 2× more, at 4× the cost per step. Hence the "somewhere in between" sweet spot.

Key takeaways

  1. Batch (vanilla) GD sums the gradient over all $N$ points: exact, guaranteed, but one update per epoch.
  2. SGD updates after every point: $N$ updates per epoch, but each uses a one-point estimate, so the path oscillates as points pull in their own directions.
  3. Mini-batch GD averages $B$ points per update: $N/B$ updates per epoch, much smoother than SGD; larger $B$ means smoother.
  4. Stochastic/mini-batch versions of momentum and NAG keep their relative advantages and add sampling noise.
  5. In practice: mini-batch versions everywhere, with batch sizes taken from the literature.
