Learning-Rate Schedules, Momentum Schedules and Line Search

Why not just crank up the learning rate to cross the plateaus? Because a large $\eta$ also multiplies the large gradients in steep regions and you overshoot. A good $\eta$ is found by a log-scale search, and then annealed as training progresses: by steps, by watching the validation loss, or by an exponential or $1/t$ decay. Momentum can be scheduled too, upwards. Line search avoids committing to one $\eta$: at each step, try several and keep the best.

1 · Why not simply a larger learning rate?

Back to the first problem: on a flat surface the gradients are tiny, so gradient descent crawls. Instead of momentum or NAG, couldn't we just increase $\eta$? A small gradient times a large $\eta$ is still a big step, and "learning rate = how fast you move" makes this sound reasonable.

The lecture sets $\eta=10$ instead of 1: it moves fast, but oscillates again. On the gentle part we want speed; in the steep part the gradient is already large, and multiplying it by a large $\eta$ throws us across the valley. What we really want is something that adapts to the slope: larger steps where the slope is small, smaller where it is large. That's what the adaptive algorithms (from L36 on) will do. For now: just raising $\eta$ is not the answer.

Try it on the toy neuron (through $(0.5,\,0.2)$ and $(2.5,\,0.9)$), with several learning rates on a log scale plus one of your own:

Push "your η" to 25–50 and the trouble appears: the step that enters the steep valley is so large that it is thrown right across it, sometimes onto another plateau where it is stuck. 2 · Choosing the initial learning rate

Context first: for most problems (machine translation, speech recognition, image recognition…) you build on existing work. Someone has trained similar models; follow their hyperparameters and experiment in a small window around them. That's the best thing to do. The tips below are for a genuinely new problem with nothing to go by:

  1. Try learning rates on a log scale: 0.0001, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, …
  2. Run each for only a few epochs and watch the loss curve. Too-high rates make the loss increase (they overshoot into high-loss regions); good ones make it decrease smoothly.
  3. Say 0.1 looks best. Zoom in with a finer, linear search around it: 0.05, 0.2, 0.3, …

These are heuristics, not a guaranteed recipe. The point is: if your loss doesn't converge or keeps increasing, experiment with $\eta$ this way. The loss panel of the demo above is exactly this experiment in miniature.

3 · Annealing the learning rate

At the start the weights are random, so a slightly high $\eta$ may be fine. Near the minimum a high $\eta$ risks overshooting it. So anneal: reduce $\eta$ as training progresses.

  • Step decay. Halve $\eta$ after a fixed number of epochs (every 5 or 10). The number of epochs serves as a proxy for "I am probably close to the minimum now".
  • Validation-driven halving (Prof. Khapra's personal choice). Keep a validation set and save the weights after every epoch.
    1. After each epoch compute the validation loss. While it decreases, keep $\eta$.
    2. If it increases after, say, epoch 7: throw away epoch 7's updates, restore the weights saved after epoch 6, halve $\eta$ and rerun the epoch.
    3. If it still increases, roll back and halve again. If it keeps increasing, you have probably converged: stop.
    The updates follow the training data, so when the validation loss rises the updates no longer help generalisation, and you make them less aggressive.
  • Exponential decay: $\eta_t=\eta_0\,e^{-kt}$.
  • $1/t$ decay: $\eta_t=\dfrac{\eta_0}{1+kt}$.

The catch with the last two: besides $\eta_0$ you now need a new hyperparameter $k$, which controls how fast the decay happens (large $k$: $\eta$ collapses almost at once; small $k$: it decays smoothly). That makes tuning harder, which is why the lecturer doesn't recommend them and prefers the validation-loss rule.

The transcript garbles the exponential formula; $\eta_0e^{-kt}$ is the standard form and matches the description ("at $t=0$ the rate is $\eta_0$, then it decays exponentially, faster for larger $k$").

4 · Scheduling the momentum

Momentum can be adjusted too. The lecture shows a schedule from a paper (Sutskever et al., 2013) that looks complicated but is easy to decode:

$$\beta_t=\min\!\Big(1-2^{-1-\log_2(\lfloor t/250\rfloor+1)},\ \beta_{\max}\Big),$$

with $\beta_{\max}$ typically one of 0.999, 0.995, 0.99 or 0.9 (or 0, which means no momentum).

  1. $t=0$: $\log_2(0+1)=0$, so $1-2^{-1}=0.5$. With $\beta_{\max}=0.9$ we get $\beta=0.5$.
  2. $t=250$: $\log_2 2=1$, so $1-2^{-2}=0.75$.
  3. $t=750$: $\log_2 4=2$, so $1-2^{-3}=0.875$. It keeps creeping up, until $\beta_{\max}$ caps it.

So $\beta$ increases as training progresses. Early on, the history is still being built and is as unreliable as the current gradient, so it shouldn't get much weight. Later, the history has led you into this region over many updates; near the minimum one faulty update could knock you away, so you trust the history more than the current gradient.

5 · Line search

The problem with one fixed $\eta$: you are married to it. Too high and you oscillate; too low and you crawl. Line search tries a bunch of learning rates at every step:

  1. Compute the derivative $\nabla w_t$ once (it does not depend on $\eta$).
  2. For each candidate $\eta\in\{0.1,\,0.5,\,1,\,2,\,\dots,\,10\}$ compute a candidate $w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\nabla w_t$ (same for $b$).
  3. Plug every candidate into the loss function and keep the one with the smallest loss. Throw the others away; repeat at the next step.

The price: with 10 candidates every step does 10 updates and 10 loss evaluations. The benefit: it adapts to the region. On flat surfaces the large learning rates win; when entering the steep valley a large $\eta$ would overshoot and raise the loss, so a small one gets selected.

As in the lecture's run: line search moves very quickly across the flat region, and it doesn't fly out of the valley, because there the larger rates would increase the loss and aren't picked. It converges much faster than gradient descent with $\eta=1$. There are some oscillations, but nothing like momentum's or NAG's.

Going beyond the video. This is a crude form of exact line search over a grid. Practical versions (backtracking with the Armijo condition) start from a large $\eta$ and halve it until the loss drops "enough", which usually needs only a few loss evaluations. Line search is rare in deep learning because each extra loss evaluation is a forward pass over the batch, and with mini-batches the loss itself is noisy. The adaptive methods coming next give a per-parameter, cheap version of the same idea.

Key takeaways

  1. Simply raising $\eta$ speeds up the plateaus but causes overshooting in steep regions; we really want a slope-adaptive rate.
  2. With no prior work to follow, try $\eta$ on a log scale for a few epochs, keep the one whose loss falls smoothly, then refine linearly around it.
  3. Anneal $\eta$: step decay, validation-driven halving with rollback (the lecturer's choice), or exponential/$1/t$ decay (which add a decay hyperparameter $k$).
  4. Momentum can be scheduled upwards, e.g. $\beta_t=\min(1-2^{-1-\log_2(\lfloor t/250\rfloor+1)},\beta_{\max})$: 0.5, 0.75, 0.875, …
  5. Line search tries several $\eta$ each step and keeps the lowest loss: big steps on plateaus, small ones near the valley, at several times the cost per step.
