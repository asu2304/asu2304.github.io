RMSProp: A History That Forgets

AdaGrad's denominator only ever grows, so learning rates die. RMSProp replaces the running sum with an exponentially decaying average. The history can then shrink again, and the effective learning rate can rise on flat ground. The price is that the rate can settle to a constant and the iterates oscillate near the minimum, so RMSProp is sensitive to the initial learning rate $\eta_0$.

1 · The problem: AdaGrad's history grows without limit

In AdaGrad (L37) the history $v_t$ just keeps adding squared gradients. Frequently updated (dense) parameters therefore get ever smaller updates, until they hardly move. Can we slow the growth of the denominator?

2 · RMSProp: take only a fraction, and decay the past

$$v_t=\beta\,v_{t-1}+(1-\beta)(\nabla w_t)^2$$ $$w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{v_t+\epsilon}}\,\nabla w_t$$ with a typical $\beta=0.9$, and the same for $b$.

Only the history line changes. In code, v_w = v_w + dw**2 becomes v_w = beta*v_w + (1-beta)*dw**2. Both multipliers are less than 1. At every step the whole accumulated history is scaled by 0.9, and only 0.1 of the new squared gradient is added. Unrolling with $\beta=0.9$:

AdaGradRMSProp ($\beta=0.9$)
$v_0$$(\nabla w_0)^2$$0.1(\nabla w_0)^2$
$v_1$$(\nabla w_0)^2+(\nabla w_1)^2$$0.09(\nabla w_0)^2+0.1(\nabla w_1)^2$
$v_2$$(\nabla w_0)^2+(\nabla w_1)^2+(\nabla w_2)^2$$0.081(\nabla w_0)^2+0.09(\nabla w_1)^2+0.1(\nabla w_2)^2$

In general, the same kind of formula we met for momentum (L32):

$$v_t=(1-\beta)\sum_{\tau=0}^{t}\beta^{\,t-\tau}(\nabla w_\tau)^2 .$$

The takeaway: $v_t$ is an exponentially decaying average, so the denominator grows less aggressively and the effective learning rate $\eta/\sqrt{v_t+\epsilon}$ decays more slowly.

3 · Running it: faster, but it oscillates

On the same sparse toy problem RMSProp reaches the minimum quickly. Its rate isn't being killed, and it follows the hypotenuse: proportionate moves in $w$ and $b$ even though the $w$-gradients are small. But once there it oscillates around the minimum. Even after the sigmoid fitted the points, the iterate kept moving. Two questions follow: why would the learning rate become constant, and why would a constant rate cause oscillations?

4 · Why the rate can become constant: the history can shrink

In AdaGrad, $v_t$ rises and then saturates once the gradients reach zero, but it never decreases. In RMSProp the plotted $v_t$ rises, then falls. How can an "accumulation" decrease?

Because of the exponential weighting, $v_t$ behaves like a moving window. Old terms get weights $0.1,\,0.09,\,0.081,\dots$ that keep shrinking. Once the current gradients are small, the new sum is less than the previous one. When the gradients are all near zero, the history levels off and barely changes from one step to the next. Then $\eta/\sqrt{v_t}$ barely changes either. So RMSProp's effective rate can increase, decrease, or stay constant.

5 · Why a constant rate can oscillate

Take a curve and a point on one side of the minimum. Compute the slope and step against it. With the right (or wrong) rate you land at the symmetrically opposite point. The slope there has the same magnitude and the opposite sign. With the rate unchanged, you step straight back, and so on forever. This needn't always happen, but it can. If the derivative is only slightly different, you still go back and forth instead of directly to the minimum. In AdaGrad this couldn't persist, because the ever-growing history kept shrinking the rate.

A constant learning rate on the curve $L(w)=w^2$: each step is $w\leftarrow w-\eta\cdot 2w$. Slide $\eta$ and watch where the iterates go.

6 · The fix is the initial learning rate, and that's the problem

The effective rate is $\eta_0/\sqrt{v_t+\epsilon}$, where the numerator $\eta_0$ is the initial learning rate we choose and it stays constant throughout. Everything else equal (same loss surface, same points), Prof. Khapra compares three values:

$\eta_0$what happens
0.6wide oscillation between two high-loss points; the sigmoid is nowhere near the data
0.1slower progress, still oscillating, but between two low-loss points: an acceptable solution
0.05no oscillation; it settles into the minimum

The message is not "tune $\eta_0$ and RMSProp works". The point is the opposite: RMSProp is sensitive to the initial learning rate. Converging, settling for an acceptable solution and staying far off can all happen depending on $\eta_0$. For well-studied benchmarks (say machine translation), people know a good range from many experiments. For a new data set, you don't.

7 · How the effective rate follows the surface

The lecture animates $v_t$, $\eta_0$ and (something proportional to) the effective rate with $\eta_0=0.6$:

  • Going down steep regions, the current gradients are large, so $v_t$ keeps increasing and the effective rate steadily decreases. That is what we want on steep ground.
  • Entering flat regions, the new gradients are tiny and the old large ones are multiplied by large powers of $\beta$. After a tipping point $v_t$ starts decreasing, so the effective rate increases. Also what we want.

With $\eta_0=0.1$ the curve has the same shape, but scaled down. It decreases more slowly, always stays below the lowest point of the 0.6 curve, and on flat ground never rises as high. That's why the oscillations are milder; with 0.05 they're smaller still. How fast the rate adapts depends on $\eta_0$. In the demo, choose $b$ and compare the blue rate curve for the different $\eta_0$ buttons.

8 · No single $\eta_0$ is right everywhere

Suppose you found a "good" $\eta_0$. Even that isn't guaranteed to stay good along the same loss surface. The lecture's illustration divides by $v_t$ directly (it drops the square root to keep the arithmetic simple):

where you are$v_t$$\eta_0=0.6$$\eta_0=0.1$preferred
steep region1.250.480.08small rate, so $\eta_0=0.1$
flat region0.1highlowlarge rate, so $\eta_0=0.6$

So the better $\eta_0$ changes as you move, but you choose it once at the start and you're stuck with it. We want the numerator to change with the gradients too. Numerator and denominator should adjust together, giving a small effective rate on steep ground and a large one on flat ground. Right now only the denominator adapts.

9 · Summary: where AdaGrad and RMSProp stand

drawbackAdaGradRMSProp
sensitive to the initial learning rateyesyes
large initial gradients make $v$ large early, so the rate is tiny from the start and never recoversyes ($v_t$ never decreases)alleviated (old terms decay)
on reaching a gentle region, no way to raise the rate againyes (seen as the slow crawl in $b$ near the minimum)resolved: $v_t$ can fall, so the rate rises

The remaining problem, dependence on $\eta_0$, is what Adadelta removes (L39).

Going beyond the video. RMSProp was never formally published; it comes from Geoffrey Hinton's Coursera lecture slides (2012), which suggested $\beta=0.9$ and a base rate around 0.001 for neural networks. In modern libraries it is mostly used with momentum (or replaced by Adam, L40). When a run "trains, then jitters around a plateau of loss", lowering the base rate or adding a decay schedule is the usual cure, exactly the sensitivity this lecture shows.

Key takeaways

  1. RMSProp: $v_t=\beta v_{t-1}+(1-\beta)(\nabla w_t)^2$, with the same per-parameter update $\eta/\sqrt{v_t+\epsilon}$ as AdaGrad. Only the history changes.
  2. The exponentially decaying average grows slowly and can shrink, so the rate decreases on steep ground and increases on flat ground.
  3. Near the minimum the history levels off and the rate becomes roughly constant. A constant rate can bounce between points on either side of the minimum.
  4. The outcome (converge, acceptable oscillation, or far off) depends on $\eta_0$; 0.6, 0.1 and 0.05 gave all three in the lecture.
  5. The ideal $\eta_0$ differs between steep and flat regions, so the numerator should adapt too. That leads to Adadelta.
