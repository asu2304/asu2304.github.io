Lecture 4 · A brief history of deep learning

From Cats to Convolutional Neural Networks

In 1959 Hubel and Wiesel showed a cat bars of light and found neurons that fire only for an edge at one orientation, in one small patch of the visual field. That idea (small local detectors, the same detector repeated everywhere, stacked in layers) became the Neocognitron, then LeCun's LeNet, then the CNNs that won ImageNet.

1 · The timeline

  • 1959Hubel and Wiesel's experiment on the cat visual cortex: individual neurons respond to simple features (edges, bars at a particular orientation) inside a small receptive field. Some cells ("complex cells") respond to the feature anywhere in a slightly larger area. Nobel Prize, 1981.
  • 1980Neocognitron (Kunihiko Fukushima): a layered network of "S-cells" (local feature detectors) and "C-cells" (pooling over position), directly inspired by the cat experiments.
  • 1989Handwritten zip codes (LeCun et al.): a convolutional network trained end-to-end with backpropagation reads digits on US mail.
  • 1998LeNet-5 (LeCun, Bottou, Bengio, Haffner): "Gradient-based learning applied to document recognition"; the MNIST dataset; deployed to read cheques.
  • 2012AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, Hinton): a deeper CNN on GPUs wins ImageNet by a wide margin (L3).

2 · What a convolution does

A filter (kernel) is a small grid of weights. Slide it over the image; at each position, multiply the overlapping pixels by the weights and add them up:

$$S(i,j)=\sum_{a}\sum_{b} I(i+a,\,j+b)\,K(a,b).$$

Each output pixel depends only on a small patch of the input (its receptive field, as in the cat), and the same weights are reused at every position (one detector, repeated everywhere). Pick a filter and an image below; click a pixel of the output to see the patch it looked at.

Output colour: blue = large positive response, orange = large negative, pale = near zero.

Hubel and Wiesel's simple cells are edge filters; a CNN learns its filters from data instead of being handed them. Early layers of trained CNNs end up looking a lot like the edge detectors above, and later layers combine them into shapes and objects (V1 → V2 → AIT again, from L1).
Going beyond the video. Strictly, the formula above is cross-correlation; true convolution flips the kernel. Deep learning libraries use cross-correlation and call it convolution, and since the kernel is learnt the difference doesn't matter. CNNs proper come later in the course.

Key takeaways

  1. Hubel and Wiesel (1959): neurons with small receptive fields that detect oriented edges.
  2. Neocognitron (1980) → LeCun's convnets (1989, LeNet-5 in 1998) → AlexNet (2012).
  3. A convolution slides one small set of weights over the image: local connectivity plus weight sharing.
