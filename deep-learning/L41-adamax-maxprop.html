MaxProp and Adamax: Swapping the L2 Norm for the Max Norm

Adam's $v_t$ is an exponentially weighted L2 norm of past gradients. Push $p$ in the $L^p$ norm all the way to infinity and the norm becomes a plain max: cheap, numerically safe, and immune to the zero-initialisation bias. Use it for $v_t$ and you get MaxProp (from RMSProp) and Adamax (from Adam), whose learning rate barely moves when a sparse feature gives zero gradients.

1 · The $L^p$ norm, and why large $p$ is awkward

For a vector $x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)$,

$$\lVert x\rVert_p=\Big(\sum_{i=1}^n |x_i|^p\Big)^{1/p}.$$

$p=2$ is the familiar L2 norm: square, add, take the square root. The lecture recalls why we care: when Adam was introduced (L40), $v_t$ was described as an exponentially weighted L2 norm of the gradient history. Now we ask whether some other norm would do.

Picture the vectors of norm 1. For $L^1$ they form a diamond: for example $(0.5,-0.5)$ lies on it, since $|0.5|+|-0.5|=1$. For $L^2$ they form the circle $x_1^2+x_2^2=1$. As $p$ grows the shape bulges out towards a square.

The trouble with large $p$ ($3, 4, 10, 100,\dots$) is numerical. A small value raised to a large power underflows towards 0; a value above 1 raised to a large power blows up. That is one reason L2 is the usual choice.

2 · As $p\to\infty$, the norm is just the max

Something useful happens in the limit: $\lVert x\rVert_\infty=\max_i |x_i|$. The lecture's proof outline:

  1. Without loss of generality let $|x_1|$ be the largest entry.
  2. Factor it out: $$\Big(\sum_i |x_i|^p\Big)^{1/p}=|x_1|\Big(1+\Big|\tfrac{x_2}{x_1}\Big|^p+\dots+\Big|\tfrac{x_n}{x_1}\Big|^p\Big)^{1/p}.$$
  3. Every ratio $|x_i/x_1|$ is below 1, so as $p\to\infty$ each term $|x_i/x_1|^p\to0$.
  4. What is left is $|x_1|\cdot 1^{1/p}=|x_1|$, the maximum entry.
$\lVert x\rVert_\infty=\max(|x_1|,\dots,|x_n|)$. No powers, no roots, so no numerical instability.

3 · Reading Adam's $v_t$ as a norm

Recall Adam's second-moment history, started at zero:

$$v_t=\beta_2 v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)(\nabla w_t)^2 .$$

Unrolled from time step 0, this is

$$v_t=(1-\beta_2)\big(\beta_2^{\,t}(\nabla w_0)^2+\beta_2^{\,t-1}(\nabla w_1)^2+\dots+(\nabla w_t)^2\big).$$

Ignore the $\beta$'s for a moment: this is a sum of squared gradients, and the update divides by its square root. So $\sqrt{v_t}$ is an exponentially weighted L2 norm of the gradient history. The question: can we use the $L^\infty$ norm instead, and what does it buy us?

4 · The max-norm history

Replace the weighted sum by a weighted max. Because a max of a max is a max, the history collapses to a pairwise rule: keep the decayed old max, or take the new gradient if it is bigger.

$$v_t=\max\big(\beta_2\,v_{t-1},\ |\nabla w_t|\big)$$

Three things change compared with Adam:

  • No $(1-\beta_2)$ in front of the current gradient. The history still decays exponentially (it is multiplied by $\beta_2$ every step), but the current gradient enters unscaled.
  • No square root. The root belonged to the L2 norm; the $L^\infty$ norm has already taken its "$p$-th root" and come out as the max.
  • No bias correction. With $v_{-1}=0$, the first step gives $v_0=\max(0,|\nabla w_0|)=|\nabla w_0|$, the true size of the gradient, not a value shrunk towards 0. The max is not susceptible to the zero-initialisation bias. (Its curve is more zig-zag than the smooth L2 average, because it jumps straight to each new maximum.)

5 · Why bother? Sparse features

The lecture's scenario: we initialise $w$ so that the first gradient is large, and the next few gradients are 0. This is the familiar sparse-feature situation that motivated adaptive learning rates: the input $x$ for this weight is 0 on those steps, so its gradient is 0.

When the gradient is 0 we have learned nothing new at that step, so ideally the effective learning rate should not change.

With the max, a zero gradient leaves $v_t=\beta_2v_{t-1}$: only the slow decay of the history. Now the lecture's worked example: every alternate gradient is 0 (gradients $1,0,1,0,\dots$), $\eta_0=1$, $\beta_2=0.999$, and the effective learning rate is $\eta_t=\eta_0/v_t$ (with $\varepsilon$ dropped for clarity).

The max version keeps the learning rate between 1 and about 1.001: effectively constant, which is what we want. The L2 version jumps to about 1.4 on every zero-gradient step and back down on the next, changing the learning rate for no reason. (On the slide, step 2's max is written as $\max(0.999,1)$; strictly it is $\max(0.999\times0.999,1)$, which is still 1.)

So if you have many sparse features and don't want their learning rates jerked around by the zero steps, the max norm is the better history. Try it, including the lecture's first scenario (one big gradient, then zeros):

6 · The update rules

MaxProp is RMSProp with the max-norm history. That is the whole change:

$$v_t=\max\big(\beta\,v_{t-1},\ |\nabla w_t|\big),\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta}{v_t+\varepsilon}\,\nabla w_t .$$

Adamax applies the same idea to Adam, and is an algorithm actually used in practice. Everything about momentum stays as in Adam; only $v_t$ changes, and it needs no bias correction.

Going beyond the video. Adamax comes from the original Adam paper (Kingma & Ba, 2015), which derives it exactly this way, as the $p\to\infty$ limit of an $L^p$ version of Adam; their suggested defaults are $\eta=0.002$, $\beta_1=0.9$, $\beta_2=0.999$. Most libraries ship it (e.g. torch.optim.Adamax). In PyTorch, $\varepsilon$ sits inside the max rather than being added to $v_t$, a detail that only matters when gradients are exactly 0 from the start.

Key takeaways

  1. $\lVert x\rVert_\infty=\max_i|x_i|$: factor out the largest entry and every other ratio raised to $p\to\infty$ vanishes. Unlike large finite $p$, it is numerically safe.
  2. Adam's $\sqrt{v_t}$ is an exponentially weighted L2 norm of past gradients; Adamax replaces it with the weighted max $v_t=\max(\beta_2v_{t-1},|\nabla w_t|)$.
  3. The max history needs no $(1-\beta_2)$ factor, no square root and no bias correction: it starts at the true gradient size.
  4. On zero-gradient (sparse-feature) steps the max keeps the learning rate almost constant; the bias-corrected L2 history makes it jump (1 → 1.41 → 1.22 … in the lecture's example).
  5. MaxProp = RMSProp with the max norm; Adamax = Adam with the max norm. Only the $v_t$ line changes.
