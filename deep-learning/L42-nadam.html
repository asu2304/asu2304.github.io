Nadam: Adam with a Nesterov Look-Ahead

Adam already uses momentum, and NAG is known to beat plain momentum. Nadam puts NAG's look-ahead into Adam. The trick is to first rewrite NAG so that the look-ahead is done one step early, inside the update itself. Then all of Adam's equations stay the same except the last one.

1 · The obvious extension

Adam (L40) combines a momentum term $m_t$ with an adaptive history $v_t$. We know from L33 that Nesterov accelerated gradient (NAG) improves on momentum-based gradient descent (L32) by computing the gradient at a look-ahead point, so it overshoots less. So: can we give Adam the Nesterov effect? That is Nadam, Nesterov-accelerated adaptive moment estimation.

Adam's update, the starting point:

$$\begin{aligned} m_t&=\beta_1m_{t-1}+(1-\beta_1)\nabla w_t, &\hat m_t&=\frac{m_t}{1-\beta_1^{\,t}},\\ v_t&=\beta_2v_{t-1}+(1-\beta_2)(\nabla w_t)^2, &\hat v_t&=\frac{v_t}{1-\beta_2^{\,t}},\\ w_{t+1}&=w_t-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat v_t}+\varepsilon}\,\hat m_t . \end{aligned}$$

A note on notation. Momentum can be written as $m_t=\beta m_{t-1}+\nabla w_t$ (as when momentum was introduced) or as $m_t=\beta m_{t-1}+(1-\beta)\nabla w_t$ (as in Adam). Both forms are popular and interchangeable. The lecture starts with the first, because it makes the look-ahead easier to see, and switches to the second when it reaches Nadam.

2 · NAG as we wrote it

The main idea of NAG: don't take the gradient at the current $w_t$. First move by the history, then take the gradient there.

$$\begin{aligned} g_t&=\nabla f\big(w_t-\beta m_{t-1}\big) &&\text{(gradient at the look-ahead point)}\\ m_t&=\beta m_{t-1}+\eta\,g_t\\ w_{t+1}&=w_t-m_t \end{aligned}$$

Two things are awkward:

  • Three time indices, $t-1$, $t$ and $t+1$, appear in one update.
  • $m_{t-1}$ is used twice. We make a temporary move $w_t-\beta m_{t-1}$, take a gradient there, then throw that temporary point away and go back to $w_t$, only to subtract $\beta m_{t-1}$ again inside $m_t$.

The lecture's walk-through with $\eta=1$ and $m_{-1}=0$:

  1. Step 0: $g_0=\nabla f(w_0-\beta\cdot0)=\nabla w_0$, so $m_0=g_0$ and $w_1=w_0-m_0$.
  2. Step 1: look ahead to $w_1-\beta m_0$ and compute $g_1$ there. Then $m_1=\beta m_0+g_1$ and $w_2=w_1-m_1=w_1-\beta m_0-g_1$.
  3. Notice: the temporary point $w_1-\beta m_0$ is built in step 1, yet $\beta m_0$ was already known at the end of step 0. We could have moved there in advance and handed step 1 a $w$ that already contains the look-ahead.

3 · Rewriting NAG: do the look-ahead one step early

Push the look-ahead into the update itself. The gradient is then taken at the current $w_t$ (no temporary point), and the final update moves by "history plus current gradient":

$$\begin{aligned} g_{t+1}&=\nabla w_t\\ m_{t+1}&=\beta m_t+g_{t+1}\\ w_{t+1}&=w_t-\eta\big(\beta m_{t+1}+g_{t+1}\big) \end{aligned}$$

Only $t$ and $t+1$ appear, and the new $w$ is computed once per step instead of twice. The lecture's analogy: it is like peeling one iteration out of the front of a while-loop, so every computation inside the loop runs one step behind and the look-ahead is already done when the loop body starts. The look-ahead now lives entirely in the momentum step, in the $\beta m_{t+1}$ term of the last line.

Check that nothing has changed. The demo runs both versions on $f(w)=\tfrac12w^2$. The rewritten $w_t$ is exactly the original's look-ahead point $w_t-\beta m_{t-1}$, the point where the original takes its gradient:

4 · The Nadam update

Bring the rewritten NAG to Adam, now with the $(1-\beta)$ form of momentum. The first four lines are Adam's, exactly (written with index $t+1$ for the new quantities and $\nabla w_t$ for the gradient at the current point):

$$\begin{aligned} m_{t+1}&=\beta_1m_t+(1-\beta_1)\nabla w_t, &\hat m_{t+1}&=\frac{m_{t+1}}{1-\beta_1^{\,t+1}},\\ v_{t+1}&=\beta_2v_t+(1-\beta_2)(\nabla w_t)^2, &\hat v_{t+1}&=\frac{v_{t+1}}{1-\beta_2^{\,t+1}}, \end{aligned}$$

and only the last line changes, to carry the look-ahead:

$$w_{t+1}=w_t-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat v_{t+1}}+\varepsilon}\left(\beta_1\hat m_{t+1}+\frac{(1-\beta_1)\,\nabla w_t}{1-\beta_1^{\,t+1}}\right)$$

How to read it:

  1. Strip the multiplying factors and it is $w_t-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{\hat v_{t+1}}+\varepsilon}\big(\hat m_{t+1}+\nabla w_t\big)$: move by the history and the current gradient, exactly the last line of rewritten NAG.
  2. $\beta_1$ and $(1-\beta_1)$ are the weights of the history and the current gradient in the $(1-\beta)$ form of momentum, which the NAG derivation above did not have.
  3. The denominator $1-\beta_1^{\,t+1}$ on the gradient term is the same bias correction that turned $m_{t+1}$ into $\hat m_{t+1}$.

Prof. Khapra admits the equation is a bit messy. The takeaway is simple: add the Nesterov look-ahead to Adam, and write NAG so the look-ahead is pushed into the last equation, so that everything else stays identical to Adam.

6 · Which optimiser should you use?

  • The default in most papers is Adam. If you don't know anything else, go with Adam.
  • Some works (a few years old now) point out issues with Adam. Still, in the practical work Prof. Khapra has seen, Adam or one of its variants (Nadam is another good choice) is the common pick.
  • People report that plain gradient descent, or momentum, can match Adam if you really know how to set the initial learning rate, the momentum value and the learning-rate schedule. That expertise takes a while to build. Without it, use Adam off the shelf.
Going beyond the video. Nadam is from Dozat (2016), "Incorporating Nesterov Momentum into Adam". The issues with Adam alluded to are, for example, its convergence counter-examples (fixed by AMSGrad) and the way it interacts with L2 regularisation (fixed by AdamW's decoupled weight decay). AdamW is the usual default for training Transformers today. The schedules that go with any of these optimisers are the next lecture, L43.

Key takeaways

  1. Nadam = Adam + Nesterov look-ahead. All of Adam's equations stay; only the final update changes.
  2. Original NAG takes the gradient at a temporary point $w_t-\beta m_{t-1}$, uses $m_{t-1}$ twice and involves three time indices.
  3. Rewritten NAG takes the gradient at $w_t$ and updates with $\beta m_{t+1}+g_{t+1}$: the look-ahead is done one step early. Its iterates are exactly the original's look-ahead points.
  4. Nadam's last line moves by the bias-corrected history and the bias-corrected current gradient: $\beta_1\hat m_{t+1}+\frac{(1-\beta_1)\nabla w_t}{1-\beta_1^{t+1}}$, scaled by $\eta/(\sqrt{\hat v_{t+1}}+\varepsilon)$.
  5. In practice: Adam (or a variant like Nadam) is a safe default; tuned SGD with momentum and a good schedule can match it, but needs expertise.
