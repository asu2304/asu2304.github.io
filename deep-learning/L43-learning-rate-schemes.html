Learning-Rate Schemes: Cyclical, Cosine Annealing, Warm Restarts and Warm-Up

A learning rate that only ever decays can leave training stuck near a saddle point, with a step size too small to get out. Adaptive methods can raise the rate there, but they pay for it by keeping histories. A cheaper idea is to make the schedule itself go up again: cyclically (triangular), with cosine annealing and warm restarts, or with a warm-up at the start, as Transformers do.

2 · The problem: saddle points

Picture a saddle-shaped loss surface. Looking along one direction, a point at the end of a valley looks like a minimum. Looking along the other direction, you could still go further down: a better minimum lies beyond. Deep-learning loss surfaces are not convex, and multiple papers report that when training stalls or slows, it is often because it is stuck near such a saddle point.

Now combine that with a decaying learning rate. We initialise at some point, come down the valley nicely, and by the time we reach the saddle the learning rate has decayed and become very small. The gradient there is small too, so the steps are tiny and we stay stuck.

What if the learning rate were allowed to increase again after some iterations? Then there is at least a chance of escaping the saddle.

Adaptive methods already try to do this: they raise the effective learning rate where gradients are small. But they keep a history per parameter and divide by it, which adds computation and complexity. The schemes below get the same effect from a simple schedule with no history at all.

3 · Cyclical learning rate (triangular)

Alternate: start small, go up to a high value, come back down, go up again, and so on. If you are in a bad region with a low rate, you just wait out the cycle until the rate grows and pushes you out. If you need a small rate, it will come back within a few steps.

$$\eta_t=\eta_{\min}+(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\cdot\max\!\Big(0,\ 1-\Big|\frac{t}{\mu}-2\Big\lfloor1+\frac{t}{2\mu}\Big\rfloor+1\Big|\Big)$$

Here $\mu$ is the half-period: the number of steps to climb from $\eta_{\min}$ to $\eta_{\max}$ (and then as many to come back). The lecture's example: $\mu=20$, $\eta_{\max}=0.5$, $\eta_{\min}=0.01$. The formula only looks complex. Substitute $t=20$:

  1. $\lfloor1+\tfrac{20}{40}\rfloor=\lfloor1.5\rfloor=1$, so $2\lfloor\cdot\rfloor=2$.
  2. $\tfrac{t}{\mu}=1$, so the inside is $1-2+1=0$, and $1-|0|=1$; $\max(0,1)=1$.
  3. $\eta_{20}=0.01+(0.5-0.01)\cdot1=0.5$: the peak, as on the plot.
  4. For $t=30$: $\lfloor1.75\rfloor=1$, the inside is $1.5-2+1=0.5$, the factor is $0.5$, so $\eta_{30}=0.01+0.49\times0.5=0.255$, halfway down.

It is a very simple, baseline idea: you know that sometimes you want the rate up and sometimes down, so instead of an algorithm that decides when, just cycle.

4 · Cosine annealing with warm restarts

Another cyclic schedule, shaped by a cosine:

$$\eta_t=\eta_{\min}+\frac{\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min}}{2}\Big(1+\cos\Big(\pi\,\frac{t \bmod (T+1)}{T}\Big)\Big)$$

$\eta_{\max}$ and $\eta_{\min}$ bound the rate, $t$ is the current epoch, and $T$ is the restart interval. Starting at $t=0$ gives $\cos0=1$, so $\eta=\eta_{\max}$. The rate then glides down. With the lecture's $T=50$, at $t=50$: $50\bmod51=50$, $\tfrac{50}{50}=1$, $\cos\pi=-1$, so $1+(-1)=0$ and $\eta=\eta_{\min}$. On the next step it jumps straight back to $\eta_{\max}$.

That jump is why it is called a warm restart. The triangular schedule climbs back gradually; here you restart from a "warm" point, a high learning rate, and anneal down from there. "Warm restart" is a term you'll see in many modern papers.

5 · Warm-up (warm start)

Every schedule so far, and every adaptive method, starts at some learning rate and then decays (step decay, exponential decay, the adaptive divisions). Warm-up reverses the start: let the learning rate increase gradually to its maximum, and only then start the decay.

The reasoning: at the start the parameters are random and you are not sure of your gradients, so keep the rate very small and avoid large updates. As you gain confidence, raise it to a high value, then decay from there. This is the default schedule for Transformer-based architectures. The warm-up steps are the number of steps for which the rate climbs. The lecture compares 2000 and 4000 warm-up steps: with 2000 the rate rises quickly and then falls; with 4000 it rises more smoothly and peaks lower and later.

Going beyond the video: the exact Transformer schedule. In "Attention Is All You Need", for step $s$, $$\eta=d_{\text{model}}^{-0.5}\cdot\min\big(s^{-0.5},\ s\cdot s_{\text{warm}}^{-1.5}\big):$$ a linear rise for $s_{\text{warm}}$ steps, then decay proportional to $1/\sqrt{s}$ (the lecture calls this part exponential; it is an inverse square root). The demo below uses this formula with $d_{\text{model}}=512$. A very common modern variant, linear warm-up followed by cosine decay, is also in the demo, marked as extra.

7 · Does cycling get us past the saddle?

The lecture's experiment: from the same initialisation on a saddle surface, an exponentially decaying learning rate (black) comes into the valley and gets stuck there. The cyclical one (blue) also slows down at first, but because its rate rises again it gets a push and escapes into the valley below. Swapping a fixed learning rate for cosine annealing similarly converged much faster, with some oscillations left at the end that early stopping could take care of.

You can test the claim on a toy saddle. The surface below is my construction, $f(x,y)=\tfrac12x^2+\tfrac14y^4-\tfrac12y^2$: a valley in $x$ that ends at a saddle at the origin, with two lower wells at $y=\pm1$. The start is a tiny distance $y_0$ off the ridge (in real training, mini-batch noise supplies such nudges). Near the saddle, each step multiplies $y$ by about $1+\eta_t$, so escape depends on how much learning rate is left once you arrive.

Right panel: loss (thick lines) and each run's learning rate (thin lines, scale on the right) over 300 iterations. The saddle has loss 0 and the wells have loss −0.25, so a run that ends near 0 is stuck.

Notice what stays fixed: all runs use the same plain gradient step with no history. Only the schedule differs. Lower $k$ (slower decay) or a larger $y_0$ and the decaying run escapes too: the failure is specifically a rate that has decayed away before the saddle. In this toy a fixed rate also escapes, but a fixed rate never anneals; the cyclic schedules give you both small steps and big pushes.

Key takeaways

  1. Purely decaying schedules can arrive at a saddle point with a tiny learning rate and get stuck; saddle points are a big pain in deep networks.
  2. Adaptive optimisers can raise the rate there but keep per-parameter histories; cyclic schedules get a similar effect for free.
  3. Triangular cyclical rate: rise linearly from $\eta_{\min}$ to $\eta_{\max}$ over $\mu$ steps, fall back, repeat ($\mu=20$: $\eta_{20}=0.5$, $\eta_{30}=0.255$ in the lecture's example).
  4. Cosine annealing glides from $\eta_{\max}$ to $\eta_{\min}$ over the restart interval $T$, then jumps straight back up: a warm restart.
  5. Warm-up grows the rate from near zero for the first few thousand steps before decaying; it is the default for Transformers. Any schedule can be combined with any optimiser.
