Bias and Variance: Simple vs Complex Models

Fit the same noisy sine data many times, each time on a different random sample. A straight line gives nearly the same answer every time, but it is always wrong: high bias, low variance. A degree-25 polynomial is right on average but changes wildly from sample to sample: low bias, high variance. Deep networks are the extreme of the second kind, and that is why we need regularisation.

1 · Why this comes up now

Everything so far has been about optimisation: how to minimise the training loss, how to do it faster, how not to overshoot the minimum. But deep learning models are usually over-parameterised: they have hundreds of millions of parameters, often more than there are training points.

Such models can drive the training error to zero. They memorise the training data, and then may not generalise to data they haven't seen. This lecture starts the regularisation chapter by building the intuition: bias, variance, and how both relate to the capacity of a model. Deep networks have very high capacity.

2 · The experiment: fitting a sine curve

The data are points $(x,y)$ taken from a sinusoid. For once we know the true relation $y=f(x)$, which makes it easy to judge an approximation. But we pretend we don't, and fit an approximation $\hat f(x)$ in two ways:

Model$\hat f(x)$Parameters
Simple (degree 1)$w_1x+w_0$2
Complex (degree 25)$w_{25}x^{25}+w_{24}x^{24}+\dots+w_1x+w_0$26

The complex model has more capacity. A 10-layer network with many neurons per layer would have far more still.

Both are trained by minimising the squared error over the $m$ training points, with gradient descent or any of its variants:

$$\min_{w}\ \sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(y_i-\hat f(x_i)\big)^2 .$$

The procedure: from 500 available points, draw a random sample of about 400, fit both models, and repeat $K$ times (30 in the lecture) with a different random sample each time. Using the full 500 every time would give nearly the same solution each time. With different samples, each run gives slightly different $w$'s, so a different line or curve.

3 · What the 30 fits show

  • Simple models trained on different samples barely differ from each other. But they are all far from the true sine: a line can only run through the middle, balancing points above and below. At a given $x$ there is a clear gap between prediction and truth. They underfit: even the training error isn't small.
  • Complex models differ a lot from each other. But on average across the $x$-axis the gap to the truth is much smaller; at least they have the sinusoidal shape. They can overfit: close to zero training error.

4 · Bias

Plug one $x$ into each of the 30 models, get 30 predictions, and average them. Doing this for every $x$ gives the average curve (green above). The mean is the empirical version of the expectation, so this curve is $\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]$.

Where does the randomness come from? From the training sample: a different random sample gives different parameters, hence a different $\hat f$.

$$\text{Bias}\big(\hat f(x)\big)=\mathbb E\big[\hat f(x)\big]-f(x)$$ The gap between the average model and the truth.

For the simple model the average line is far from the sine, so the bias is high. For the complex model the average curve is close to the sine, so the bias is low.

5 · Variance

Now compare each individual fit with the average curve, square the gap, and average over the fits:

$$\text{Variance}\big(\hat f(x)\big)=\mathbb E\Big[\big(\hat f(x)-\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]\big)^2\Big]$$

This is the ordinary statistical variance $\mathbb E[(Z-\mathbb E[Z])^2]$ of the random variable $Z=\hat f(x)$. The simple fits all hug their average: low variance. For the complex model, at the same $x$ one fit predicts a value here and another a value far away: high variance.

6 · The trade-off

BiasVarianceTraining error
Simple modelhighlowhigh (underfits)
Complex modellowhighnear zero (overfits)

So why not always pick the complex model? Because high variance means the model you get depends heavily on which training data you happened to receive. Had you been given a different sample, your $\hat f$ would have looked quite different, so a test point that resembles that other sample will be predicted badly.

Low variance alone isn't enough either: if it comes with high bias, all the fits agree with each other and are all far from the truth, which is useless. What we want is a balance, reasonable bias and reasonable variance: models that don't differ much from each other and aren't far from the true function.

Key takeaways

  1. Deep networks are over-parameterised, high-capacity models: they can memorise the training data and fail to generalise.
  2. Retrain on different random samples and look at the spread of the fitted functions: that spread is the variance; the gap between their average and the truth is the bias.
  3. $\text{Bias}=\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]-f(x)$ and $\text{Variance}=\mathbb E[(\hat f(x)-\mathbb E[\hat f(x)])^2]$; the expectation is over training samples.
  4. Simple models: high bias, low variance, underfit. Complex models: low bias, high variance, overfit.
  5. Aim for the middle: medium bias and medium variance.
