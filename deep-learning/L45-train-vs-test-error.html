Training Error vs Test Error

The expected error of a model on a point it has never seen splits into bias² + variance + σ². Simple models lose on bias, complex models on variance, so the test error is U-shaped in model complexity while the training error just keeps falling. The training error is therefore an over-optimistic guide; the test (or validation) error tells you where the sweet spot is.

1 · The error on an unseen point

We estimated $\hat f$ from training data. Now take a new point $(x,y)$ that was not seen during training, and predict $y$ with $\hat f(x)$. The mean squared error is

$$\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big].$$

Why an expectation? There are many test points, practically infinitely many. Train an image classifier on all images up to today, and next week millions of new images will have been uploaded that it has never seen. We want its average error over all of them, and "average" is written as an expectation.

One can show that this quantity splits into three parts:

$$\mathbb E\big[(y-\hat f(x))^2\big]=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\sigma^2$$ with $\text{Bias}=\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]-f(x)$, $\text{Variance}=\mathbb E\big[(\hat f(x)-\mathbb E[\hat f(x)])^2\big]$ (both from L44), and $\sigma^2$ the variance of the noise in $y$.

Prof. Khapra doesn't derive it in the video. The idea: the bias and variance formulas contain the same terms, $\hat f(x)$, $\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]$ and $f(x)$; open up the square with $(a-b)^2=a^2-2ab+b^2$ and rearrange.

Going beyond the video: the derivation in four lines. Write $y=f+\varepsilon$ with $\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0$, $\mathbb E[\varepsilon^2]=\sigma^2$, and let $\bar f=\mathbb E[\hat f(x)]$. The new point's noise $\varepsilon$ is independent of $\hat f$ (which came from other data).
  1. $y-\hat f=(f-\bar f)+(\bar f-\hat f)+\varepsilon$.
  2. Square and take expectations. The cross terms vanish: $\mathbb E[\bar f-\hat f]=0$ by definition of $\bar f$; $\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0$ and $\varepsilon$ is independent of the rest.
  3. What is left: $(f-\bar f)^2+\mathbb E[(\hat f-\bar f)^2]+\mathbb E[\varepsilon^2]$.
  4. That is $\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\sigma^2$. The demo below checks it numerically.

2 · Why this forces a trade-off

A high-bias model (the straight line) has a high expected test error. So does a high-variance model (the degree-25 polynomial). To make the sum small you need both bias and variance small, but the previous lecture showed they pull in opposite directions. So you look for a sweet spot in the middle: medium bias, medium variance, the lowest test error.

3 · Two errors, one picture

The parameters of $\hat f$ (all the $w$'s) are estimated from the training data, because that's all we have. But the model exists to be used at test time, on unseen inputs: is this a bird, a parrot, a lion? That gives two quantities, the training error and the test error. Ideally both would be zero. What typically happens as model complexity grows:

  • Training error keeps falling. Push the degree past 25 and the polynomial fits the training data almost perfectly.
  • Test error falls, then rises. A very simple model has high test error. A very complex one does too: it did everything it could to fit the points it saw, its whole universe, and so it misses points unlike them.

4 · The definitions

Let there be $n$ training points and $m$ test points, numbered $n+1$ to $n+m$:

$$\text{train err}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_i-\hat f(x_i)\big)^2,\qquad \text{test err}=\frac1m\sum_{i=n+1}^{n+m}\big(y_i-\hat f(x_i)\big)^2 .$$

Both are mean squared errors, one over the points used for fitting, one over points held out.

5 · Trust the test error

As complexity grows, the training error becomes overly optimistic. A training error of zero suggests you've found the perfect relation between $x$ and $y$, but only for that training data. The true picture of whether $\hat f$ is good comes from the validation or test data.

Driving the error to zero on known points is easy: keep adding complexity. To find the sweet spot you have to watch the error on unknown points, and stop adding complexity the moment it starts to rise.

The next videos turn this intuition into maths, and that maths makes the case for regularisation.

Key takeaways

  1. Expected squared error on an unseen point $=\text{Bias}^2+\text{Variance}+\sigma^2$.
  2. Both bias and variance must be small for a small test error; since they trade off, aim for the middle.
  3. As complexity grows, training error keeps falling while test error is U-shaped.
  4. Left of the sweet spot: high bias (high train and test error). Right: high variance (low train error, high test error).
  5. Training error is over-optimistic; judge a model, and choose its complexity, on held-out data.
