True Error vs Model Complexity: Why We Regularise

Estimate the true error from the training data and a covariance term no longer vanishes, because the training labels were used to fit $\hat f$. Stein's lemma turns that term into a sensitivity: how much $\hat f(x_i)$ moves when $y_i$ is nudged. Complex models are very sensitive, so the gap between training error and true error grows with complexity. Hence we minimise training loss plus a complexity penalty $\Omega(\theta)$, which is the basis of every regularisation method.

1 · Case 2: estimate from the training data

From L46, with $y=f(x)+\varepsilon$:

$$\mathbb E\big[(\hat f-f)^2\big]=\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat y_i-y_i)^2}_{\text{empirical train error}}-\ \sigma^2\ +\ 2\,\mathbb E\big[\varepsilon(\hat f-f)\big].$$

On test data the last term was zero, because $\varepsilon=y-f(x)$ and $\hat f-f$ were independent: the test $y$'s had no part in estimating $\hat f$. That argument fails here. The training $y$'s did participate in estimating $\hat f$, so the two are not independent. The expectation can't be split into $\mathbb E[\varepsilon]\cdot\mathbb E[\hat f-f]$, and the term does not vanish.

If you report the training error as your model's error, you leave out $2\,\mathbb E[\varepsilon(\hat f-f)]$, a quantity you don't even know how to compute. If it is large, your estimate is way off. That is why the training error is over-optimistic, and the test error is not.

We also said the training error gets more optimistic as model complexity grows. So this term must grow with complexity. Why?

2 · Stein's lemma: the term is a sensitivity

Estimate the troublesome term empirically, as an average over the $n$ training points. Stein's lemma says (for Gaussian noise) that

$$\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varepsilon_i\big(\hat f(x_i)-f(x_i)\big)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\partial \hat f(x_i)}{\partial y_i}.$$

Prof. Khapra's running joke: you don't ask him what Stein's lemma is and he won't ask you. We take it as given. Strictly, the equality holds in expectation.

When is $\partial\hat f(x_i)/\partial y_i$ large? When a small change in an observation causes a large change in the estimate. Say a training point was $(x_i,y_i)=(10,20)$, and it becomes $(10,20.1)$. That's a tiny change in the data, so ideally $\hat f$, and the $w$'s behind it, should barely move. If the derivative is large, it moves a lot, and that's not good.

We've seen which models do this. In L44, retraining the line on 30 different samples barely changed it; retraining the degree-25 polynomial changed it a great deal. Changing the sample is changing the training data. So:

$\sum_i \partial\hat f(x_i)/\partial y_i$ is high for complex models and low for simple ones.

4 · True error = train error + complexity

Putting it together:

$$\text{true error}=\text{empirical train error}+\text{small constant}+\Omega(\text{model complexity}).$$

The higher the complexity, the larger $\Omega$, and the further the training error sits from the true error. So if someone says "my training error is almost zero", don't believe them: they've said nothing about $\Omega$.

If you minimise only the training loss and push it to zero, you may do it at the cost of increasing $\Omega$ significantly, so the true error, the error on unseen test instances, stays high. Therefore minimise both:

$$\min_\theta\ \ \mathcal L_{\text{train}}(\theta)+\Omega(\theta)$$ $\Omega(\theta)$ is a function we design to be high for complex models and low for simple ones. It acts as a proxy for the sensitivity term: making $\Omega$ small reduces model complexity, which reduces the term that was bothering us, which reduces the true error.

This is the basis of all regularisation methods: add a regularisation term to the empirical training error and minimise the sum. Pictorially, $\mathcal L_{\text{train}}$ falls as complexity grows, $\Omega$ rises, and their sum has a sweet spot.

Going beyond the video: for least squares the sensitivity counts parameters. A polynomial least-squares fit is linear in the labels, $\hat{\mathbf y}=H\mathbf y$ with $H=A(A^\top A)^{-1}A^\top$, so $\partial\hat f(x_i)/\partial y_i=H_{ii}$ and $\sum_i H_{ii}=\operatorname{tr}H=p$, the number of parameters. The missing term becomes exactly $2\sigma^2p/n$ (Mallows' $C_p$). Here $\Omega$ is literally "how many parameters". 5 · Why this matters for deep learning

Regularisation controls model complexity, and deep networks are highly complex models: many layers, many non-linearities, many parameters. By the universal approximation theorem you can get arbitrarily close to the training data by adding layers or neurons. A big network can drive the training error to zero, but the complexity it adds along the way gives poor generalisation, a higher test error. That's why we add regularisation. The methods used in deep learning start in the next lecture.

Key takeaways

  1. From training data: true error $=$ train error $-\sigma^2+2\,\mathbb E[\varepsilon(\hat f-f)]$, and the last term is not zero, because the training $y$'s shaped $\hat f$.
  2. Stein's lemma: that term is $\frac{2\sigma^2}{n}\sum_i\partial\hat f(x_i)/\partial y_i$, the sensitivity of the fit to its own labels.
  3. Complex models are highly sensitive (one moved point reshapes the curve), so the gap between training and true error grows with complexity.
  4. So minimise $\mathcal L_{\text{train}}(\theta)+\Omega(\theta)$, with $\Omega$ a proxy for complexity. That's the basis of all regularisation.
  5. Deep networks have enormous capacity, which is exactly why they need it.
