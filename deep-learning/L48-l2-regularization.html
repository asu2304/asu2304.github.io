L2 Regularisation: Shrinking Weights Along the Hessian's Eigenvectors

Add $\frac{\alpha}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2$ to the loss. The update rule gains one term, $-\eta\alpha w$. Near the unregularised optimum $w^*$, a second-order Taylor expansion gives the regularised optimum in closed form: $\tilde w=Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\Lambda Q^\top w^*$. Rotate into the Hessian's eigenbasis, scale each component by $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$, rotate back. Directions the loss cares about (large $\lambda_i$) survive; the others shrink towards zero.

1 · Where we are

From L47: minimising the training error alone doesn't minimise the true error, which also contains a term that grows with model complexity. So we minimise

$$\tilde{\mathcal L}(\theta)=\mathcal L_{\text{train}}(\theta)+\Omega(\theta),$$

where $\Omega$ is a proxy for complexity: high when the model is complex, low when it is simple. The methods ahead are L2 regularisation, dataset augmentation, parameter sharing, adding noise to the inputs, adding noise to the outputs, early stopping, ensemble methods and dropout. This lecture looks at the formula in detail for L2.

2 · The L2-regularised loss

$$\tilde{\mathcal L}(w)=\mathcal L(w)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\lVert w\rVert^2,\qquad \lVert w\rVert^2=w^\top w,$$

where $\mathcal L(w)$ is the training loss (squared error, averaged over the $m$ examples). $\alpha$ decides how much weight the penalty gets. With $\alpha=0$ you're back to the unregularised loss.

How does this control complexity? Think of two weights, $w_1,w_2$. Without the penalty, the whole $\mathbb R^2$ plane is open to you: any values, however large, if they lower the training loss. With it, you're told the weights can't blow up: the further out you go, the larger $\lVert w\rVert^2$, and the larger the total loss. You may have as many parameters as you like, a million or a billion, but you don't get the freedom to give them any values you want. That restriction is the proxy for model complexity.

3 · Gradient and update rule

The penalty is $\frac{\alpha}{2}w^\top w$; think of it as $\frac\alpha2 w^2$, whose derivative is $\alpha w$ (the 2 cancels). So

$$\nabla\tilde{\mathcal L}(w)=\nabla\mathcal L(w)+\alpha w,$$ $$w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\nabla\mathcal L(w_t)-\eta\,\alpha\,w_t .$$

If you've implemented gradient descent, L2 regularisation is one extra term in the update. (It gets more involved for Adam and its variants, which the lecture doesn't go into.)

4 · What does the optimum look like?

A slightly longer derivation, but each step follows from the previous one.

  1. Let $w^*$ be the optimum of the unregularised loss $\mathcal L(w)$. Take a point near it, $w=w^*+u$, i.e. $u=w-w^*$.
  2. Second-order Taylor expansion (so far the course has used first order): $$\mathcal L(w)=\mathcal L(w^*)+u^\top\nabla\mathcal L(w^*)+\tfrac12u^\top Hu,$$ with $H$ the Hessian, the matrix of second-order partial derivatives.
  3. $w^*$ is a minimum, so $\nabla\mathcal L(w^*)=0$ and the middle term drops: $$\mathcal L(w)=\mathcal L(w^*)+\tfrac12(w-w^*)^\top H(w-w^*).$$
  4. Differentiate (like the derivative of $\frac12 hx^2$ being $hx$): $\ \nabla\mathcal L(w)=H(w-w^*)$.
  5. So the regularised gradient is $\ \nabla\tilde{\mathcal L}(w)=H(w-w^*)+\alpha w$.
  6. Let $\tilde w$ be the optimum of the regularised loss. Its gradient is zero: $\ H(\tilde w-w^*)+\alpha\tilde w=0$.
  7. Collect the $\tilde w$ terms and write $\alpha=\alpha I$: $\ (H+\alpha I)\,\tilde w=Hw^*$.
$$\tilde w=(H+\alpha I)^{-1}H\,w^*$$ Sanity check: $\alpha\to0$ gives $\tilde w=H^{-1}Hw^*=w^*$. No regulariser, same optimum.

On the inverse: the lecture justifies it by $H$ being positive semi-definite. More precisely, $H$ is PSD at a minimum, so for any $\alpha>0$ the matrix $H+\alpha I$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_i+\alpha>0$ and is invertible.

5 · Eigen-decomposition: rotate, scale, rotate back

$H$ is symmetric positive semi-definite, so it has an eigen-decomposition with orthonormal eigenvectors: $H=Q\Lambda Q^\top$, with $QQ^\top=Q^\top Q=I$.

  1. Substitute, and write $I=QQ^\top$: $\ \tilde w=(Q\Lambda Q^\top+\alpha QQ^\top)^{-1}Q\Lambda Q^\top w^*$.
  2. Factor the bracket as a product of three matrices: $\ \big[Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)Q^\top\big]^{-1}Q\Lambda Q^\top w^*$.
  3. $(ABC)^{-1}=C^{-1}B^{-1}A^{-1}$, with $(Q^\top)^{-1}=Q$ and $Q^{-1}=Q^\top$: $\ Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}Q^\top Q\Lambda Q^\top w^*$.
  4. $Q^\top Q=I$: $\ \tilde w=Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\Lambda\,Q^\top w^*$. (Again, $\alpha=0$ gives $QQ^\top w^*=w^*$.)

$(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\Lambda$ is a product of diagonal matrices, so it is diagonal. Call it $D$:

$$\tilde w=Q\,D\,Q^\top w^*,\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}\!\Big(\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_1+\alpha},\ \dots,\ \frac{\lambda_n}{\lambda_n+\alpha}\Big)$$

Read it right to left: rotate $w^*$ by $Q^\top$ (its coordinates along the eigenvectors), scale each coordinate by its diagonal entry, rotate back by $Q$. Without the scaling, the two rotations would cancel.

Eigenvalue$\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$Effect on that component
$\lambda_i\gg\alpha$$\approx1$kept as it was
$\lambda_i\ll\alpha$$\approx0$shrunk almost to zero

The weights shrink, as we hoped, but not uniformly. The significant directions, those with large eigenvalues, are retained; the others are heavily penalised. And the model now behaves as if it had fewer parameters:

$$n_{\text{effective}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_i}{\lambda_i+\alpha}\ <\ n .$$

You have given the model capacity, but not the freedom to use all of it.

6 · The picture

Draw the contours of the unregularised loss $\mathcal L(w)$ (ellipses around $w^*$) and of the penalty $w^\top w$ (circles around the origin). $\tilde w$ is where the two balance. Two observations:

  1. The weights shrank: $\tilde w$ is closer to the origin than $w^*$.
  2. Not uniformly. One coordinate shrinks much more than the other, by the factors $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$.

Which one shrinks more? Look at the spacing of the loss contours. Along a direction where they are far apart, the loss changes slowly (small slope, small eigenvalue): you can shrink that weight a lot and lose little. Along a direction where they are close together, the loss changes fast (large eigenvalue): even a small shrink costs a lot of loss, which isn't acceptable when you want both a small penalty and a small loss. So the compromise shrinks the insensitive direction hard and the sensitive one gently. The Hessian's eigenvalues are exactly what say which directions are sensitive.

In the video the labels "$w_1$" and "$w_2$" get swapped a couple of times while explaining the figure. The rule itself is unambiguous: the direction with widely spaced contours (small $\lambda$) shrinks more. The demo's default reproduces the lecture's figure.

The loss is the quadratic $\frac12(w-w^*)^\top H(w-w^*)$ from step 3 of the derivation, with $w^*=(2,2)$ and $H$ built from your eigenvalues and rotation. "Run GD" applies $w\leftarrow w-\eta\nabla\mathcal L(w)-\eta\alpha w$ from a corner and lands exactly on the closed-form $\tilde w$. Set α to 0 and it lands on $w^*$.

Going beyond the video. For linear regression the Hessian of the squared loss is $X^\top X$ (up to a constant), so this is exactly ridge regression, where the scaling $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$ acts on the principal directions of the data: ML L37 · ridge and ML L38 · ridge geometry (the ellipse-meets-circle picture). ML L36 shows the same penalty arising from a Gaussian prior on $w$. For a neural network the quadratic expansion only holds near $w^*$, so this analysis is local, but the update rule in section 3 applies everywhere; it's usually called weight decay.

Key takeaways

  1. L2 regularisation: $\tilde{\mathcal L}(w)=\mathcal L(w)+\frac\alpha2\lVert w\rVert^2$; you keep all the parameters but can't let them grow freely.
  2. Gradient $\nabla\mathcal L+\alpha w$; the update gains one term, $-\eta\alpha w_t$.
  3. Near the optimum (second-order Taylor): $\tilde w=(H+\alpha I)^{-1}Hw^*=Q(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\Lambda Q^\top w^*$.
  4. In the Hessian's eigenbasis, each component of $w^*$ is scaled by $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$: kept if $\lambda_i\gg\alpha$, near zero if $\lambda_i\ll\alpha$.
  5. Effective number of parameters $\sum_i\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$ is less than $n$: that's how it controls complexity.
  6. Geometrically: weights shrink, more in directions where the loss is flat, less where it is steep.
