Early Stopping

Train for long enough and the training error goes to zero while the validation error turns back up. Early stopping watches the validation error, stops once it has failed to improve for $p$ steps, and keeps the model from $p$ steps back. It changes nothing in the loss, yet it behaves like L2 regularisation: the number of steps limits how far the weights can travel from where they started.

1 · The plot again, with steps on the x-axis

In L47 the x-axis was model complexity: training error falls towards zero, validation error falls and then rises. The same picture appears with the number of training steps on the x-axis. The longer we train, the more the parameters keep adjusting until they drive the training error to zero, and in doing so they overfit: a large gap opens between training and validation (or test) error. As usual we want the sweet spot where the training error is low and the validation error is as low as it can be.

2 · The algorithm: a patience parameter

track the validation error along with the training error choose a patience p (in steps or in epochs) slide a window of p steps along the run: if the validation error has not decreased for p consecutive steps (at step k): stop training return the model saved at step k − p

In every window of $p$ steps we check whether the validation error came down. As long as it does, carry on. The first time it hasn't decreased for $p$ consecutive steps while the training error kept falling, something is wrong: throw away the last $p$ updates and release the model from step $k-p$.

  • Very effective and very widely used.
  • Trivial to implement: no change to the loss function at all; just watch a number and stop automatically.
  • Combines with other regularisers: e.g. keep an L2 term in the loss and stop early.

A linear model on 12 Gaussian bumps plus a bias (13 weights) is trained by gradient descent from $w_0=0$ on 14 noisy points of $\sin 2\pi x$. Because the loss is quadratic, $w_t$ can be computed exactly for any $t$ with the formula of §4, so the run goes up to 10 million steps. Validation error is checked at 160 log-spaced steps, and the patience counts those checks.

3 · Intuition: the step count is a leash

Gradient descent (and, at its core, every variant with momentum or adaptive rates) starts at $w_0$ and keeps subtracting gradients. Unroll the updates:

$$w_{t+1}=w_0-\eta\sum_{i=0}^{t}\nabla w_i .$$
  1. Let $\tau$ be the largest of the gradients $\nabla w_0,\dots,\nabla w_t$ in magnitude, so each one is at most $\tau$.
  2. A sum of $t$ such terms is at most $t\tau$, hence $$\lvert w_{t+1}-w_0\rvert\le\eta\,t\,\tau .$$
  3. So the number of steps $t$ controls how far the weights can wander from their initial value. Each update moves away from $w_0$; even in the worst case, where every gradient pushes the same way, $t$ steps can only take you so far, and if the gradients sometimes point back you end up even closer.

That is what L2 regularisation does too: its circular contours (L48) limit how far the solution may go from the origin, shrinking the weights. Early stopping limits the space of exploration in the same way, by limiting $t$.

4 · Analysis: the same form as L2

As in the L2 derivation, take a quadratic (second-order Taylor) approximation of the unregularised training loss around its optimum $w^\ast$, where the gradient is zero:

$$\mathcal L(w)=\mathcal L(w^\ast)+\tfrac12(w-w^\ast)^\top H\,(w-w^\ast)\quad\Rightarrow\quad\nabla\mathcal L(w)=H(w-w^\ast).$$
  1. Gradient descent with learning rate $\varepsilon$ (the $\eta$ above), using that gradient at $w_{t-1}$: $$w_t=w_{t-1}-\varepsilon H(w_{t-1}-w^\ast)=(I-\varepsilon H)\,w_{t-1}+\varepsilon Hw^\ast .$$ (The $I$ is there so that $I-\varepsilon H$ is a valid $n\times n$ matrix.)
  2. $H$ is symmetric positive semi-definite, so $H=Q\Lambda Q^\top$ with orthonormal eigenvectors ($Q^{-1}=Q^\top$). Substitute: $$w_t=Q(I-\varepsilon\Lambda)Q^\top w_{t-1}+Q\,\varepsilon\Lambda\,Q^\top w^\ast .$$
  3. Start from $w_0=0$ and unroll the recursion (by induction): $$w_t=Q\big[I-(I-\varepsilon\Lambda)^t\big]Q^\top w^\ast .$$
  4. Compare with the L2-regularised optimum from L48: $$\tilde w=Q\big[I-(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\alpha\big]Q^\top w^\ast .$$ Both are $Q\,(\text{diagonal})\,Q^\top w^\ast$; only the diagonal differs (early stopping's has a power $t$).
Early stopping after $t$ steps and L2 regularisation with constant $\alpha$ give the same weights when $$(I-\varepsilon\Lambda)^t=(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\alpha,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad(1-\varepsilon\lambda_i)^t=\frac{\alpha}{\lambda_i+\alpha}\ \text{ for every } i .$$ In this quadratic setting, limiting the number of updates is equivalent to an L2 penalty.
Going beyond the video. To check the induction step: if $w_{t-1}=[I-(I-\varepsilon H)^{t-1}]w^\ast$, then $w_t=(I-\varepsilon H)w^\ast-(I-\varepsilon H)^t w^\ast+\varepsilon Hw^\ast=[I-(I-\varepsilon H)^t]w^\ast$. Taking logs of the condition and using $\log(1+x)\approx x$ for small $\varepsilon\lambda_i$ and $\lambda_i\ll\alpha$ gives $t\approx1/(\varepsilon\alpha)$ (Goodfellow, Bengio and Courville, §7.8): the number of steps behaves like the inverse of the weight-decay constant. 5 · Important directions grow, unimportant ones stay small

For L2 we saw that weights along important directions shrink little and weights along unimportant ones shrink a lot. Early stopping does the same, implicitly.

  • If $w_1$ matters for the loss, a small change in it changes the loss a lot: its gradients are large. If $w_2$ doesn't matter, its gradients are small.
  • Training stops for all parameters at the same step $t$. Starting both at 0, after, say, $t=4$ steps $w_1$ has moved well away from its initial value and $w_2$ hardly at all.
  • Both are clipped, but proportionately: the important parameter is allowed to grow relatively more. The diagonal factor $1-(1-\varepsilon\lambda_i)^t$ is near 1 for large curvature $\lambda_i$ and near 0 for small $\lambda_i$, just like $\lambda_i/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$.

Key takeaways

  1. Training longer keeps lowering training error but eventually raises validation error; early stopping looks for the sweet spot.
  2. Track validation error with a patience $p$: if it hasn't decreased for $p$ consecutive steps (at step $k$), stop and return the model from step $k-p$.
  3. No change to the loss, easy to automate, and it can be combined with other regularisers such as L2.
  4. $\lvert w_{t+1}-w_0\rvert\le\eta t\tau$: the number of steps limits how far the weights can move, like an L2 constraint.
  5. On a quadratic loss from $w_0=0$, $w_t=Q[I-(I-\varepsilon\Lambda)^t]Q^\top w^\ast$, which matches L2's $Q[I-(\Lambda+\alpha I)^{-1}\alpha]Q^\top w^\ast$ when $(1-\varepsilon\lambda_i)^t=\alpha/(\lambda_i+\alpha)$; high-curvature (important) directions are fitted first.
