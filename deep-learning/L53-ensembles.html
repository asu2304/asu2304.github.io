Ensemble Methods and Bagging

Don't rely on one model: train several and average their outputs. Bagging does this with copies of the same model, each trained on its own sample (with replacement) of the training data, so each overfits differently. How much the average helps depends on one number, the covariance between the models' errors: with $k$ models the expected squared error is $V/k+\frac{k-1}{k}C$.

1 · Combine the outputs of several models

Train, say, a logistic regression, an SVM and a naive Bayes classifier separately on the same data. At test time ask all three and combine their answers, by voting or by averaging: for a two-class problem each model outputs a distribution over $\{0,1\}$, and the final prediction is the average of the three distributions. This acts as a regulariser because no single model's quirks decide the answer.

2 · Bagging

Bagging is the ensemble of different instances of the same classifier. From the one training set, build $k$ training sets $T_1,\dots,T_k$ by sampling with replacement: draw a sample for the first network, put it back, draw again from the full original set for the second network, and so on. Model $i$ is trained on $T_i$.

Each model now overfits to a different sample, and none has been able to overfit the whole training set. Combine them and the idiosyncrasies of each can average out, giving a better generalisation error. My ML notes treat bagging from the bias–variance angle, together with random forests: ML L67 (and ML L66 for the trade-off itself).

3 · When does averaging help?

Take $k$ models (the lecture uses logistic regression). On a test example, model $i$ makes an error $\varepsilon_i$. Assume the errors come from a zero-mean multivariate normal with

$$\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i^2]=V,\qquad\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j]=C\quad(i\ne j).$$

$V$ is each model's variance (its own mean squared error) and $C$ the covariance between two models' errors. Averaging the $k$ predictions gives an ensemble whose error is the average of the individual errors, $\frac1k\sum_i\varepsilon_i$.

  1. Expected squared error of the ensemble; pull the constant $1/k$ out as $1/k^2$: $$\mathrm{MSE}=\mathbb E\Big[\Big(\tfrac1k\textstyle\sum_i\varepsilon_i\Big)^2\Big]=\frac1{k^2}\,\mathbb E\Big[\Big(\textstyle\sum_i\varepsilon_i\Big)^2\Big].$$
  2. Expand $(\varepsilon_1+\dots+\varepsilon_k)^2$ into the squares ($i=j$) and the cross terms ($i\ne j$): $$=\frac1{k^2}\,\mathbb E\Big[\textstyle\sum_i\varepsilon_i^2+\sum_i\sum_{j\ne i}\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_j\Big].$$
  3. Expectation of a sum is the sum of expectations. There are $k$ squares, each with expectation $V$, and $k(k-1)$ cross terms, each with expectation $C$: $$=\frac1{k^2}\big(kV+k(k-1)C\big).$$
$$\mathrm{MSE}_{\text{ensemble}}=\frac{V}{k}+\frac{k-1}{k}\,C$$

A regulariser is supposed to improve the expected error on test instances; this formula shows there is room to do so unless the models are perfectly correlated. Hence the push for diverse members: different data samples, different features, different architectures.

(b) Real bagging. Fit high-degree polynomials (degree 7) to 30 noisy points, each on its own bootstrap sample, and average them. The error of model $i$ at a test input $x$ is $\varepsilon_i(x)=f_i(x)-f(x)$. Measuring $V$ and $C$ from the fitted models and plugging them into the formula reproduces the ensemble's error, and shows why bagging stops improving: the models share most of their training points, so $C$ is far from 0.

Going beyond the video. As $k\to\infty$ the formula tends to $C$, not 0: correlation sets a floor no amount of averaging removes. Random forests attack $C$ directly by adding the "different features" idea on top of bagging: each split considers only a random subset of features, which decorrelates the trees (see ML L67). The formula needs $V$ and $C$ to be second moments of the errors; if the models share a bias, that bias also shows up in $C$ and is not averaged away either.

Key takeaways

  1. Ensembles combine several models' outputs (vote or average) to reduce generalisation error.
  2. Diversity can come from different model families, hyperparameters, feature subsets, or training samples.
  3. Bagging: the same model trained on $k$ datasets sampled with replacement from the training set; each overfits a different sample.
  4. With error variance $V$ and pairwise covariance $C$, averaging $k$ models gives $\mathrm{MSE}=V/k+\frac{k-1}{k}C$.
  5. Perfectly correlated errors ($C=V$): no gain. Uncorrelated ($C=0$): $V/k$. The less the models' errors are correlated, the more averaging helps.
