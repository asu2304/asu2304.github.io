Regularisation: Loss Landscapes and a Summary

One way to see what regularisation does: it smooths the loss landscape, and a flat minimum is less sensitive to small changes in the weights than a sharp one. The lecture then puts all the regularisers of this module into one map, grouped by what they act on (data, architecture, optimisation, the loss), and into explicit versus implicit ones.

1 · Regularisation smooths the loss landscape

Real neural-network loss landscapes look nothing like the tidy bowls we have been drawing (L31): they are bumpy, with many plateaus and valleys and some very sharp slopes. People have shown that an effect of regularisation is to smoothen this landscape. The work the lecture cites studies skip connections (which we will meet with convolutional networks) acting as a regulariser, and finds that the loss surface becomes smoother, which also makes optimisation easier.

Why does flatness help generalisation?

  • Flat region: the loss stays about the same over a large neighbourhood. Having found a good $w$, any nearby $w$ would have given a similar loss, so the model is not sensitive to the exact values.
  • Sharp, bumpy region: if you overfit into one narrow valley, it is surrounded by hills. A small change gives a very large change in the loss: the model is very sensitive.

Going beyond the video. The study with skip connections is very likely Li, Xu, Taylor, Studer and Goldstein, "Visualizing the Loss Landscape of Neural Nets" (NeurIPS 2018), whose plots show ResNets with skip connections having far smoother surfaces than the same networks without them. The flat-versus-sharp argument for generalisation goes back to Hochreiter and Schmidhuber's "Flat Minima" (1997).

3 · Explicit versus implicit

The same techniques can be grouped another way.

explicitimplicit
Things we deliberately add: L2 (and L1) penalties, data augmentation, noise injection, dropout; skip connections, weight sharing and pooling (to come with CNNs). Regularisation that comes from how we train: early stopping; the preference of gradient-descent methods for simpler solutions; even the initial learning rate, since it shapes how training proceeds.

The module covered mostly the explicit kind (plus early stopping); the implicit effects of gradient descent and learning rates are mentioned here for the full picture but not covered in the course. Key takeaways

  1. Neural loss landscapes are bumpy; regularisation (e.g. skip connections) has been shown to smooth them.
  2. Flat minima are robust: nearby weights give similar loss. Sharp minima are sensitive: a small change in $w$ gives a big change in loss.
  3. Regularisers act on the data (augmentation, input/output noise), the architecture (dropout, skip connections, weight sharing, pooling), the optimisation (gradient descent, early stopping), or the loss (L2, L1).
  4. Explicit regularisers are added on purpose; implicit ones (early stopping, the bias of gradient descent, learning rates) come from the training procedure.
