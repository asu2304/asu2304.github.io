Why Deep Networks Were Hard to Train Before 2006

Training a neural network is a game of gradients, and the gradient for any weight is proportional to the input that weight is connected to. Backpropagation has been around since at least 1986, yet networks just four or five layers deep would not converge reliably until a 2006 idea, unsupervised pre-training, changed things. This short lecture sets up that puzzle.

1 · Recap: everything runs on the gradient

Start with the smallest network: one input, one weight $w$ (the bias is dropped to keep it simple). Whatever variant of gradient descent we use (plain, momentum, NAG, Adam, …), the update contains the derivative:

$$w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\,\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial w}.$$

For a sigmoid neuron $f(x)=\sigma(wx)$ with squared error, we derived earlier

$$\frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial w}=(f(x)-y)\,f(x)\,(1-f(x))\cdot x .$$
Key observation: the derivative has the input $x$ as a factor. If $x$ is large, something happens (big updates); if $x$ is small or zero, the update is small or zero. That observation is what led to adaptive learning rates for sparse inputs.

The same factor shows up as the network grows:

NetworkHow the derivative is computedThe "input" factor
Wide, shallow (many inputs, one output)Same update rule, applied to every $w_i$$x_i$, the input wired to $w_i$
Thin, deep (one neuron per layer)Chain rule along one path$h_0=x$ for $w_1$; in general $h_{i-1}$ for $w_i$
Deep and wideChain rule summed over all paths (backprop)$h_{i-1}$, the activation feeding that weight

The $h$'s are outputs of one layer but inputs to the next. (On the slide the subscript was typeset as $h_i-1$; he corrects it to $h_{i-1}$.)

Points to remember.
  1. Training is a game of gradients: compute them at every layer, then feed them to your favourite optimizer.
  2. The gradient measures a parameter's responsibility for the loss: higher gradient, higher responsibility.
  3. $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w$ is proportional to the input connected to $w$: $x$ in a single-layer network, $h_{i-1}$ in a multi-layer one.

2 · See the "proportional to the input" claim

A thin sigmoid chain $x=h_0\to h_1\to\dots\to h_L=\hat y$ with $a_i=w_ih_{i-1}$, $h_i=\sigma(a_i)$ and $\mathcal L=\tfrac12(\hat y-y)^2$, target $y=1$. Backprop gives $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_i=\delta_i\,h_{i-1}$, where $\delta_i=\partial\mathcal L/\partial a_i$. Set $x=0$ and watch the gradient of $w_1$ vanish while the others don't.

Going beyond the video: why depth hurt. Each step back through a sigmoid multiplies $\delta$ by $w_i\,\sigma'(a_{i-1})$, and $\sigma'\le\tfrac14$. Unless the weights are large (which saturates the neurons instead), the first layers get gradients many times smaller than the last ones: try depth 8 with $w=1.5$. This is one concrete face of the convergence problem described below, and it is why the following lectures look at activation functions (L59) and initialization.

3 · The puzzle: backprop is old, deep learning is new

Feed-forward networks, and later CNNs, RNNs and Transformers, are all trained with backpropagation. So was backprop discovered around 2009–2010, which is roughly when deep learning became popular (around 2014 in NLP)? No. It existed in the late 1970s, possibly earlier, and was made popular for neural networks by Rumelhart and colleagues in 1986.

So what was stopping deep learning in the 1990s and early 2000s? In theory you could chain the gradients through any depth. In practice, networks four or five layers deep did not converge reliably.

Three things have changed since the 1990s:

  1. More data. A deep network has many parameters, and you need lots of data to fit them.
  2. Faster compute. Training that used to take many days now takes a few.
  3. Other, more theoretical things that had to fall into place. It was not only a compute problem. This is the focus of the next few lectures.
Until 2006, deep networks were very hard to train. A seminal 2006 work, unsupervised pre-training, made it possible and revived interest in deep networks. The work that followed (roughly 2006 to 2009) led to better optimizers, regularizers, activation functions and initialization methods, and from there to today.

Key takeaways

  1. Every gradient-based optimizer is driven by $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w$; the gradient measures how responsible a parameter is for the loss.
  2. For any weight, $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_i\propto h_{i-1}$, the input wired to it ($x$ itself in the first layer).
  3. Backprop dates back to at least 1986, but in practice networks with 4–5 layers did not converge reliably before 2006.
  4. More data and compute helped, but the revival also needed new ideas, starting with unsupervised pre-training in 2006.
  5. That work pointed research towards better optimization, regularization, activation functions and initialization: the subject of the coming lectures.
