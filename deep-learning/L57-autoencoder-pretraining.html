Unsupervised Pre-training, One Layer at a Time

A deep network was hard to train, but a one-hidden-layer network was not. So train each layer on its own, as a small autoencoder that must reconstruct its own input, freeze it, and stack the next one on top. Once every layer has been pre-trained this way without labels, use those weights as the starting point for ordinary supervised training of the whole network.

1 · What changed in 2006?

The seminal 2006 paper that made deep networks trainable phrased its idea with RBMs (restricted Boltzmann machines). RBMs aren't part of this offering of the course, and neither are autoencoders (they were dropped to make room for newer optimization methods and Transformers; his older recorded lectures cover them). So Prof. Khapra explains the idea with autoencoders, introducing just the parts he needs.

One caution: unsupervised pre-training still exists today, mostly for Transformers, with different loss functions and many other moving parts. Conceptually it is similar, but this lecture is about the 2006 version for feed-forward networks.

Setting: inputs $x\in\mathbb R^n$, one real output $y\in\mathbb R$, and a deep network (four hidden layers plus input and output) as the function $f(x)$. Before 2006, networks four, five or six layers deep did not converge well.

2 · Step 1: make the first layer an autoencoder

Forget the full network. Keep only the input and the first hidden layer $h_1$, cut the connection to the next layer, and ask the network to predict $x$ again. For example, a 1024-dimensional input, a 256-dimensional hidden layer and a 1024-dimensional output:

$$h_1=\sigma(W_1x+b_1),\qquad \hat x=W^{*}h_1+c .$$

(He drops the biases at first and adds them back "if that makes you more comfortable".) The objective is reconstruction error, averaged over the $m$ training examples and summed over the $n$ dimensions:

$$\min\ \frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\big(\hat x_{ij}-x_{ij}\big)^2 .$$

Why reconstructing the input is a useful goal

Suppose the error reaches zero. We went from 1024 numbers down to 256 and back, losing nothing, so $h_1$ captures all the information in $x$. That's possible because real inputs are full of redundancy:

  • weight, height and BMI: from weight and height you can compute BMI;
  • weight in kilograms, in pounds and in grams; height in centimetres;
  • salary and income tax: completely correlated.

The narrow middle layer (the bottleneck) forces a compact representation that keeps the important characteristics and drops the rest. That is exactly what an autoencoder does. Under certain conditions it is equivalent to PCA, which is also a dimensionality-reduction technique (see my ML notes on PCA as error minimization).

The trick. Training the whole deep network was the hard problem, so set it aside. Define what a good first layer means (it keeps everything needed to rebuild its input), and train for that. This is a network with one hidden layer, and shallow networks were never hard to train.

Where "unsupervised" comes from: this objective never uses $y$. Only the inputs appear in the loss, so it would work even if nobody gave you labels.

4 · Step 2 onwards: repeat, one layer at a time

  1. After step 1, $W_1$ is good: training loss has dropped and $h_1$ is a good representation. Freeze it. Compute $h_1$ for all $m$ training examples. These become the new training data.
  2. Plug in $h_2$ on top of $h_1$ and reconstruct $\hat h_1$ from it, with the same objective and $x$ replaced by $h_1$. Again a shallow network: input $h_1$ (fixed), hidden $h_2$, output $\hat h_1$. Only this layer trains.
  3. Just as $x$ is $h_0$, $h_1$ is now the input. $h_1$ is an abstract representation of $x$; $h_2$ captures everything in $h_1$, so it is a deeper abstract representation of $x$. That is the whole idea of a feed-forward network: each layer is more abstract than the last.
  4. Freeze $h_1$ and $h_2$, use $h_2$ as input, add $h_3$, reconstruct $h_2$, and so on through all $L$ layers.

It is greedy because each layer is trained without caring what happens in the other layers, and unsupervised because no label is used.

5 · Step last: fine-tune with the real objective

Return to the original problem: learn $y=f(x)$. $f$ is a composition of layers, and each of those components has already been learned well. So:

  • Keep the pre-trained weights as the initialization (instead of random).
  • Initialize the output-layer weights randomly (they were never pre-trained).
  • Train on the supervised objective with full backprop, updating every weight, including the pre-trained ones:
$$\min_\theta\ \mathcal L(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^{m}\big(y_i-f(x_i)\big)^2 .$$

He calls the reconstruction objective $\Omega(\theta)$ and the supervised one $\mathcal L(\theta)$, a naming that matters in the next lecture. With this recipe, the 2006 authors could train quite deep networks effectively, though by modern standards they were still shallow.

6 · Why does it work? Two candidate answers

In effect, the network's weights were initialized with a greedy, unsupervised objective. Why does that help? Two options:

Better optimization?Better regularization?
ConcernsTraining data only: driving $\mathcal L(\theta)$ on the training set downGeneralization: doing better on test data
The claim would beWithout pre-training we could not even drive training error to zeroTraining error was fine, but pre-training gives better test performance

So far in the course we assumed training error can always be driven to zero by making the model complex enough. Was that false for deep networks before 2006?

Why the question mattered more than its answer. The 2006–2009 investigations gave no conclusive answer. But they convinced people that something real was happening, and that it might be optimization, regularization, initialization or the gradients themselves. So researchers went after each of these directly:
  • better optimizers (from 2011–12, converging on Adam and its variants);
  • better regularizers (dropout, around 2012–14, still popular; early stopping);
  • better initialization (pre-training looks like a clever initialization, so why not design one directly?);
  • better activation functions (they change the gradients).

The optimizers and regularizers are done. The remaining two, activation functions and initialization, come next, after a look at the evidence for each view in L58.

Going beyond the video. The first 2006 papers were Hinton, Osindero and Teh (deep belief nets, RBMs) and Hinton and Salakhutdinov (deep autoencoders in Science); Bengio et al. (2007) did the stacked-autoencoder version described here. The modern descendant is self-supervised pre-training of Transformers (e.g. masked-word prediction in BERT): still "learn from inputs alone, then fine-tune", but on the whole network at once instead of greedily layer by layer.

Key takeaways

  1. An autoencoder layer learns $h=\sigma(Wx+b)$ such that $x$ can be rebuilt from $h$; the reconstruction error uses no labels.
  2. Zero reconstruction error through a bottleneck means $h$ keeps all the information in $x$, which is possible because real inputs are redundant.
  3. Greedy layer-wise pre-training: train $h_1$ to rebuild $x$, freeze it, train $h_2$ to rebuild $h_1$, and so on. Each step is an easy shallow network.
  4. Then initialize the deep network with these weights (output layer random) and fine-tune everything on the supervised loss.
  5. Whether this helps optimization or regularization was unclear, but asking that question launched the work on optimizers, regularizers, initialization and activations.
