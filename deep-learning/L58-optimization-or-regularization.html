Pre-training: Better Optimization or Better Regularization?

Why did unsupervised pre-training make deep networks trainable? One camp showed that small-capacity networks could only drive training error to zero after pre-training: an optimization effect. Another argued that pre-training confines the weights to a region of parameter space, which is what a regularizer does. A third observation was that pre-trained networks were far less sensitive to random initialization. No lens was conclusive, but together they set the research agenda: better optimizers, regularizers, initialization and activation functions.

Recall from L57: $\Omega(\theta)$ is the layer-wise reconstruction objective used for pre-training, and $\mathcal L(\theta)$ is the supervised loss we actually care about.

1 · The optimization view

The optimization problem is to minimize $\mathcal L(\theta)$ (squared error, cross-entropy, whatever) on the training data. The hypothesis: without pre-training this problem itself is hard and $\mathcal L(\theta)$ can't be driven to zero; with pre-training it can.

The error surface of a deep network is highly non-convex: plateaus, valleys, very steep points that are hard to climb out of, several minima, maybe several equally good ones. A convex surface has just one minimum.

Still, a deep network has large capacity, and the universal approximation theorem says a big enough network can reach any precision, so it should be possible to land in one of the zero-error regions. The catch: the theorem's "big enough" can mean an exponential number of neurons. Maybe a large but reasonable capacity is enough.

The experiment: grow the last layer

Researchers tested this by increasing the capacity of the last layer, i.e. adding many neurons just before the output. His illustration: 100 inputs, a few hidden layers of 20 neurons, then a last hidden layer of 100 neurons predicting 10 outputs. That's $20\times100$ weights into the last layer and $100\times10$ out of it. Make that layer 1000 wide and the capacity jumps.

NetworkWithout pre-trainingWith pre-training
Large capacity (wide last layer)Training error → 0Training error → 0
Smaller capacityCan't drive training error to zeroTraining error → 0

(On the slide this reads "even without training"; he means without pre-training.) So for smaller networks, pre-training makes the optimization problem solvable. Large networks don't need it, but they cost more to train.

These were early, small-scale, empirical experiments. The lasting insight: if pre-training helps optimization, then designing better optimizers should help too. Pre-training is one way to optimize better; what are the others?

2 · The regularization view

(The slide heading says "generalization"; he corrects it to "regularization".) What does a regularizer actually do? It constrains the weights to lie in certain regions of parameter space:

  • L2 keeps them inside a circle (the magnitude can't grow beyond it);
  • L1 keeps them inside a diamond;
  • early stopping doesn't let them grow too much.

Pre-training does the same thing. Minimizing $\Omega(\theta)$ layer by layer puts the weights in a region where they capture the characteristics of the input data. Then $\mathcal L(\theta)$ is optimized starting from there. Most of the parameter space is never visited; you may move out of that region, but where you go is governed by where you started.

  1. Regularization as studied earlier minimizes the sum, jointly: $\ \min_\theta\ \mathcal L(\theta)+\Omega(\theta)$.
  2. Pre-training does it sequentially: first $\min_\theta \Omega(\theta)$, then $\min_\theta \mathcal L(\theta)$ starting from that solution.
  3. Same two ingredients, different schedule. Seen this way, pre-training acts as a regularizer.

3 · Robustness to random initialization

A third experiment: train the same network many times (say 100), each time from a different random initialization, and record the final error. Draw the spread as a box plot.

  • Shallow networks: small spread.
  • Deep networks, no pre-training: the error varies a lot from one initialization to the next. The outcome depends heavily on the starting point.
  • Deep networks with pre-training (random $W\to$ pre-trained $W_u\to$ fine-tuned): the spread is low even for deep networks.
So deep networks are sensitive to initialization, and pre-training is a way to escape that. Natural next step: design better initialization methods directly. People did, and training deep networks got better again.

Going beyond the video. The experiments described are from Erhan, Bengio et al., "Why does unsupervised pre-training help deep learning?" (JMLR 2010), and Bengio et al. (2007). Erhan et al. leaned towards the regularization explanation: pre-trained networks reached worse training error in some settings but better test error. Today, with ReLUs, careful initialization, normalization and Adam, layer-wise pre-training of feed-forward networks is rarely needed, which supports the view that it mostly patched problems those later ideas solved directly.

5 · What the 2006–2009 period left behind

Unsupervised pre-training worked, but nobody fully understood why. People examined it through different lenses, each giving suggestive but not conclusive answers:

LensWhat it suggestedWhat followed
OptimizationPre-training makes $\mathcal L(\theta)$ easier to minimizeA series of better optimizers (momentum … Adam)
RegularizationPre-training restricts the weights to a good regionL2/L1, early stopping popularized for deep learning; dropout invented for it
InitializationDeep nets are sensitive to the starting pointBetter weight-initialization strategies
Activation functionsThe gradients themselves may be the problemBetter activation functions

That explains why the course studied optimizers and regularizers, and it sets up the next two topics: activation functions (starting in L59) and weight initialization.

Key takeaways

  1. Optimization concerns training error; regularization concerns test error. The question was which one pre-training improves.
  2. Optimization evidence: with a wide enough last layer, training error reaches zero without pre-training; smaller networks need pre-training to get there.
  3. Regularization view: pre-training minimizes $\Omega$ first, then $\mathcal L$. It is a sequential version of minimizing $\mathcal L+\Omega$, and it confines the weights to a region.
  4. Deep networks without pre-training are very sensitive to random initialization; with pre-training the spread of outcomes is small.
  5. The answers were inconclusive, but they pointed research at four axes: optimization, regularization, initialization and activation functions.
