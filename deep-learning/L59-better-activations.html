Better Activation Functions: Sigmoid, tanh, ReLU and Its Variants

A deep network's power comes from its non-linearities, so the choice of activation function matters, and the thing to watch is its gradient. Sigmoid saturates, isn't zero-centred and is costly to compute. tanh fixes the centring. ReLU fixes saturation on the positive side and is cheap, but its neurons can die. Leaky ReLU, parametric ReLU and ELU keep some gradient flowing for negative inputs.

1 · Why activation functions matter

Take a thin deep network and replace every sigmoid with the identity: $a_i=W_ih_{i-1}$ and $h_i=a_i$ (no non-linearity). Then

  1. $h_1=W_1x$
  2. $h_2=W_2h_1=W_2W_1x$
  3. … and the output is $y=W_4W_3W_2W_1\,x=W x$ with $W=W_4W_3W_2W_1$.

However deep, it is one linear transformation. It can only learn linear decision boundaries (a line, or a hyperplane). For data like brown points inside a ring of blue points, you need a circular boundary, and no line separates them without high error.

Depth alone doesn't give a network its power: the non-linearities do. The universal approximation theorem (L20) needed only one sigmoid layer, but it did need it. The non-linearities come from the activation functions. Is any non-linear function fine, or do some have better properties? Since training is a game of gradients, we judge each by its gradient.

2 · Sigmoid (logistic)

$$\sigma(x)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}},\qquad \sigma'(x)=\sigma(x)\big(1-\sigma(x)\big).$$

It is applied element-wise to the pre-activation vector $a$ and squashes each value into $(0,1)$. It has three problems.

Problem 1: saturated neurons kill gradients

A sigmoid neuron is saturated when its output is at (or close to) 0 or 1. Then $\sigma'=1\cdot(1-1)=0$ or $0\cdot(1-0)=0$, so the gradient vanishes. Backprop to any weight below a sigmoid passes through $\partial h_3/\partial a_3=\sigma'(a_3)$, so if that factor is ≈ 0, the weights get no update. Worse, a neuron saturated at 1 was strongly involved in the output, so it does affect the loss, but that signal never reaches its weights.

Why would it saturate? The neuron outputs $\sigma(\sum_i w_ix_i)$. Inputs are usually standardized, so they are small. But:

  • if weights are initialized large (100, 200, …), $\sum_i w_ix_i$ is large and the neuron saturates. Already at 2.5 the sigmoid is close to 1 (≈ 0.92, with $\sigma'\approx0.07$); large negative weights saturate it at 0;
  • even with small weights, a layer with a thousand inputs sums a thousand terms. Reaching 2 or 3 is not surprising.

So weights must be initialized carefully: the link to the weight-initialization lectures.

Problem 2: not zero-centred

Outputs lie in $(0,1)$, centred around 0.5. Why does that hurt? Take an output neuron fed by two sigmoid neurons: $a_3=w_1h_{21}+w_2h_{22}$, then $h_3=\sigma(a_3)$. By the chain rule:

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_1}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial h_3}\frac{\partial h_3}{\partial a_3}}_{\text{common to both}}\,h_{21},\qquad \frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial w_2}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial y}\frac{\partial y}{\partial h_3}\frac{\partial h_3}{\partial a_3}}_{\text{common to both}}\,h_{22}.$$
  1. $h_{21},h_{22}$ are outputs of sigmoid neurons, so both are positive.
  2. The sign of each derivative is therefore the sign of the common factor, which is the same for both.
  3. So the gradient vector is either all positive or all negative. With $n$ inputs, the same argument holds for all $n$ derivatives.

In the $(\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_1,\ \partial\mathcal L/\partial w_2)$ plane, only the first and third quadrants are possible: half the directions are thrown away. If the optimum lies in a direction with one component up and one down, you must zig-zag to it, taking more steps. (It's like being told to drive from A to B but forbidden certain kinds of turns.) If the inputs came from a function with range $(-1,1)$, the signs could differ and any direction would be possible.

Problem 3: computationally expensive

Every evaluation needs an exponential. There are fast ways to compute it, but it is still work.

3 · tanh

$$\tanh(x)=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}\in(-1,1),\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\tanh(x)=1-\tanh^2(x).$$

tanh is not new (used since the 1990s), but it fixes one problem: it is zero-centred, so the limited-directions problem goes away. It still saturates: when $\tanh=\pm1$ the derivative $1-\tanh^2$ is 0, which is clear from the flat ends of the graph. It is still expensive (exponentials).

4 · ReLU (rectified linear unit)

$$f(x)=\max(0,x),\qquad f'(x)=\begin{cases}0 & x<0\\1 & x>0\end{cases}$$

Proposed about a decade ago in the context of convolutional networks, and still a de facto choice across feed-forward networks, RNNs and Transformers (though GELU is perhaps the Transformer default). In a network, the pre-activation $a_{12}=\sum_iw_ix_i$ goes in: if positive it passes through unchanged, if negative it is clamped to 0.

Is it really non-linear? Yes: linear on each side but bent at 0. In fact, the difference of two ReLU units looks like a piecewise-linear sigmoid, close to a step function, and adjusting the parameters gives an even better step. (The capped version drawn on the slide is called ReLU6, after its maximum value of 6.)

Neurons can die

Dead neurons

  1. A neuron computes $a_1=w_1x_1+w_2x_2+b$, then $h_1=\max(0,a_1)$. Suppose one training example sends a large gradient into $b$, and the update makes $b$ very negative. Nothing contrived: this can happen.
  2. Now $a_1<0$ whatever $x_1,x_2$ are, so $h_1=0$ and $\partial h_1/\partial a_1=0$.
  3. Every gradient through this neuron has that factor: $\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_1=\partial\mathcal L/\partial w_2=\partial\mathcal L/\partial b=0$. Nothing updates.
  4. Next example: $b$ is still very negative, so the neuron is dead again, and again… Once dead, it stays dead forever.

In practice, more than 50% of ReLU units can die if the learning rate is set too high (a large gradient times a large learning rate is exactly what pushes $b$ far negative). Remedies: set the learning rate carefully, initialize the bias to a positive value such as 0.01 (fairly large, given how many terms add into the sum), or use a variant.

5 · ReLU variants

VariantFormulaWhat it adds
Leaky ReLU$\max(0.1x,\ x)$A small slope for $x<0$: some gradient always flows, so it doesn't saturate or die; still cheap; outputs closer to zero-centred, so the one-direction problem is eased
Parametric ReLU$\max(\alpha x,\ x)$Why 0.1? Make $\alpha$ a parameter: start at 0.1 and update it by backprop like any weight, $\alpha\leftarrow\alpha-\eta\,\partial\mathcal L/\partial\alpha$
ELU (exponential linear unit)$x$ if $x>0$; $\ a(e^x-1)$ otherwiseSmooth exponential decay on the negative side; ReLU's benefits, some gradient always, closer to zero-centred, but expensive again (exponent)

Each was proposed in a specific context, and some came with theory explaining why they beat ReLU. But in practice they are not very popular: plain ReLU is still the default, or you use the newer GELU and Swish (L61).

Going beyond the video. Most libraries default the leaky slope to 0.01, not 0.1; the lecture's 0.1 is just as valid. The "decade-old paper" is Krizhevsky, Sutskever and Hinton (AlexNet, 2012), which reported ReLU networks reaching a training-error target about six times faster than tanh ones.

Key takeaways

  1. Without non-linear activations a deep network collapses to one linear map; the activation functions supply its power, and their gradients decide how well it trains.
  2. Sigmoid: saturates at 0 and 1 (gradient $\sigma(1-\sigma)\to0$), is not zero-centred (all of a neuron's input-weight gradients share one sign, so updates zig-zag), and needs an exponent.
  3. Saturation comes from large weights or simply many inputs adding up, which is why initialization matters.
  4. tanh fixes zero-centring but still saturates; ReLU doesn't saturate for $x>0$ and is cheap, but a very negative bias can kill a neuron permanently (watch the learning rate, start biases at a small positive value).
  5. Leaky ReLU, PReLU (learned slope) and ELU keep gradient flowing for $x<0$, yet plain ReLU (and now GELU/Swish) remain the usual choices.
