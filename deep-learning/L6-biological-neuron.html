Biological Neurons: Where the Artificial Neuron Comes From

The most basic unit of a neural network is the artificial neuron, and the name comes from biology. A biological neuron receives signals, processes them, and if it is excited enough, passes a signal on. The brain wires about 100 billion of them into layers, each doing a more abstract job. That layered, massively parallel picture is the one the whole course builds on.

If neurons in the brain can do complex processing, can artificial ones do useful computation?

3 · A cartoon of how it works

You're watching a comedy (the lecture's example is a scene with Sheldon). Your eyes see it and your ears hear it; the sense organs relay this to neurons. A neuron processes the signal and, if it is excited enough, fires and signals other neurons, and eventually you laugh.

In reality it isn't one neuron but a network arranged in layers. The lowest layer talks to the sense organs; some of those neurons get excited and activate neurons in the next layer, and so on, until a physical response (laughter) is triggered. This layered architecture will appear again and again in the course.

4 · Massively parallel, with a division of work

The average human brain has about $10^{11}$ (100 billion) neurons, interconnected and working in parallel. They also divide the work: a neuron doesn't handle visual and auditory and every other kind of input. The lecture's cartoon:

  • one neuron fires only if the visual is funny,
  • one fires only if the speech or sound is funny,
  • one fires only if the text (what is being said) is funny,
  • and a fourth neuron fires if at least two of the three are on, then activates others or triggers an action.

The lecture's four-neuron cartoon. Each sensory neuron fires when its stimulus passes 0.5. The fourth neuron counts how many fired and fires if the count reaches its threshold (the lecture uses "at least two of three").

Keep this cartoon in mind: a unit that counts its active inputs and fires if the count reaches a threshold is exactly the McCulloch–Pitts neuron of the next lecture.

5 · Layers in the visual cortex

A more real example. Light hits the retina (the input). The signal passes through several layers of the brain, each doing some processing and passing it on, until an action is generated and sent to the spinal cord, say to move your hand. Focus on the layers labelled V1, V2, V4, in a very simplified story:

  1. First layer: detects only dots, edges and corners.
  2. Next layer: groups them into feature groups: these edges form a nose, these form a mouth, these dots are eyes.
  3. Next layer: combines the groups into a bigger object: a face.

Each layer does a different job, and each does more abstract processing than the one before.

A toy three-layer "visual cortex". Pick an input and step through the layers. Layer 1 finds edges, layer 2 groups them into parts, layer 3 checks whether the parts form a face.

Going beyond the video. This toy is mine; real visual cortex is far more complicated, and the lecture's own disclaimer applies: the explanation suits this course, not a biology course. The idea it shows is the one the lecture stresses: later layers depend on arrangements of what earlier layers found, so jumbled parts pass layer 2 but fail layer 3. Convolutional networks build features in a similar hierarchy.

Key takeaways

  1. A biological neuron: dendrites receive, synapses connect, the soma processes, the axon sends the output on.
  2. A neuron fires when it is excited enough by its inputs; that idea becomes a threshold.
  3. The brain is a massively parallel network of about $10^{11}$ neurons, arranged in layers, with a division of work.
  4. Layers build abstraction: edges, then parts, then objects (V1 → V2 → V4).
  5. Prof. Khapra's disclaimer: this is a cartoon of the brain, enough to motivate artificial neural networks.
