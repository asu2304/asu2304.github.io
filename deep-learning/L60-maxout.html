Maxout: Letting the Strongest Unit Specialize

In bagging, each model gets to overfit its own bucket of data. In dropout, each sub-network is sampled so rarely that it never gets the chance to specialize. Maxout restores some of that: compute several affine functions of the input and pass on only the largest, so the gradient flows only to the unit that responded most strongly. The result also generalizes ReLU and its variants.

1 · Recap: model averaging lets each model overfit its bucket

Bagging (L53): from a dataset of 1000 points, build a new dataset of 1000 by sampling with replacement. Pick a random example, copy it into the bucket, leave the original in place, and repeat until the bucket holds 1000. Do this $K$ times and train model $k$ (all its parameters together called $W_k$) on bucket $k$.

  • Each bucket contains many duplicates (he quotes roughly half of the samples).
  • So each model sees the same examples again and again, and gets to overfit its own bucket.
  • At inference, combine the $K$ predictions: arithmetic mean, geometric mean, max or voting.
Going beyond the video: how many duplicates? The chance a given point is never picked in $n$ draws is $(1-1/n)^n\approx e^{-1}\approx0.368$. So a bootstrap bucket contains only about 63% of the distinct original points, and about 37% of its entries are repeats. See my ML note on bagging.

2 · The gap in dropout

Dropout was the cheap substitute for training many models. But there is a difference:

BaggingDropout
Number of models$K$, each trained fullyExponentially many sub-networks, sharing weights
How often a model sees a sampleMany times (duplicates, many epochs)A given sub-network is sampled so rarely it may not see a sample even once
SpecializationEach model overfits its bucketNo sub-network gets to specialize

Could we make a larger update whenever a sub-network does see a sample? Raising the learning rate is risky in general, and because parameters are shared across sub-networks, a big step for one disturbs all the others. At inference, dropout scales each neuron by $p$, effectively assuming all neurons were equally on and equally sensitive to all examples. Can we close this gap?

3 · From dropout to max

Hidden layer $h_{11},\dots,h_{14}$ with a dropout mask: say only $h_{11}$ and $h_{14}$ are on. Then

$$a_{21}=w_{11}h_{11}+w_{14}h_{14},\qquad a_{22}=w_{21}h_{11}+w_{24}h_{14}.$$

Suppose both are positive (ReLU, so neither is dead) and $0<a_{21}<a_{22}$. Then neuron 22 is reacting more strongly to this training example (like an MP neuron firing harder for a certain input pattern). We would like to respect that preference: let neuron 22 specialize for this example and leave neuron 21, which showed little interest, alone. Take the max:

$$h_{21}=\max(a_{21},a_{22})=a_{22}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\partial h_{21}}{\partial a_{22}}=1,\qquad \frac{\partial h_{21}}{\partial a_{21}}=0 .$$
The gradient flows only to the winner. Different units end up adapted to different training examples, which is the specialization model averaging enjoyed.

$a_{21}$ and $a_{22}$ are two linear (really affine, since a bias can be added) transformations of the same input, and we keep the larger. Nothing restricts us to two:

$$h=\max_{i=1,\dots,k}\big(w_i^\top x+b_i\big).$$

Comparing what happens in a layer:

  • Dropout: nodes are either dropped (no part in the computation) or kept. Every kept node gets gradient, strong or weak responder alike, so none specializes on this input. At inference all participate with equal weight.
  • Maxout: one maxout neuron holds $k$ affine units $w_1^\top x,\ w_2^\top x,\ w_3^\top x$; only the max passes forward. The other two are effectively dropped as well: among the units that take part, only the strongest is active.

4 · Maxout generalizes ReLU, and more

Pieces inside one maxout neuronResult
$0\cdot x+0$ and $1\cdot x+0$$\max(0,x)$ = ReLU
$0.1x$ and $x$$\max(0.1x,x)$ = leaky ReLU (learn the slope and it's PReLU)
$0.5x$ and $-0.5x$a V shape, $0.5\lvert x\rvert$: the absolute value
4 suitably chosen linesa piecewise-linear approximation of $x^2$

(The transcript's version of leaky ReLU, "max of 0 and 0.1x", is garbled; the formula above is the one that makes sense.)

  • No saturation, no death: the max always passes through some affine piece, so gradient always flows.
  • Cost: parameters grow in proportion to $k$.
  • Power: there is a proof that two maxout neurons, each with enough affine pieces, form a universal approximator.

Despite all this, maxout isn't very popular: its special cases, ReLU and its variants, are used far more. It is still worth knowing for the dropout analogy, for seeing how it generalizes ReLU, and for the two-neuron universality.

Going beyond the video. Maxout is from Goodfellow et al. (2013), designed as a companion to dropout. A single maxout unit is always convex (a max of lines), so it cannot fit $\sin(2x)$ above however large $k$ is. The universality result uses the difference of two maxout units, because any continuous function can be approximated by a difference of two convex piecewise-linear ones. Also note that a piece which never wins anywhere gets no gradient at all: the unit as a whole can't die, but individual pieces can sit idle.

Key takeaways

  1. Bagging lets each model overfit its own bucket; dropout's rarely-sampled, weight-sharing sub-networks never get to specialize.
  2. Maxout passes on $\max_i(w_i^\top x+b_i)$; the gradient reaches only the winning affine unit, so units specialize on the inputs they respond to most.
  3. ReLU, leaky ReLU and PReLU are maxout with two pieces; more pieces give $\lvert x\rvert$, approximations of $x^2$, and so on.
  4. No saturation and no dead units, at the cost of $k$ times the parameters; two maxout neurons with enough pieces are universal approximators.
  5. Still, ReLU and its variants are far more popular in practice.
