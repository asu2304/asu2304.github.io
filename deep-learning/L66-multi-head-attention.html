Multi-Head Attention and the Transformer Encoder Layer

One scaled dot-product unit is one head: one learned way of deciding which words matter to which. Just as a CNN uses many filters, a transformer runs several heads side by side, each with its own $W_Q,W_K,W_V$, concatenates their outputs word by word, and applies a linear map. Add a small feed-forward network applied to every position and you have one encoder layer; stack 6–12 of them and you have the encoder.

1 · One head

Recap of the self-attention block from L65, now called one head of scaled dot-product self-attention:

  1. Stack the word representations $h_1,\dots,h_T$ into $H$.
  2. Linear projections, three matrix products in parallel: $Q=W_QH$, $K=W_KH$, $V=W_VH$.
  3. Inside the head: $Z=\text{softmax}(Q^\top K/\sqrt{d_k})\,V^\top$, giving $z_1,\dots,z_T$, refined representations that carry context.

Nothing stops you from having a second copy of the same block with its own parameters $W_Q^{(2)},W_K^{(2)},W_V^{(2)}$. Head 1 gives $z^{(1)}_1,\dots,z^{(1)}_T$, head 2 gives $z^{(2)}_1,\dots,z^{(2)}_T$. Two questions: why bother, and which output do you use?

2 · Why several heads?

We've seen this argument with convolutional networks: several filters slide over the same image, each with its own weights, in the hope that each captures a different characteristic (one edges, another blur, …). The same holds here. One head learns one way of capturing context, but there may be more than one useful way.

Take “The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired”. When building the representation of “it”:

  • you may want high attention on animal, because “it” is the pronoun for the animal;
  • you may also want high attention on was, the verb of which “it” is the subject.

One row of $\alpha$ has to share its weight between the two. With two heads, one can put a high $\alpha$ on “animal” and the other on “was”.

No one tells the heads what to do. There is no signal saying “head 1, focus on the pronoun's referent”. If attending to “was” helps reduce the loss, the projections $W$ of some head will be pushed so that its $\alpha$ does that. Multiple heads just give the model more freedom (more projection matrices, hence more possible $\alpha$ patterns). The lecture shows that in an actual trained transformer with two heads, one head did attend mostly to “animal” and the other to “was”.

In the demo the two heads have hand-set weights mimicking that finding; switch to random heads to see that each projection simply produces its own pattern.

3 · Combining the heads

Simple: concatenate the head outputs for each word, then pass the result through a linear transformation (often written $W_O$):

$$z_i=W_O\big[\,z^{(1)}_i\,;\,z^{(2)}_i\,;\,\dots\,;\,z^{(h)}_i\,\big].$$

The concatenation is per word: all heads' $z_1$'s are joined, then all heads' $z_2$'s, and so on. $T$ embeddings go in and $T$ come out, at every layer.

The lecture's dimension walk-through:

  1. Two heads, $h_i\in\mathbb R^{512}$. Project down: each head's $q,k,v$ are 256-dimensional, so each head outputs 256-dimensional $z$'s.
  2. Concatenating two 256-dim vectors gives 512 again. Then any linear map you like: $512\times1024$, $512\times256$ or $512\times512$; with the last, the final $z$'s are 512-dim.
  3. Typically there are 8 heads. If each produced 512 dims, the concatenation would be 4096-dim, which is too large: it blows up the parameters of the next linear map.
  4. So choose the per-head size to undo the concatenation: multiply each 512-dim $h$ by a $64\times512$ matrix, get 64-dim outputs per head, and $8\times64=512$ after concatenating.

4 · The feed-forward network

Back to the encoder layer: multi-head self-attention turns $h_1,\dots,h_T$ into $s_1,\dots,s_T$. Then each $s_i$ goes through a feed-forward network, and that's all there is to it:

$$m_i=g\big(W_1^{\text{FFN}}s_i\big),\qquad z_i=W_2^{\text{FFN}}m_i ,$$

for example $512\to1024\to512$, where $g$ is any non-linearity, typically ReLU-based or GELU. Two points:

  • The same parameters $W_1^{\text{FFN}},W_2^{\text{FFN}}$ are used at every position: each $s_i$ passes through an identical network.
  • It mixes nothing across words; it just projects whatever the attention produced. All the cross-word mixing happens in attention.

5 · The encoder: stacked identical layers

One encoder layer = two sub-layers: multi-head self-attention, then the feed-forward network. The encoder is a stack of $N$ identical layers (6, 8, 12, … depending on the architecture); layer $\ell$'s outputs $z_1,\dots,z_T$ are layer $\ell+1$'s inputs.

RNN encoderTransformer encoder
within a layer (“horizontally”)sequential: $z_1$, then $z_2$, …parallel: all $T$ outputs from matrix products
across layerssequentialstill sequential: layer 2 needs layer 1's output

The last layer's outputs are called $e_1,\dots,e_T$: one refined representation per input token, contextual and passed through several deep layers of abstraction. They are what the decoder will attend to.

Going beyond the video: two pieces these videos skip. (1) Residual connections and layer normalisation. In the original transformer each sub-layer is wrapped as $\text{LayerNorm}(x+\text{SubLayer}(x))$; without these, deep stacks are hard to train. (2) Positional encodings. Self-attention treats its inputs as a set: shuffle the words and the outputs are just shuffled the same way. Word order has to be injected by adding a position-dependent vector to each embedding. Also, the original paper used $d_{\text{model}}=512$, 8 heads of 64 and an FFN hidden size of 2048 (the lecture's 1024 is an example). More in my transformer revision notes.

Key takeaways

  1. One head = one scaled dot-product self-attention unit with its own $W_Q,W_K,W_V$.
  2. Several heads, like several CNN filters, can capture different kinds of context (e.g. “it” → animal and “it” → was); the loss, not a label, decides what each learns.
  3. Head outputs are concatenated per word and linearly transformed; with 8 heads of 64 dims the concatenation is back to 512.
  4. The feed-forward network ($512\to1024\to512$, ReLU/GELU) is the same network applied at every position.
  5. An encoder layer = multi-head attention + FFN; $N$ identical layers are stacked. Parallel within a layer, sequential across layers; the final outputs are $e_1,\dots,e_T$.
