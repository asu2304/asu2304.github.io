Lecture 7 · A brief history of deep learning

Beating Humans at Their Own Games, the Madness, and the Need for Sanity

Deep reinforcement learning beat humans at Atari, Go, poker and Dota. Meanwhile the field exploded: GANs, ResNets, transformers, BERT, GPT and a new architecture every week (the "madness"). Then the hard questions arrived: why does any of this work, can we trust it, and why can an imperceptible change to an image fool it? That is the "need for sanity", and the reason this course insists on the fundamentals.

1 · Beating humans at their own games

  • 2015Atari (DeepMind's DQN, Nature): one network learns 49 Atari games from raw pixels and the score, reaching human-level play on many of them.
  • 2016Go: AlphaGo beats Lee Sedol 4–1. Go has far more possible positions than chess, so brute-force search was thought hopeless.
  • 2017Poker: DeepStack and Libratus beat professionals at heads-up no-limit Texas hold'em, a game of imperfect information (you can't see the other hand).
  • 2019Dota 2 and StarCraft II: OpenAI Five beats the world champion team; AlphaStar reaches Grandmaster level. Long horizons, teamwork, partial observation.

2 · The madness (2013 onward)

The number of deep learning papers, models and applications grew explosively. A small sample of what arrived in a few years:

Generating data
Variational autoencoders (2013), generative adversarial networks (2014), and later diffusion models: networks that produce realistic images, audio and text.
Going very deep
ResNets (2015) use skip connections to train networks with 100+ layers; DenseNets and others follow.
Language
Transformers (2017), BERT (2018), GPT-2 and GPT-3 (2019–20), and the large language models that followed.

Alongside: image captioning, visual question answering, video generation, neural machine translation in production, protein structure prediction, and models applied to almost every field.

3 · The need for sanity

  • Why does it work? Deep networks are heavily over-parameterised and their losses are non-convex, yet they train and generalise. Theory is still catching up (the "paradox" of deep learning).
  • Can we explain it? Interpretability: which inputs led to this decision? Which neurons detect what?
  • Is it robust? Adversarial examples (Szegedy et al., 2013; Goodfellow et al., 2014): a perturbation too small for a human to see can flip a network's prediction, e.g. a "panda" confidently classified as a "gibbon".
  • Is it fair and safe? Bias in data and models, privacy and misuse.

Prof. Khapra's point: with so much hype, a solid grasp of the fundamentals is what lets you separate real progress from noise. That is what the rest of the course builds.

4 · Why an invisible change can flip a decision

Even a linear classifier $s=w^\top x$ is vulnerable. Change every pixel by a tiny $\varepsilon$ in the direction of $-\text{sign}(w_i)$. Each pixel moves by at most $\varepsilon$, but the score drops by $\varepsilon\sum_i|w_i|=\varepsilon\|w\|_1$, which grows with the number of pixels $d$.

Going beyond the video. This is Goodfellow et al.'s "linear explanation" of adversarial examples and the idea behind the fast gradient sign method, $x'=x+\varepsilon\,\text{sign}(\nabla_x \text{loss})$. Here the image and weights are random stand-ins, not a trained model.

Key takeaways

  1. Deep RL beat humans at Atari (2015), Go (2016), poker (2017), Dota 2 and StarCraft II (2019).
  2. The "madness": VAEs, GANs, ResNets, transformers, BERT, GPT and an explosion of papers and applications.
  3. The "need for sanity": why it works, interpretability, adversarial robustness, fairness and safety.
  4. High dimension alone makes tiny per-pixel changes add up to a large change in score.
