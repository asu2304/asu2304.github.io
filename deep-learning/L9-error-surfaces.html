Errors and Error Surfaces

Fix the bias, and the number of mistakes a perceptron makes becomes a function of its weights. Plot that function over the $(w_1,w_2)$ plane and you get an error surface: a staircase of flat regions at 0, 1, 2 or 3 errors. Any weights in the zero-error region implement the function. Reading this off a picture works in two dimensions; for $n$ inputs we need an algorithm, which is the next lecture.

1 · Counting errors for one choice of weights

Take the AND function: output 1 only for $(1,1)$, and 0 for the other three inputs. The perceptron outputs 1 iff $w_0+w_1x_1+w_2x_2\ge0$. Fix $w_0=-1$ and try a few $(w_1,w_2)$, counting the inputs that land in the wrong half-space:

$(w_1,w_2)$Line $-1+w_1x_1+w_2x_2=0$What goes wrongErrors
$(-1,-1)$$-1-x_1-x_2=0$$(1,1)$ should be positive but falls in the negative half-space1
$(1.5,0)$$-1+1.5x_1=0$$(1,0)$ should be negative but falls in the positive half-space1
$(10,-10)$$-1+10x_1-10x_2=0$$(1,0)$ wrongly positive and $(1,1)$ wrongly negative2
"Above" or "below" the line doesn't define the sides. With $(w_1,w_2)=(-1,-1)$, all four inputs lie above the line, yet they're in its negative half-space. What decides the side is only which inequality a point satisfies: $\ge0$ (positive) or $<0$ (negative).

2 · Error as a function of the weights

With $w_0$ fixed, the number of errors depends only on $w_1$ and $w_2$. So think of it as a function, $\text{error}(w_1,w_2)$. It can only take whole-number values, which makes its graph a step function: flat regions separated by sudden jumps. The lecture colours the levels 0, 1, 2 and 3.

Why never 4? The lecture's argument: at worst every point falls in one half-space, and then either the positive point or the negative points are still right. More precisely, with $w_0=-1$ the input $(0,0)$ always gets $w_0<0$, so it is always (correctly) classified 0.

A sharper count. With $w_0=-1$ even 3 errors can't happen: all three of $(0,1),(1,0),(1,1)$ wrong would need $w_2\ge1$, $w_1\ge1$ and $w_1+w_2<1$ at once, which is impossible. So for AND at $w_0=-1$ the surface only has levels 0, 1 and 2, which is what the demo shows. Other values of $w_0$ (try the slider) reach 3, and positive $w_0$ even 4.

3 · The error surface

The lecture plots this function for every $(w_1,w_2)$ in $[-4,4]^2$, by simply plugging the weights in and counting errors, which is exactly what the demo below does. The dark blue region has zero errors: pick any weights there and the perceptron implements AND. The other levels are the 1-, 2- and 3-error regions.

Looking at the picture and choosing a point in the zero region works for two weights. With $n$ inputs we can't even draw the surface. So we want an algorithm that, whenever the data are linearly separable, finds weights that put every positive point and every negative point on the correct side. That is the perceptron learning algorithm.

Going beyond the video. (1) The bound on errors relies on $w_0$ being negative. Make $w_0$ positive in the demo and $(0,0)$ flips to the positive side; then 4 errors become possible for AND, for example $w_0=1,\ w_1=w_2=-0.9$ (which is a perfect NAND). (2) For XOR the zero region is empty for every $w_0$: that's what "not linearly separable" looks like on an error surface. (3) This surface is flat almost everywhere, so its gradient is zero or undefined. That's why later lectures replace the hard threshold with a smooth sigmoid, so that gradient descent has a slope to follow.

Key takeaways

  1. For fixed weights, count the inputs in the wrong half-space: that's the error.
  2. Which side a point is on is decided by the sign of $w_0+w_1x_1+w_2x_2$, not by "above" or "below" the line.
  3. With $w_0$ fixed, error is a function of $(w_1,w_2)$; plotted, it's a step-shaped error surface.
  4. Any weights in the zero-error region implement the function, but finding them by eye doesn't scale to $n$ inputs, so we need a learning algorithm.
