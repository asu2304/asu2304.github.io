Lecture 9 · McCulloch–Pitts neuron, perceptrons

Perceptrons, Errors and Error Surfaces

Rosenblatt's perceptron fixes three of the McCulloch–Pitts neuron's limits: inputs can be real numbers, each input gets its own weight, and the weights (including the threshold, folded in as a bias $w_0=-\theta$) can be learnt. It is still a line, but now we can ask which line, measure how many points a choice of weights gets wrong, and picture that count as an error surface to search.

1 · The perceptron

Proposed by Frank Rosenblatt (1958) and refined and analysed by Minsky and Papert (1969):

$$y=\begin{cases}1 & \sum_{i=1}^{n}w_i x_i\ge\theta\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad y=\begin{cases}1 & \sum_{i=0}^{n}w_i x_i\ge 0\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\quad\text{with } x_0=1,\ w_0=-\theta.$$

Moving $\theta$ to the left-hand side turns it into an ordinary weight $w_0$ on a constant input $x_0=1$: the bias. Now everything the neuron does is set by the vector $w$, and learning means finding $w$.

2 · What the bias means: choosing a movie

Inputs: $x_1$ = "Matt Damon is in it", $x_2$ = "it is a thriller", $x_3$ = "Christopher Nolan directed it". The weights say how much each factor matters to you. The bias $w_0$ is your prior, how picky you are:

  • A movie buff will watch almost anything: $\theta$ near 0, so $w_0$ is close to 0.
  • A Nolan fan only watches Nolan films: a high $\theta$ that only a large $w_3 x_3$ can clear.

The weights and the bias are learnt from your past choices, not hand-coded.

3 · Perceptron vs McCulloch–Pitts

McCulloch–Pitts (1943)Perceptron (1958)
Inputsbooleanreal (boolean is a special case)
Weightsall equal (1), plus inhibitory vetoesone learnt weight per input, any sign
Thresholdset by handlearnt, as the bias $w_0$
Decision boundarya line / hyperplane in both: $\sum_i w_ix_i=0$. Both need linearly separable data.

4 · Implementing OR, and the system of inequalities

For OR, the four inputs give four conditions on $(w_0,w_1,w_2)$:

$$\begin{aligned} (0,0)\to 0:&\quad w_0<0\\ (0,1)\to 1:&\quad w_0+w_2\ge 0\\ (1,0)\to 1:&\quad w_0+w_1\ge 0\\ (1,1)\to 1:&\quad w_0+w_1+w_2\ge 0 \end{aligned}$$

One solution: $w_0=-1$, $w_1=1.1$, $w_2=1.1$. There are infinitely many. Solving inequalities by hand doesn't scale, so we need an algorithm (L10). First, a way to see how good any particular $w$ is.

5 · Errors and the error surface

Fix $w_0$ and look at every $(w_1,w_2)$. For each, count how many of the four inputs the perceptron gets wrong. That count, drawn over the $(w_1,w_2)$ plane, is the error surface. Learning = finding a point where the error is 0.

Going beyond the video. The error surface is piecewise constant: it jumps between 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 and is flat in between, so its gradient is zero almost everywhere. That is why the perceptron can't be trained by gradient descent on the error count, and why later lectures switch to smooth (sigmoid) neurons and smooth losses.

Key takeaways

  1. Perceptron: $y=1$ iff $\sum_{i=0}^n w_ix_i\ge0$, with real inputs, learnt weights and bias $w_0=-\theta$.
  2. The bias encodes a prior (how picky the decision-maker is).
  3. Still a hyperplane: like the MP neuron, it needs linearly separable data.
  4. OR → a system of inequalities in $w$; the error count over weight space is the error surface, and learning means reaching its zero region.
