Lecture 4: Factorization into \(A = LU\)

This summary is based on the lecture's transcript on MIT OCW.

Topics:

  1. Inverse of a product, and inverse of a transpose
  2. \(A = LU\), the matrix form of Gaussian elimination
  3. The cost of elimination (operation count)
  4. Permutation matrices, as a preview of row exchanges

1. Inverse of a product

\[(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\]

The inverses come in reverse order. Check it using associativity:

\[(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I\]

Strang's analogy: you put on socks, then shoes. To undo this, you take off the shoes first, then the socks.

2. Inverse of a transpose

Start from \(AA^{-1} = I\) and transpose both sides. A transpose of a product also reverses order, \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\), and \(I^\top = I\). So

\[(A^{-1})^\top A^\top = I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad (A^\top)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^\top\]

For a single matrix, transposing and inverting can be done in either order.

3. \(A = LU\) for a 2×2 matrix

Take

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 8 & 7 \end{bmatrix}\]
Strang avoids a 4 in the (2,2) position, because that would make the second pivot 0 and the matrix singular.

Elimination. The multiplier is 4. Subtracting 4 × row 1 from row 2 is the matrix

\[E_{21} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad E_{21}A = U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\]

Move \(E\) to the other side. Multiply both sides by \(E_{21}^{-1}\):

\[A = E_{21}^{-1}U = LU, \qquad L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]
The inverse has a plus sign, because it adds back what \(E_{21}\) removed.

What \(L\) and \(U\) look like.

  • \(L\) is lower triangular with 1s on the diagonal.
  • \(U\) is upper triangular with the pivots on the diagonal.

The \(LDU\) variant

Pull the pivots out of \(U\) into a diagonal matrix \(D\). Dividing each row of \(U\) by its pivot leaves 1s on the diagonal:

\[A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & \tfrac12 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = LDU\]

Now both triangular factors have 1s on the diagonal, and the pivots sit in \(D\). Strang mostly uses plain \(LU\).

4. \(A = LU\) for a 3×3 matrix (no row exchanges)

Forward elimination as matrices:

\[E_{32}\,E_{31}\,E_{21}\,A = U\]

Moving the \(E\)'s to the right. Invert each one, in reverse order:

\[A = E_{21}^{-1}\,E_{31}^{-1}\,E_{32}^{-1}\,U = LU\]

Why \(L\) is better than \(E\)

Example. Suppose the multiplier for step (2,1) is 2, step (3,1) needs nothing (\(E_{31} = I\)), and the multiplier for step (3,2) is 5.

The forward product \(E = E_{32}E_{21}\) is messy:

\[E = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ -2 & 1 & 0 \\ \mathbf{10} & -5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

The 10 appears because row 1 affects row 3 indirectly. Two copies of row 1 were removed from row 2, and then five copies of the new row 2 were removed from row 3. In total, 10 copies of row 1 entered row 3.

The inverse product \(L = E_{21}^{-1}E_{32}^{-1}\) is clean:

\[L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 5 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\]

In this order, the multipliers don't interfere with each other. No 10 appears.

The key result

If there are no row exchanges, the multipliers go directly into \(L\).

Building \(L\) takes no extra work: record each multiplier \(\ell_{ij}\) in position \((i,j)\).

Practical consequence. As elimination proceeds, you save each new row of \(U\) and each multiplier into \(L\). After that, you no longer need \(A\), because everything is stored in \(L\) and \(U\).

5. How much does elimination cost?

What counts as one operation: one multiply plus one subtract, as in "multiply row 1 by \(\ell\) and subtract it from row \(i\)".

Cost step by step, for an \(n\times n\) matrix with no zeros:

  • The first stage clears column 1. It touches about \(n^2\) entries.
  • The second stage works on the remaining \((n-1)\times(n-1)\) block, costing about \((n-1)^2\).
  • The stages keep shrinking down to \(1^2\).

Total cost for \(A\):

\[n^2 + (n-1)^2 + \cdots + 1^2 \;\approx\; \int_0^n x^2\,dx = \frac{n^3}{3}\]

The cost is not the full \(n \cdot n^2 = n^3\), because the matrices being worked on get smaller at each stage. That shrinking is where the factor \(\tfrac13\) comes from.

Cost for each right-hand side \(b\): about \(n^2\). This covers carrying \(b\) through elimination plus back substitution.

Practical consequence. Factor \(A = LU\) once, at a cost of \(\sim n^3/3\). Then each new \(b\) costs only \(\sim n^2\).

Comparison with determinants. Strang gives determinants a "bad mark": computing them by expansion costs on the order of \(n!\), which is far worse.

6. Permutation matrices

Row exchanges are needed when a zero appears in a pivot position. A permutation matrix is the identity matrix with its rows reordered.

All 3×3 permutation matrices (there are 6):

  • The identity \(I\) (no exchange).
  • Three that exchange one pair of rows: \(P_{12}\), \(P_{13}\), \(P_{23}\).
  • Two that cycle all three rows. For example, \(\begin{bmatrix} 0&1&0\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{bmatrix}\) moves row 2 up to row 1, row 3 up to row 2, and row 1 down to row 3.

Properties:

  • The 6 matrices form a group. The product of two permutation matrices is again on the list, and so is the inverse of any one.
  • The inverse is the transpose: \(P^{-1} = P^\top\).
  • A single exchange undoes itself. For example, \(P_{12}^{-1} = P_{12}\).
  • The count is \(n!\). There are 6 for 3×3 and 24 for 4×4.

In the next lecture, permutation matrices are used to handle elimination when row exchanges are needed.

Big ideas from Lecture 4

  1. Reverse order: \((AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}\) and \((AB)^\top = B^\top A^\top\).
  2. Transpose and inverse commute: \((A^\top)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^\top\).
  3. \(A = LU\): \(L\) is lower triangular with 1s on the diagonal and holds the multipliers. \(U\) is upper triangular with the pivots on the diagonal. This is Gaussian elimination written as a factorization.
  4. \(L\) is clean, \(E\) is not: in \(L\) the multipliers sit untouched, while in \(E\) they mix (the stray 10).
  5. Cost: about \(\tfrac{n^3}{3}\) operations to factor \(A\), and about \(n^2\) for each right-hand side.
  6. Permutations: there are \(n!\) of them, they form a group, and \(P^{-1} = P^\top\).
