Gradient-Boosted Decision Trees in Practice

D1 built gradient boosting for any loss. Here it becomes the classifier people actually use: gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDT) with the logistic loss. The model outputs log-odds, each tree is fitted to $y-p$, and each leaf's value comes from one Newton step. Then the knobs: number of rounds, learning rate, tree depth, leaf size, subsampling and early stopping, each read through the bias–variance lens of L66.

1 · Classification: boost the log-odds

Labels are $y\in\{0,1\}$. As in logistic regression (L56), the model produces a real-valued score $F(x)$ that is read as log-odds, and squashed by the sigmoid into a probability:

$$p(x)=\sigma\big(F(x)\big)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-F(x)}},\qquad F(x)=\log\frac{p(x)}{1-p(x)} .$$

The difference from L56: there, $F(x)=w^\top x$ was linear. Here $F=F_M$ is a sum of trees, so the boundary $F(x)=0$ can bend wherever the data needs it to. The loss is the usual negative log-likelihood (cross-entropy):

$$\ell(y,F)=-\big[y\log p+(1-y)\log(1-p)\big],\qquad p=\sigma(F).$$

Its derivatives with respect to the score $F$ are short, using $\sigma'(F)=p(1-p)$:

$$g_i=\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial F}=p_i-y_i,\qquad h_i=\frac{\partial^2\ell}{\partial F^2}=p_i(1-p_i),$$

evaluated at the current model, $p_i=\sigma(F_{m-1}(x_i))$. (Careful: in this detour $h_i$ is the second derivative, not L68's weak classifier $h_t$.) So the pseudo-residual is

$$r_i=-g_i=y_i-p_i :$$

"how far the predicted probability is from the label". A positive point predicted at $p=0.9$ sends $+0.1$; one predicted at $p=0.1$ sends $+0.9$. Unlike AdaBoost's $e^{-z}$, this can never exceed 1 in size, which is why logistic-loss boosting copes better with mislabelled points.

Same loss as L70, different labels. With $\tilde y=2y-1\in\{\pm1\}$ and margin $z=\tilde yF$, the cross-entropy above equals L70's $\log(1+e^{-z})$. Check: for $y=1$, $-\log\sigma(F)=\log(1+e^{-F})$; for $y=0$, $-\log(1-\sigma(F))=\log(1+e^{F})$. And the pseudo-residual in ±1 form, $\tilde y/(1+e^{\tilde yF})$, is $1-p$ when $y=1$ and $-p$ when $y=0$, that is, $y-p$ in both cases.

2 · The starting point and the leaf values

Start: the best constant score $c$ minimises $\sum_i\ell(y_i,c)$. Setting the derivative $\sum_i(\sigma(c)-y_i)$ to zero gives $\sigma(c)=\bar y$, the fraction of positives, so

$$F_0=\log\frac{\bar y}{1-\bar y},$$

the log-odds of the base rate. A dataset with 30% positives starts every point at $p=0.3$.

Leaf values. D1's line search asks, for each leaf $j$ with points $I_j$, for the $w$ minimising $\sum_{i\in I_j}\ell(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)+w)$. There's no closed form, so take one Newton step from $w=0$: expand each loss to second order,

$$\ell\big(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)+w\big)\approx\ell\big(y_i,F_{m-1}(x_i)\big)+g_iw+\tfrac12h_iw^2,$$

sum over the leaf, and minimise the quadratic $G_jw+\frac12H_jw^2$, where $G_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}g_i$ and $H_j=\sum_{i\in I_j}h_i$:

$$\boxed{\;w_j=-\frac{G_j}{H_j}=\frac{\sum_{i\in I_j}(y_i-p_i)}{\sum_{i\in I_j}p_i(1-p_i)}\;}$$
  • The numerator is the total "pull" in the leaf; the denominator measures how confident the current predictions already are. Points with $p$ near 0 or 1 have tiny $h_i$, so a leaf full of confident points takes a big step for a small residual.
  • For squared loss $h_i=1$, so $w_j=-G_j/n_j$, the mean residual: D1's answer is the special case where the Newton step is exact.
  • For logistic loss a single Newton step can overshoot when $H_j$ is tiny (a leaf of very confident points). Shrinkage $\eta$ softens this; implementations also cap $|w_j|$ or require a minimum $H_j$ per leaf, and XGBoost adds $\lambda$ to the denominator (D3).
  • This "first derivative over second derivative per leaf" is the seed of XGBoost (D3), which also uses $G_j$ and $H_j$ to choose the splits, not just the leaf values.
GBDT for binary classification
  1. $F_0=\log\frac{\bar y}{1-\bar y}$.
  2. For $m=1,\dots,M$: compute $p_i=\sigma(F_{m-1}(x_i))$ and pseudo-residuals $r_i=y_i-p_i$.
  3. Fit a regression tree (depth $\le d$, at least $n_{\min}$ points per leaf) to $(x_i,r_i)$ with SSE-reduction splits (D1 §3).
  4. Newton leaf values: $w_j=\sum_{I_j}(y_i-p_i)\,/\sum_{I_j}p_i(1-p_i)$.
  5. $F_m(x)=F_{m-1}(x)+\eta\,w_{j(x)}$.
Predict $p(x)=\sigma(F_M(x))$; classify as 1 if $p(x)>\tfrac12$ (or another threshold).
More than two classes. With $K$ classes, keep $K$ score functions $F^{(1)},\dots,F^{(K)}$, turn them into probabilities with the softmax $p^{(k)}=e^{F^{(k)}}/\sum_le^{F^{(l)}}$, and each round fit $K$ trees, the $k$-th to the pseudo-residuals $y^{(k)}_i-p^{(k)}_i$ (with $y^{(k)}_i=1$ if point $i$ is in class $k$). That's $K$ times the work of the binary case.

4 · The knobs, and what they do to bias and variance

L66 split error into bias (too simple, systematically wrong) and variance (too flexible, changes with the dataset). Boosting starts from a high-bias model (a constant) and lowers bias with every tree, so more rounds always fit the training data better: past some point the extra trees fit noise and variance takes over. Every knob below controls how fast that happens.

KnobWhat it controlsTurning it up…
rounds $M$how many trees are addedlowers bias; eventually overfits. Choose by early stopping.
learning rate $\eta$how much of each tree is used (shrinkage, D1 §9)fewer rounds needed, but each tree overshoots more and overfitting comes sooner. Small $\eta$ + more rounds often generalises a little better, and is much less sensitive to the exact stopping round.
depth $d$ (or number of leaves $T$)how complex each tree is; a depth-$d$ tree can model interactions of up to $d$ featureseach tree is less "weak": lower bias per round, higher variance. Typical: 3–8.
min points (or min $H_j$) per leafsmallest leaf allowed(raising it) smoother, lower variance; stops leaves that fit one noisy point.
row subsample $s$each tree sees a random fraction $s$ of the rows(lowering $s$) adds randomness that decorrelates trees, like bagging (L67); also faster.
column subsampleeach tree (or split) sees a random subset of features(lowering it) decorrelates trees, like a random forest (L67).
Interaction order. A model built from depth-1 trees (stumps) is a sum of one-variable functions, $F(x)=\sum_kf_k(x_k)$: an additive model with no interactions. Depth 2 lets a tree say "if $x_1$ is big and $x_2$ is small", a pairwise interaction. So depth is not just a size knob; it decides which kinds of patterns the ensemble can express at all.

5 · Stochastic gradient boosting: bagging inside boosting

Friedman (2002) added one line: at each round, fit the tree to a random subsample of the training rows (typically 50–80%, drawn without replacement). Two effects:

  • Variance reduction. Consecutive trees see different data, so their errors are less correlated, the same reason averaging bootstrapped trees helps in bagging (L67). It also acts as regularisation: no single noisy point influences every tree.
  • Speed. Each tree costs roughly a fraction $s$ of the full fit.

The pseudo-residuals are still computed from the full current model; only the tree fit (and its leaf values) use the subsample. Column subsampling, borrowed from random forests, is the same trick across features. XGBoost and LightGBM expose both; LightGBM's GOSS (D4) is a smarter, gradient-aware version of row subsampling.

6 · Early stopping is cross-validation over $M$

L35 chose the ridge penalty $\lambda$ by holding out a validation set and picking the value with the lowest validation error. For boosting, the number of rounds $M$ is the regulariser, and it's free to tune: train once, track validation loss after every round, and keep $M^\star=\arg\min_m\text{val-loss}(m)$. In practice training stops when the validation loss hasn't improved for, say, 50 rounds ("patience"), and the model is truncated at $M^\star$. 8 · What's still slow, and where D3–D5 go

Plain GBDT has three practical weaknesses, each fixed by one of the next detours:

  1. Splits are chosen by fitting the pseudo-residuals with squared error, while leaf values use Newton steps: two different criteria. XGBoost (D3) derives both from one regularised second-order objective, with an explicit penalty on leaf values and on the number of leaves.
  2. Finding the best split means sorting every feature at every node: slow for millions of rows. LightGBM (D4) buckets features into histograms, grows trees leaf-wise, and subsamples rows by gradient size.
  3. Categorical features (city, product ID) don't fit "feature < threshold", and naive fixes leak the label. CatBoost (D5) handles them with ordered target statistics and ordered boosting.

Key takeaways

  1. GBDT for classification boosts the log-odds $F$; $p=\sigma(F)$, starting from the base-rate log-odds.
  2. Logistic loss gives $g_i=p_i-y_i$ and $h_i=p_i(1-p_i)$; the trees fit the pseudo-residuals $y_i-p_i$, which are bounded, unlike AdaBoost's.
  3. Leaf values come from one Newton step, $w_j=-G_j/H_j$; for squared loss this is the mean residual.
  4. Rounds, learning rate, depth, leaf size and subsampling trade bias against variance; depth also sets the interaction order.
  5. Stochastic gradient boosting brings bagging's decorrelation into boosting; early stopping picks $M$ on a validation set, like L35.
  6. Bagging reduces variance of deep trees in parallel; boosting reduces bias of shallow trees in sequence.
