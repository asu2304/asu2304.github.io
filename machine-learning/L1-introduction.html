Lecture 1 · Course introduction

Introduction to Machine Learning

Machine learning sits under most of today's technology trends. It is not a fixed procedure, not memorisation and not magic. It is algorithms that learn from data, aim to generalise to data they have never seen, and are built on mathematics.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈22 min). Next: L2.

1 · Why study machine learning?

  • Applications. A Forbes list of top digital-transformation trends included analytics, AI and machine learning, conversational AI and autonomous drones. All of them rely on machine learning algorithms one way or another.
  • Popularity. Google Trends for "machine learning", "deep learning" and "artificial intelligence" show a boom from about 2016. "Artificial intelligence" (the older, broader idea of a computer mimicking a human) was searched a lot in the early 2000s, faded as machine learning took over, and has picked up again with newer ideas such as causality. Deep learning is a subset of machine learning.

2 · Where can machine learning be applied?

Start from a prototypical human and the things that come naturally to us. If machines are to mimic humans, these are the abilities to copy:

Human abilityMachine learning areaThe challenge
SeeingComputer visionLook at a picture and say "this is a tree": object recognition, mimicking an eye that evolved over millions of years.
Hearing speechSpeech / natural languageDifferent people say the same thing in different ways; speech is unstructured, yet we extract meaning.
Reading textText understandingBillions of unstructured documents; understand their semantics, or even just read handwriting.
ThinkingUnderstanding the brainThe "holy grail": how do interconnected neurons produce decisions? We are nowhere close, only baby steps.

Beyond the senses, any activity that generates a lot of data is a candidate: stock markets (will a stock go up?), biology, chemistry and materials experiments, sports analytics (will Chennai or Bangalore win the next IPL match?), and e-commerce, where "people similar to you bought this" recommendations are chosen to maximise the chance you buy.

3 · What machine learning is not

Not procedural.
Tax calculation takes salary and savings, applies fixed rules and outputs the tax. Input → steps → output, with nothing learnt.
Not memorisation.
Memorise 1,000 pictures with trees and 1,000 without, and you still can't judge a new picture. Memorising every assignment solution aces an exam that only repeats them, but you haven't learnt the subject.
Not magic.
"AI", "deep learning", "ML" are thrown around as buzzwords, and the results can feel like magic. They aren't.

4 · What it is

It's not……because it hasMeaning
proceduraldataThe "secret sauce". It is still algorithmic, but the algorithm learns from data and uses what it learnt on test data.
memorisationgeneralisationDoing well on unseen data. Being specific to the data you've seen is easy (just memorise); to be general you have to actually learn.
magicmathematicsLinear algebra, probability, statistics and optimisation (L2 explains why each is needed).

Memorising vs generalising. 40 labelled training points: blue = "tree", orange = "no tree" (think of each point as a picture summarised by two numbers). 5 · Concrete machine learning problems

ProblemWhat has to be learnt
Spam filtering (email or SMS)From many emails known to be spam or not spam, learn to tell them apart. It occasionally makes mistakes but is mostly right.
Forecasting rainfallFrom 10–15 years of daily precipitation, pressure, humidity, … predict whether (or how much) it will rain tomorrow.
Recommending moviesAn OTT platform's "recommended for you" list, tailored to the genres you watch, different from your friend's.
Friend suggestions"People you may know" on social or professional networks: which non-connected people are likely to connect, as an ordered list.
Voice/instrument separationAn mp3 mixes a singer and instruments. Given voice-only and instrument-only recordings, learn their structure and separate a new song.
Grouping photos on a phoneWithout being told who is in which photo, find that photos from different angles, places and times show the same face (then ask you to name the person).
Robot navigationChoose forward/left/right/back, get feedback (hit a wall?), and learn by mistakes which move is good where. Learning by experience.
Stock market predictionPredict in the morning, see the result in the evening, adjust for tomorrow. Learning over time, judged by money made or chances missed.

L2 sorts these into the broad paradigms of machine learning.

Key takeaways

  1. Machine learning underlies vision, speech, text, recommendation, finance, sports and science: anywhere there's a lot of data.
  2. It is not procedural: the extra ingredient is data.
  3. It is not memorisation: the goal is to generalise to unseen data.
  4. It is not magic: it is mathematics.
  5. Typical problems: spam, rainfall, recommendations, friend suggestions, source separation, photo grouping, robot navigation, stock prediction.
