Lecture 11 · Computing dot products without the map

Kernel Functions and Mercer's Theorem

PCA in the mapped space only needs the dot products $\phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j)$, never $\phi(x)$ itself. Some functions compute that high-dimensional dot product directly from the low-dimensional inputs: $(x^\top x'+1)^2$ is a dot product in $\mathbb R^6$, and the Gaussian (RBF) function is one in an infinite-dimensional space. Such functions are kernels. Mercer's theorem says how to recognise one: it must be symmetric and give positive semi-definite kernel matrices.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈29 min). Previous: L10 · Next: L12.

1 · The problem, restated

$\phi(x)\in\mathbb R^D$ may be too large to compute (L10: $D\approx d^p$). But L9 showed that PCA only needs the $n\times n$ matrix of pairwise dot products. In the mapped space that is

$$K_{ij}=\phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j).$$

The obvious route is $x_i,x_j\;\to\;\phi(x_i),\phi(x_j)\;\to$ dot product. Is there a direct route, from $x_i,x_j$ straight to the number, without ever forming $\phi$?

2 · An example that works

Let $x=(f_1,f_2)$ and $x'=(g_1,g_2)$, and consider $k(x,x')=(x^\top x'+1)^2$. Expand it:

$$(f_1g_1+f_2g_2+1)^2=f_1^2g_1^2+f_2^2g_2^2+1+2f_1g_1f_2g_2+2f_1g_1+2f_2g_2 .$$

Group the $f$-parts and the $g$-parts into two vectors of the same form (that's what the $\sqrt2$'s are for):

$$\phi\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}a^2& b^2& 1& \sqrt2\,ab& \sqrt2\,a& \sqrt2\,b\end{bmatrix}^\top\in\mathbb R^6,\qquad (x^\top x'+1)^2=\phi(x)^\top\phi(x').$$
The insight: computing $(x^\top x'+1)^2$ is a cheap 2-D calculation, yet the number it produces is the dot product of $x$ and $x'$ after a 6-D transformation. We got $\phi(x)^\top\phi(x')$ without computing $\phi(x)$.

3 · More kernels

Mapping 2-D to 6-D isn't impressive on its own. What matters is that the trick extends to general $d$ and high powers.

KernelFormulaHidden feature space
Polynomial, power $p\ge1$$k(x,x')=(x^\top x'+1)^p$all monomials up to degree $p$: dimension grows like $d^p$, but the kernel costs only one $d$-dimensional dot product
Gaussian / radial basis function (RBF), $\sigma>0$$k(x,x')=\exp\!\left(-\dfrac{\lVert x-x'\rVert^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$infinite-dimensional: think of each point as mapped to a function, with dot products becoming integrals. The power series of $e^{(\cdot)}$ contains every polynomial power, with decreasing weights.

For $(x^\top x'+1)^p$ the lecture leaves an exercise: write out $\phi$ explicitly for $p=3$ and $p=4$ (with general $d$) and watch how its dimension grows. Exhibiting a $\phi$ is a certificate that the function is a valid kernel.

4 · What is a kernel?

Definition. A function $k:\mathbb R^d\times\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R$ is a (valid) kernel if there exists a map $\phi:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb R^D$ (possibly $D=\infty$) with $k(x,x')=\phi(x)^\top\phi(x')$ for all $x,x'$.

Not every function qualifies. If $k$ isn't a dot product in some space, its "kernel matrix" isn't a legitimate $\phi(X)^\top\phi(X)$, and PCA on it makes no sense. Two ways to check validity:

  1. Exhibit $\phi$ explicitly, as for the quadratic kernel. Often hard.
  2. Mercer's theorem.

5 · Mercer's theorem (informal)

$k$ is a valid kernel if and only if
  1. $k$ is symmetric: $k(x,x')=k(x',x)$ for all $x,x'$; and
  2. for any dataset $x_1,\dots,x_n$, the $n\times n$ matrix $K_{ij}=k(x_i,x_j)$ is positive semi-definite: all its eigenvalues are $\ge0$.

The theorem says these conditions are sufficient as well as necessary; sufficiency is the hard part and isn't proved in the course. Necessity is easy:

  • Symmetry: $k(x,x')=\phi(x)^\top\phi(x')=\phi(x')^\top\phi(x)=k(x',x)$. If you can find one pair where $k(x,x')\ne k(x',x)$, $k$ is not a kernel.
  • Non-negative eigenvalues: $K=\phi(X)^\top\phi(X)$. By L9's fact, its non-zero eigenvalues equal those of $\phi(X)\phi(X)^\top$, which is a (scaled) covariance matrix in the high-dimensional space. Its eigenvalues are variances along its eigenvectors, so they can't be negative.

Going beyond the video. The last three functions are my counter-examples. $\lVert x-x'\rVert$ is symmetric but its matrix has a zero diagonal and positive off-diagonal entries, so its trace is 0 and some eigenvalue must be negative. $x^\top x'-1$ adds the constant $-1$ to a valid kernel, and a negative constant is not a dot product. $x_1x'_2$ fails symmetry. For a non-symmetric matrix the demo checks the eigenvalues of its symmetric part $(K+K^\top)/2$.

Key takeaways

  1. PCA in the mapped space needs only $\phi(x_i)^\top\phi(x_j)$.
  2. A kernel computes that dot product directly: $(x^\top x'+1)^2=\phi(x)^\top\phi(x')$ with $\phi(x)\in\mathbb R^6$ for 2-D inputs.
  3. Polynomial kernel $(x^\top x'+1)^p$; Gaussian/RBF kernel $\exp(-\lVert x-x'\rVert^2/2\sigma^2)$, whose feature space is infinite-dimensional.
  4. Valid kernel ⇔ some $\phi$ exists with $k(x,x')=\phi(x)^\top\phi(x')$.
  5. Mercer: valid ⇔ symmetric and every kernel matrix is positive semi-definite. Necessity follows from dot-product symmetry and covariance eigenvalues being variances.
