Paradigms of Machine Learning

Three broad paradigms: supervised learning (data with labels), unsupervised learning (data alone) and sequential learning (decide, get feedback, update). Each splits into sub-problems. Building any learning algorithm needs three ingredients, structure, uncertainty and decisions, which is why the prerequisites are linear algebra, probability and optimisation.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈26 min). Previous: L1 · Next: L3.

1 · Supervised learning

Besides the data you have supervision. A pile of emails is just data, but a teacher or labeler marks some as spam and others as non-spam. Now each data point (its features) comes with a label, and the task is to learn to tell one label from the other.

2 · Unsupervised learning

Only data points, no labels: "make sense of this". With a collection of animal pictures you could group animals vs. background, dogs vs. cats, four animals vs. six, white vs. black or brown animals… The problem isn't well posed until you fix a notion of similarity. Fix "cats vs. dogs" and you get three groups: only cats, only dogs, both. Automatically finding groups like this is one example of unsupervised learning.

3 · Sequential learning

You don't learn in one shot. You make a decision, observe feedback (a form of supervision), compare it with your prediction, update your model, and do better over time. Not all data and supervision are handed over at the start. This course won't cover it, but it's worth knowing it exists.

6 · Why the prerequisites? Three ingredients

Take the height and weight of 100 people and plot them. To predict height from weight, notice that on average height rises with weight, roughly linearly. So look for the best line. But there are infinitely many lines, even if you only allow lines through the origin, and none passes through all the points. Building the predictor needs three things:

IngredientIn the exampleMathematics
StructureHeight and weight are linearly related, so we look for a line.Linear algebra (lines and their "calculus"; the base for non-linear structure later)
UncertaintyNo point lies on the line: noise from data collection, faulty equipment, missing data…Probability
DecisionsDefine what "best" means, then pick the line that does best: data → decision.Optimisation (maximise or minimise something)

The three ingredients in one picture. Points are (weight, height) for 100 people, measured from the average person, so a line through the origin makes sense. Structure: the line (slope slider). Uncertainty: the noise slider. Decision: one criterion for "best" (average squared vertical gap), plotted on the right for every slope.

7 · Course goals, roadmap and references

  • Goals: a clear view of the paradigms; the ability to pose a new real-world problem as a machine learning problem; understand the key algorithms, how they differ, when each works and why; appreciate the mathematics, not treat them as black boxes; implement them from scratch and show results with plots.
  • Roadmap: unsupervised learning first (representation learning, clustering, estimation), then supervised learning (basic algorithms, advanced algorithms, advanced topics).
  • References (no single textbook; lecture notes suffice): Gilbert Strang, Linear Algebra and its Applications; Sheldon Ross, A First Course in Probability; Christopher Bishop, Pattern Recognition and Machine Learning (the go-to reference, a superset of the course, though notation and order differ); Mathematics for Machine Learning (free soft copy).

Key takeaways

  1. Supervised = data + labels; unsupervised = data only; sequential = decide, get feedback, update.
  2. Supervised: classification (finite labels), regression (continuous), ranking, structured. Unsupervised: clustering, representation learning.
  3. Sequential (not in this course): online learning (full feedback), bandits (partial feedback), reinforcement learning (policy: state → action).
  4. Three ingredients of a learning algorithm: structure (linear algebra), uncertainty (probability), decisions (optimisation).
