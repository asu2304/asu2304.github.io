Lecture 35 · Regression: a better estimator, and how to tune it

A Better Estimator and Cross-Validation

The ML error is $\sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i$ over the eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ of $XX^\top$, so small eigenvalues make it blow up. Adding $\lambda I$ before inverting lifts every eigenvalue by $\lambda$. For some $\lambda$, the new estimator $(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$ has lower mean squared error than ML. Which $\lambda$? Pick it by cross-validation.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈20 min). Previous: L34.

Notation: the lecture uses λ for two things. Here $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d$ (with a subscript) are eigenvalues of $XX^\top$, and plain $\lambda$ is the number we add to the diagonal.

1 · The trace is a sum of eigenvalues

The trace is the sum of the diagonal entries, $\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^d a_{ii}$, and it also equals the sum of the eigenvalues, $\sum_{i=1}^d\lambda_i(A)$ (a standard fact, from the characteristic polynomial). If $XX^\top$ has eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_d$, then $(XX^\top)^{-1}$ has eigenvalues $1/\lambda_1,\dots,1/\lambda_d$ (same eigenvectors, reciprocal eigenvalues). So L34's result becomes

$$\text{MSE}(\hat w_{\text{ML}})=\mathbb E\lVert\hat w_{\text{ML}}-w\rVert^2=\sigma^2\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{1}{\lambda_i}.$$

If some $\lambda_i$ is small, $1/\lambda_i$ is huge and so is the error, even with modest noise.

2 · A new estimator, pulled out of a hat

Consider, for some positive real number $\lambda$ and the $d\times d$ identity $I$,

$$\boxed{\;\hat w_{\text{new}}=\big(XX^\top+\lambda I\big)^{-1}Xy\;}\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad \hat w_{\text{ML}}=\big(XX^\top\big)^{-1}Xy .$$

Why would adding $\lambda I$ help? Look at what it does to eigenvalues.

  1. Let $v_1$ be an eigenvector of $A$: $\;Av_1=\lambda_1v_1$.
  2. Then $\;(A+\lambda I)v_1=Av_1+\lambda v_1=(\lambda_1+\lambda)\,v_1$.
  3. So $v_1$ is still an eigenvector, and its eigenvalue moved up by $\lambda$. The eigenvalues of $A+\lambda I$ are $\lambda_1+\lambda,\dots,\lambda_d+\lambda$.
  4. Hence $\;\operatorname{tr}\big((XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}\big)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\dfrac{1}{\lambda_i+\lambda}$: every denominator grows, so the sum shrinks.

The intuition: the error was large because the $\lambda_i$ were small. We can't touch $\sigma^2$, but artificially raising the eigenvalues makes the feature term smaller, and we hope the mean squared error goes down with it.

3 · The existence theorem

Theorem (stated informally, not proved in the course). There exists a $\lambda$ such that $\hat w_{\text{new}}=(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$ has lower mean squared error than $\hat w_{\text{ML}}$. On average, it's a better estimator.

The catch: it only says such a $\lambda$ exists. The right value depends on the data (and on the unknown $w$), so there's no formula for it.

Why only "exists", going beyond the video. The new estimator is biased: $\mathbb E[\hat w_{\text{new}}]=(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}XX^\top w\neq w$, so it pays a bias price for its lower variance. Writing $u_i$ for the eigenvectors of $XX^\top$, $$\mathbb E\lVert\hat w_{\text{new}}-w\rVert^2=\underbrace{\sigma^2\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda_i}{(\lambda_i+\lambda)^2}}_{\text{variance}}+\underbrace{\lambda^2\sum_i\frac{(u_i^\top w)^2}{(\lambda_i+\lambda)^2}}_{\text{bias}^2}.$$ At $\lambda=0$ this is L34's $\sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i$. The derivative at $\lambda=0$ is negative, so a small enough $\lambda>0$ always helps; too large a $\lambda$ shrinks $\hat w$ towards 0 and the bias takes over. The demo plots both parts.

4 · Finding λ in practice: validation

We want the $\lambda$ that does well on test data we don't have yet. So mimic it: hold back part of the training data.

  1. Split the $n$ training points into a train set (say 80%) and a validation set (20%).
  2. For each candidate $\lambda$: compute $\hat w_{\text{new}}=(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$ using only the train set.
  3. Predict the labels of the validation points and measure the error against their true labels.
  4. Pick the $\lambda$ with the least validation error.

The validation set is a proxy for the test data. The weak spot: with one random split you may be unlucky, and that 20% may not represent the data well, so the error you measure for each $\lambda$ isn't the true error.

5 · K-fold cross-validation

Do the validation several times and average.

  • Divide the data into $K$ equal parts, folds $F_1,\dots,F_K$ (10,000 points and 10 folds: 1,000 points each).
  • For each $i$: train on all folds except $F_i$, validate on $F_i$. With $K=3$: train on $F_1,F_2$ and validate on $F_3$; train on $F_1,F_3$ and validate on $F_2$; train on $F_2,F_3$ and validate on $F_1$.
  • For every $\lambda$, average the $K$ validation errors, and pick the $\lambda$ with the least average error.
Choice of KTrade-off
Larger $K$Generally better, because it averages over more train/validation splits.
$K=n$: leave-one-out (LOOCV)Train on $n-1$ points, test on the one left out, for every point. The extreme case.
Huge $n$ (a billion points)LOOCV means a billion fits, which is a computational nightmare. Choose $K$ by the time and resources you have.

6 · Still open: where does this estimator come from?

We argued for $\hat w_{\text{new}}$ by intuition and an existence theorem, and tuned $\lambda$ by cross-validation. Is there a principled way to arrive at it? Yes, and we've met the tool before: the alternative to maximum likelihood from unsupervised estimation (L21). Applied to supervised learning, it gives this estimator, which is the subject of L36.

Key takeaways

  1. $\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_i\lambda_i(A)$, and $A^{-1}$ has eigenvalues $1/\lambda_i$, so $\text{MSE}(\hat w_{\text{ML}})=\sigma^2\sum_i 1/\lambda_i$. Small eigenvalues hurt.
  2. $A+\lambda I$ has the same eigenvectors and eigenvalues $\lambda_i+\lambda$.
  3. $\hat w_{\text{new}}=(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$: for some $\lambda$ its MSE beats $\hat w_{\text{ML}}$ (existence only).
  4. Choose $\lambda$ by validation: train on part of the data, evaluate on the held-out part, keep the best $\lambda$.
  5. $K$-fold CV averages over $K$ splits; LOOCV is $K=n$. Bigger $K$ is better but costs more.
