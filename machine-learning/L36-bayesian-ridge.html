Lecture 36 · Regression: the Bayesian view

Where the Better Estimator Comes From: a Gaussian Prior

L35 pulled $(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$ out of a hat. Here it gets a principled origin. Put a Gaussian prior on $w$, centred at 0, and the MAP estimate (the peak of the posterior) is exactly that estimator, with $\lambda=1/\gamma^2$, where $\gamma^2$ is the prior's variance.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈15 min). Previous: L35 · Next: L37.

1 · The recipe: prior × likelihood → posterior

Bayesian modelling, as in L21: put a prior on the unknown parameter, multiply by the likelihood of the data, and get the posterior. Here the parameter is $w\in\mathbb R^d$, a whole vector, so the prior must be a density over all of $\mathbb R^d$.

  • Likelihood (from L33): $y\mid x\sim\mathcal N(w^\top x,\,1)$. The noise variance is set to 1 just to keep the algebra tidy; a general $\sigma^2$ works the same way.
  • Which prior? One that is conjugate to the likelihood, so the posterior has the same form as the prior. In L21, Beta was conjugate to the coin (Bernoulli) likelihood. A Gaussian likelihood is $e^{(\text{something})}$, so a prior that is also $e^{(\text{something})}$ multiplies neatly: the exponents just add. The natural choice is a Gaussian prior.

2 · The Gaussian prior on w

$$w\sim\mathcal N\big(0,\;\gamma^2 I\big),\qquad \gamma^2 I=\begin{bmatrix}\gamma^2&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\gamma^2\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}.$$

The mean is the zero vector, and the covariance is $\gamma^2$ on the diagonal and 0 elsewhere. So each component $w_j$ is an independent $\mathcal N(0,\gamma^2)$: to draw a $w$ from the prior, draw each component separately from a zero-mean Gaussian with variance $\gamma^2$.

3 · The posterior, and its peak

Dropping constants (they don't move the peak):

$$P(w\mid\text{data})\;\propto\;\underbrace{\prod_{i=1}^{n}e^{-\frac{(y_i-w^\top x_i)^2}{2}}}_{\text{likelihood}}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{e^{-\frac{\lVert w\rVert^2}{2\gamma^2}}}_{\text{prior}} .$$

The prior factor is $\prod_j e^{-w_j^2/2\gamma^2}=e^{-\sum_j w_j^2/2\gamma^2}=e^{-\lVert w\rVert^2/2\gamma^2}$. The MAP estimate maximises the posterior. Take the log (products become sums) and flip max to min:

$$\hat w_{\text{MAP}}=\arg\min_{w}\;\frac12\sum_{i=1}^{n}(y_i-w^\top x_i)^2+\frac{1}{2\gamma^2}\lVert w\rVert^2 .$$

Compared with maximum likelihood, there's one extra term: a penalty on the length of $w$, coming from the prior.

  1. Gradient (worth checking yourself): $\;\nabla f(w)=XX^\top w-Xy+\dfrac{1}{\gamma^2}w$.
  2. Set it to zero: $\;\big(XX^\top+\tfrac{1}{\gamma^2}I\big)w=Xy$.
  3. $\boxed{\;\hat w_{\text{MAP}}=\Big(XX^\top+\frac{1}{\gamma^2}I\Big)^{-1}Xy\;}$
The punchline. This is exactly L35's estimator $(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$ with $\lambda=1/\gamma^2$. What was guessed by intuition is the MAP estimate under a zero-mean Gaussian prior. In practice you cross-validate $\gamma^2$, just as you cross-validated $\lambda$: it's the same single number.

(Going beyond the video: with noise variance $\sigma^2$ instead of 1, the same steps give $\lambda=\sigma^2/\gamma^2$.)

Key takeaways

  1. Bayesian regression: prior on $w$ times the Gaussian likelihood gives the posterior.
  2. A Gaussian prior is conjugate to a Gaussian likelihood. Use $w\sim\mathcal N(0,\gamma^2I)$: independent zero-mean components.
  3. The MAP estimate minimises squared loss plus $\tfrac{1}{2\gamma^2}\lVert w\rVert^2$, giving $\hat w_{\text{MAP}}=(XX^\top+\tfrac1{\gamma^2}I)^{-1}Xy$.
  4. That's L35's estimator with $\lambda=1/\gamma^2$. Choose $\gamma^2$ (equivalently $\lambda$) by cross-validation.
