Lecture 37 · Regression: regularisation

Ridge Regression: Loss Plus a Regulariser

The Bayesian estimator from L36 is ridge regression: the usual squared loss plus a penalty $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$. The penalty, called a regulariser, pulls $w$ towards 0. That's useful when many features are redundant, because then many different weight vectors fit the data equally well, and ridge picks a short one.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈10 min). Previous: L36 · Next: L38.

1 · Two estimators side by side

ProblemSolution
Linear regression (ML)$\hat w_{\text{ML}}=\arg\min_w\sum_i(w^\top x_i-y_i)^2$$(XX^\top)^{-1}Xy$
Ridge regression (MAP)$\hat w_{\text{R}}=\arg\min_w\sum_i(w^\top x_i-y_i)^2+\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$$(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$
  • $\sum_i(w^\top x_i-y_i)^2$ is the loss. $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ is the regulariser: a penalty that makes some kinds of $w$ preferred over others.
  • $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter, chosen by cross-validation (L35). From L36, it plays the role of $1/\gamma^2$, one over the prior's variance.
  • The name "ridge" comes from classical statistics.

2 · What the regulariser prefers: short w

The prior was centred at $\mathbf 0$, so its peak is at $w=0$. Correspondingly, $\lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$ penalises length and pulls $w$ towards 0, while the loss pulls $w$ towards fitting the data. The answer balances the two. If several $w$ have the same loss, ridge prefers the shortest one, the one with small components. (It never pushes all the way to $w=0$, which would be useless.)

How strong is the pull? $\lambda\approx1/\gamma^2$. A strong belief that most weights should be near zero means a small prior variance $\gamma^2$, so a large $\lambda$, so long weight vectors are heavily penalised. A weak belief means a large $\gamma^2$ and a small $\lambda$, and ridge behaves almost like ML.

3 · Why prefer short w? Redundant features

With thousands or millions of features, many are redundant: some feature is a combination of others. Then many weight vectors explain the label equally well. The lecture's example uses three features, $f_1=$ height, $f_2=$ weight, and a third sensor that reports $f_3=2\cdot\text{height}+3\cdot\text{weight}$, with the label $y\approx3\cdot\text{height}+4\cdot\text{weight}$:

All four fit perfectly. The lecture's motivation is to avoid giving weight to redundant features. Notice what "shortest" actually picks, though: $(\tfrac37,\tfrac17,\tfrac97)$ spreads weight over all three features rather than dropping one. Ridge makes weights small, but not exactly zero. 4 · What's next

Could we push redundant weights to exactly zero instead of just making them small? Is ridge the only way to regularise? Understanding ridge geometrically answers both, and leads to a different regulariser (L38–L39).

Key takeaways

  1. Ridge regression: minimise loss $+\ \lambda\lVert w\rVert^2$, giving $\hat w_{\text{R}}=(XX^\top+\lambda I)^{-1}Xy$. It is the MAP estimate with a zero-mean Gaussian prior.
  2. The regulariser pulls $w$ towards 0, and $\lambda\approx1/\gamma^2$ sets how hard.
  3. With redundant features many $w$ fit equally well; ridge picks a short one.
  4. Short isn't sparse: ridge makes weights small but generally not exactly zero.
