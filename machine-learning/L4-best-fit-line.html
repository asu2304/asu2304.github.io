Finding the Best Line: Reconstruction Error and the Covariance Matrix

Real data never lies exactly on a line, and nobody tells us which line to project onto. Every line gives the same compression, but not the same reconstruction error. Minimising that error over unit vectors $w$ turns into maximising $w^\top Cw$, where $C=\frac1n\sum_i x_ix_i^\top$ is the covariance matrix. The answer is the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈17 min). Previous: L3 · Next: L5.

1 · Nobody gives us the line

L3 assumed we knew which points were on the line and which weren't. In practice we just get a dataset. Measure the height and weight of 100 people: weight tends to rise with height, but maybe no point lies exactly on any line. An exact linear relationship between features is a myth (physical laws, perhaps; real-world data, practically never). Off-line points are the rule, not the exception, so every point needs a proxy, and we must find the line too.

2 · Same compression, different error

Given any line, we can project every point onto it, store one representative and one coefficient per point, and get the same ~50% compression. So two candidate lines, say a "red" one along the cloud and a "blue" one across it, compress equally well. The red one is still better: the proxies on it are closer to the original points, so reconstructing the data from them loses less.

Goal: given a dataset, find the line with the least reconstruction error.

3 · The objective

Data: $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}$, $x_i\in\mathbb R^d$. A line through the origin is represented by a unit vector $w$ ($\lVert w\rVert^2=w^\top w=1$). From L3, the proxy for $x_i$ is $(x_i^\top w)\,w$, so its error is the squared length of $x_i-(x_i^\top w)w$. Add up over the data, or average (the same $w$ minimises both, and the average turns out easier to interpret):

$$f(w)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\lVert x_i-(x_i^\top w)\,w\big\rVert^2,\qquad \min_{\lVert w\rVert=1} f(w).$$

4 · Simplifying

  1. Squared length = vector transposed times itself: $\lVert x_i-(x_i^\top w)w\rVert^2=\big(x_i-(x_i^\top w)w\big)^\top\big(x_i-(x_i^\top w)w\big)$.
  2. Expand: $x_i^\top x_i-(x_i^\top w)^2-(x_i^\top w)^2+(x_i^\top w)^2\,w^\top w$.
  3. Use $w^\top w=1$: the last term cancels one middle term, leaving $\lVert x_i\rVert^2-(x_i^\top w)^2$.
  4. $\frac1n\sum_i\lVert x_i\rVert^2$ doesn't involve $w$, so drop it; the same $w$ minimises $g(w)=-\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w)^2$.
  5. Minimising a negative is maximising the positive: $\;\max_{\lVert w\rVert=1}\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w)^2.$
  6. Write $(x_i^\top w)^2=w^\top x_i\,x_i^\top w$ (just regrouping the matrix product), and pull $w$ out of the sum since it doesn't depend on $i$: $\;w^\top\Big(\frac1n\sum_i x_ix_i^\top\Big)w .$

The bracket is an average of $n$ matrices $x_ix_i^\top$, each $d\times d$ ($x_i$ is a $d\times1$ column, $x_i^\top$ a $1\times d$ row). Call it $C$:

$$\boxed{\;\max_{\lVert w\rVert=1}\;w^\top C\,w,\qquad C=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_ix_i^\top=\frac1n XX^\top\;}$$

with $X\in\mathbb R^{d\times n}$ the matrix whose columns are the data points. $C$ is the covariance matrix of the dataset.

5 · The solution: an eigenvector

From linear algebra, $w^\top Cw$ over unit vectors $w$ is maximised by the eigenvector of $C$ corresponding to its largest eigenvalue. So the line that best represents the data (least reconstruction error) is the line through that eigenvector. The takeaway for now: this is not a hard problem. It has a well-understood solution in terms of the eigenvectors of a matrix built from the data. Why "covariance" shows up in an error-minimisation problem is explained in later lectures.

Two remarks beyond the video. (1) Step 4 says $f(w)+w^\top Cw=\frac1n\sum_i\lVert x_i\rVert^2$, a constant. That's why the two curves in the demo are mirror images: lowering the error raises $w^\top Cw$ by exactly the same amount. (2) Why the top eigenvector? Write $w$ in $C$'s orthonormal eigenvectors, $w=\sum_j a_ju_j$ with $\sum_ja_j^2=1$. Then $w^\top Cw=\sum_j\lambda_ja_j^2\le\lambda_{\max}$, with equality at $w=u_{\max}$. Also, $\frac1n\sum x_ix_i^\top$ is the covariance only when the data has mean zero (as here); the course deals with centring when it returns to PCA.

Key takeaways

  1. Real data never sits exactly on a line, so every point gets a proxy, and the line itself must be found.
  2. All lines compress equally; the best one has the least reconstruction error $\frac1n\sum_i\lVert x_i-(x_i^\top w)w\rVert^2$ over unit $w$.
  3. Expanding with $w^\top w=1$ gives $\lVert x_i\rVert^2-(x_i^\top w)^2$; dropping the constant turns minimising error into maximising $\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w)^2=w^\top Cw$.
  4. $C=\frac1n\sum_ix_ix_i^\top$ is the $d\times d$ covariance matrix of the data.
  5. The best $w$ is the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue.
