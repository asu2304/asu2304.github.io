Lecture 44 · Classification: growing the tree

Building a Decision Tree, and What It Learns

Greedy recipe: try every candidate question, keep the one with the highest information gain, split, and repeat on each side. Each question cuts the plane with an axis-parallel line, so the tree carves it into rectangles. Grow it too deep and it boxes in every outlier; pick the depth by cross-validation. The payoff: every prediction comes with a readable explanation.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈24 min). Previous: L43.

1 · The algorithm

  1. Make the candidate questions. For each feature, discretise its [min, max] range, say into 10 parts. Height from 100 to 200 gives "height ≤ 110?", "height ≤ 120?", and so on.
  2. Pick the question with the largest information gain (L43). It splits the data into $D_{\text{yes}}$ and $D_{\text{no}}$.
  3. Repeat on $D_{\text{yes}}$ and on $D_{\text{no}}$, each with its own best question. Questions that can't split a side are pointless there: once you know height ≤ 150, asking "height ≤ 170?" gets "yes" from everyone.
  4. Stop when a node is pure enough (say over 90% one label) or the tree hits a maximum depth. A leaf predicts its majority label.

3 · Depth is the knob: cross-validate it

Every question draws an axis-parallel line, so leaves are rectangles. If no two identical points have different labels, a deep enough tree can surround every point with its own box and get 100% training accuracy, noise included. That's like $k=1$ in k-NN. So treat the maximum depth as a hyperparameter: grow trees of different depths and keep the one that does best on held-out data.

4 · Other impurity measures

Entropy isn't the only option. The Gini index is another popular one: $\text{Gini}(p)=2p(1-p)$ for two classes. Any function that is 0 at $p=0$ and $p=1$, peaks at $p=\tfrac12$ and is symmetric is a reasonable impurity measure. Toggle it above: the trees usually come out very similar.

5 · Why trees are popular: interpretability

A doctor using a model to flag a disease wants to know why. A tree answers in plain terms: "predicted positive because temperature > … and blood pressure ≤ …". Shallow trees, around ten questions or fewer, are explanations a person can check. And the tree is a compact model: once built, the training data can be discarded.

What's next: k-NN and trees make no assumptions about how the data was generated. The next lectures model the data probabilistically, with assumptions about structure and noise, which leads to a new family of classifiers.

Key takeaways

  1. Greedy building: discretise each feature into candidate thresholds, take the max-gain question, split, recurse.
  2. Stop at enough purity or a maximum depth. Leaves predict the majority label.
  3. Decision boundaries are axis-parallel rectangles. Deep trees fit the noise, so choose the depth by cross-validation.
  4. Entropy or Gini (or any symmetric impurity that peaks at ½) can score questions.
  5. Trees are interpretable models: each prediction comes with its chain of reasons.
