Lecture 48 · Classification: learn the coins, then predict

The Naive Bayes Algorithm

The coin model has $2d+1$ unknowns. Maximum likelihood estimates them by counting: the fraction of emails that are spam, and for each word, the fraction of spam (and of non-spam) emails containing it. To classify a new email, Bayes' rule turns $P(x\mid y)$ into $P(y\mid x)$, and we pick the more probable label. "Naive" for the independence assumption, "Bayes" for the rule.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈29 min). Previous: L47 · Next: L49.

1 · Estimating the parameters: just count

Parameters: $p$, and $p^1_1,\dots,p^1_d$, $p^0_1,\dots,p^0_d$. Every one is the bias of a coin (a Bernoulli), and we already know the maximum-likelihood estimate of a coin's bias: the fraction of heads.

  • The label coin was tossed once per email, and only the labels record it: $$\hat p=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i=\text{fraction of emails that are spam.}$$
  • Word $j$'s coin in room $y$ was tossed once for each email with label $y$, so only those emails matter, and only their feature $j$: $$\hat p^{\,y}_j=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb 1\big(f^i_j=1,\;y_i=y\big)}{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb 1\big(y_i=y\big)}=\text{fraction of $y$-labelled emails that contain word $j$.}$$

All $2d+1$ estimates are closed-form counting. No optimisation needed.

2 · Predicting: compare two posteriors

Given a new email $x_{\text{test}}$, predict the label that is more probable given the email:

$$\hat y_{\text{test}}=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if } P(y=1\mid x_{\text{test}})\ \ge\ P(y=0\mid x_{\text{test}})\\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

But the model gives $P(y)$ and $P(x\mid y)$, not $P(y\mid x)$. Bayes' rule flips it:

$$P(y=1\mid x_{\text{test}})=\frac{P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y=1)\,P(y=1)}{P(x_{\text{test}})},\qquad P(y=0\mid x_{\text{test}})=\frac{P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y=0)\,P(y=0)}{P(x_{\text{test}})} .$$

Both have the same denominator, a positive number, so it can't change which is bigger. Compare only the numerators, plugging in the estimates. With $x_{\text{test}}=[f_1,\dots,f_d]$, predict spam if

$$\Big[\prod_{j=1}^d(\hat p^{1}_j)^{f_j}(1-\hat p^{1}_j)^{1-f_j}\Big]\,\hat p\ \ \ge\ \ \Big[\prod_{j=1}^d(\hat p^{0}_j)^{f_j}(1-\hat p^{0}_j)^{1-f_j}\Big]\,(1-\hat p).$$

3 · Why the name

  • Naive: the class-conditional independence assumption. Given "spam", knowing "lottery" is present shouldn't change our belief about "money". That is often false in practice.
  • Bayes: the model is generative, giving $P(y)$ and $P(x\mid y)$, so Bayes' rule is needed to reach $P(y\mid x)$ for prediction.

Despite the naive assumption, the algorithm works well in practice, especially when the number of features is large (dictionary-sized). The experiment below shows one reason.

Key takeaways

  1. $\hat p$ = fraction of spam emails. $\hat p^{\,y}_j$ = fraction of $y$-labelled emails containing word $j$. All counting.
  2. Predict the label with the larger $P(y\mid x_{\text{test}})$; by Bayes' rule, compare $P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y)\,P(y)$ for $y=1$ and $y=0$ (the denominator cancels).
  3. $P(x_{\text{test}}\mid y)$ is the product of per-word factors: $\hat p^{\,y}_j$ if the word is present, $1-\hat p^{\,y}_j$ if absent.
  4. Naive = class-conditional independence; Bayes = Bayes' rule. Simple, and effective in practice.
