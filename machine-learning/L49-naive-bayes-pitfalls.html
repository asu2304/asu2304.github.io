Lecture 49 · Classification: making naive Bayes robust

Naive Bayes Pitfalls: Zero Counts and Laplace Smoothing

If a word never appears in training, its estimated probability is 0 in both classes. One such word in a test email makes both products 0, erasing everything the other words said. The standard fix is Laplace smoothing: add two pretend emails containing every word, one per label. A second, numerical pitfall: multiply many small probabilities and the result underflows, so compare sums of logs instead.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈12 min). Previous: L48 · Next: L50.

1 · The zero-frequency problem

Say word $j$ ("zebra") is in the dictionary but in no training email. Counting gives $\hat p^{1}_j=0$ and $\hat p^{0}_j=0$. Now a test email contains it, so $f_j=1$ and its factor on each side is

$$(\hat p^{\,y}_j)^{1}\,(1-\hat p^{\,y}_j)^{0}=0\times 1=0 .$$

A product with one zero factor is zero, so both sides are 0 and can't be compared. The email might also say "lottery, money, win", strong signs of spam, but that one unseen word destroys all of it, because the evidence is multiplied.

2 · Fixes

  • Ignore the feature: skip any word never seen in training. It works, but you must keep track of which words those are.
  • Laplace smoothing (the usual simple fix): add two pseudo-emails to the training set, each containing every word: $x=(1,1,\dots,1)$ once with label 1 and once with label 0. Then do maximum likelihood as before.

Now every word has appeared at least once in a spam email and once in a non-spam email, so no estimate can be 0. With $c$ = number of $y$-labelled emails containing word $j$, and $n_y$ = number of $y$-labelled emails:

$$\hat p^{\,y}_j=\frac{c}{n_y}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\frac{c+1}{n_y+1},\qquad\qquad \hat p=\frac{n_1}{n}\quad\longrightarrow\quad\frac{n_1+1}{n+2}.$$

A Bayesian reading. The two pseudo-emails act like a prior: as if someone told you beforehand, "I've seen one spam and one non-spam email with every word in them", so you never dismiss a word as impossible. Adding pseudo-data and then doing maximum likelihood is equivalent to a Bayesian estimate with that prior (compare the Beta prior's pseudo-counts in L21).

Beyond the lecture. The lecture's version protects against a word that is never seen. The mirror-image problem remains: a word present in every email of a class gets $\hat p=(n_y+1)/(n_y+1)=1$, so an email lacking it gets a factor $1-\hat p=0$. Many textbooks therefore also add two pseudo-emails containing no words, giving $(c+1)/(n_y+2)$, which is never 0 or 1. That's the third button above. Try it: in the demo, "report" is in every non-spam email after you flip one cell, or use the slider with $c=n_y$.

3 · A numerical pitfall: use logs

Each side is a product of $d$ probabilities, all below 1. With a dictionary-sized $d$ the product becomes too small for floating-point numbers and turns into 0, for both classes. Since $\log$ is increasing, comparing the products is the same as comparing sums of logs, which are perfectly ordinary numbers:

$$\sum_{j=1}^d\Big[f_j\log\hat p^{1}_j+(1-f_j)\log(1-\hat p^{1}_j)\Big]+\log\hat p\ \ \ge\ \ \sum_{j=1}^d\Big[f_j\log\hat p^{0}_j+(1-f_j)\log(1-\hat p^{0}_j)\Big]+\log(1-\hat p).$$

Careful: logs do not cure the zero-frequency problem. $\log 0$ is undefined, so a zero estimate still breaks everything. Smooth first, then take logs.

Key takeaways

  1. A word unseen in training has $\hat p^1_j=\hat p^0_j=0$; if it shows up at test time both products are 0 and all other evidence is lost.
  2. Laplace smoothing: add two pseudo-emails with all words present, one labelled 1 and one labelled 0. No estimate can be 0 any more.
  3. Smoothing is the same as a Bayesian estimate with a prior worth those two emails; its effect fades as real data grows.
  4. Compare sums of logs instead of products to avoid underflow. Logs don't fix zeros.
