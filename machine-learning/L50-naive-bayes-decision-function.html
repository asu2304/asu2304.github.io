Lecture 50 · Classification: what naive Bayes really computes

The Decision Function of Naive Bayes

For k-NN and decision trees we looked at the shape of the decision boundary. Do the same for naive Bayes: take the log of the ratio of the two posteriors, and the rule "predict spam if spam is more probable" turns into $w^\top x+b\ge 0$. Behind the coins and Bayes' rule, naive Bayes is a linear classifier.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈17 min). Previous: L49 · Next: L51.

1 · From "which is bigger" to "is the log-ratio positive"

Naive Bayes predicts $\hat y_{\text{test}}=1$ when $P(y=1\mid x_{\text{test}})\ge P(y=0\mid x_{\text{test}})$. Three equivalent ways to say it:

$$P(y{=}1\mid x)\ \ge\ P(y{=}0\mid x)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{P(y{=}1\mid x)}{P(y{=}0\mid x)}\ \ge\ 1\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log\frac{P(y{=}1\mid x)}{P(y{=}0\mid x)}\ \ge\ 0 .$$

The log is there because it turns the model's products into sums, and sums are easier to read.

2 · The algebra, in four moves

  1. Bayes' rule on top and bottom; $P(x)$ cancels: $$\log\frac{P(x\mid y{=}1)\,P(y{=}1)}{P(x\mid y{=}0)\,P(y{=}0)}\ \ge\ 0 .$$
  2. Plug in the model with $x=[f_1,\dots,f_d]$, and pair up the factors that share an exponent: $$\log\left[\prod_{i=1}^d\Big(\frac{\hat p^1_i}{\hat p^0_i}\Big)^{f_i}\Big(\frac{1-\hat p^1_i}{1-\hat p^0_i}\Big)^{1-f_i}\cdot\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\right]\ \ge\ 0 .$$
  3. Log of a product = sum of logs: $$\sum_{i=1}^d\Big[f_i\log\frac{\hat p^1_i}{\hat p^0_i}+(1-f_i)\log\frac{1-\hat p^1_i}{1-\hat p^0_i}\Big]+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}\ \ge\ 0 .$$
  4. Collect the $f_i$ terms; everything without an $f_i$ is a constant: $$\sum_{i=1}^d f_i\,\underbrace{\log\frac{\hat p^1_i\,(1-\hat p^0_i)}{\hat p^0_i\,(1-\hat p^1_i)}}_{w_i}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^d\log\frac{1-\hat p^1_i}{1-\hat p^0_i}+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}}_{b}\ \ge\ 0 .$$
Result. Predict $\hat y_{\text{test}}=1$ if $\;w^\top x_{\text{test}}+b\ge 0$, with $$w_i=\log\frac{\hat p^1_i\,(1-\hat p^0_i)}{\hat p^0_i\,(1-\hat p^1_i)},\qquad b=\sum_{i=1}^d\log\frac{1-\hat p^1_i}{1-\hat p^0_i}+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}.$$ The features enter only through $w^\top x$: the decision function is linear. (On a tie, exactly 0, either label is fine; the lecture breaks ties towards spam.) Here $b$ is the bias of the linear function, how far the boundary sits from the origin, not to be confused with the bias of a coin.

3 · Each word is a weight on a scale

$w_i$ depends only on word $i$'s two coins. If the word is more common in spam ($\hat p^1_i>\hat p^0_i$) then $w_i>0$: its presence pushes towards spam. If it is more common in non-spam, $w_i<0$. Equal coins give $w_i=0$: the word says nothing.

Words that are absent matter too. Their evidence, $\log\frac{1-\hat p^1_i}{1-\hat p^0_i}$, was added for every word inside $b$, and $w_i$ takes it back out for the words that turn out to be present. So $b$ is exactly the score of the empty email, prior included.

4 · The picture: a line cutting the corners of a cube

Binary features put every email on a corner of the $d$-dimensional cube $\{0,1\}^d$. The set $w^\top x+b=0$ is a hyperplane; corners on one side are predicted spam, on the other side not spam. With $d=2$ words the cube is a square with four corners:

Beyond the lecture. One consequence of linearity: no setting of the sliders can make corners 01 and 10 spam while 00 and 11 are not spam ("exactly one of the two words"), because no straight line separates them that way. Also, since the score is the log of the posterior ratio, the posterior itself is $P(y{=}1\mid x)=1/(1+e^{-(w^\top x+b)})$, a form that returns later in the course.

5 · Where this is going

Key takeaways

  1. Predict 1 when $\log\frac{P(y=1\mid x)}{P(y=0\mid x)}\ge0$; logs turn the products into sums.
  2. That sum is $w^\top x+b$ with $w_i=\log\frac{\hat p^1_i(1-\hat p^0_i)}{\hat p^0_i(1-\hat p^1_i)}$ and $b=\sum_i\log\frac{1-\hat p^1_i}{1-\hat p^0_i}+\log\frac{\hat p}{1-\hat p}$.
  3. The decision function of naive Bayes is linear in the (binary) features.
  4. $w$ and $b$ depend on the estimated parameters in a complicated way, but in the end they're just a vector and a number.
