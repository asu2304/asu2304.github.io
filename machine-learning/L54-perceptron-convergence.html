Lecture 54 · Classification: a guarantee for the perceptron

Perceptron Convergence: The Radius-Margin Bound

If the data is linearly separable with margin $\gamma$ and lies inside a ball of radius $R$, the perceptron makes at most $R^2/\gamma^2$ mistakes, and therefore stops. The proof traps $\lVert w\rVert^2$ between two quantities: it can grow at most linearly in the number of mistakes, yet must grow at least quadratically. Those can't both hold for long.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈32 min). Previous: L53 · Next: L55.

1 · Three assumptions (only the first is a real one)

  1. Margin. There is a $w^*$ with $(w^{*\top}x_i)\,y_i\ge\gamma$ for all $i$, for some $\gamma>0$.
  2. Radius. $\lVert x_i\rVert\le R$ for all $i$. Always true for a finite dataset: take $R$ = the length of the longest point.
  3. Unit length. $\lVert w^*\rVert=1$, without loss of generality. Dividing $w^{*\top}x=\gamma$ by $\lVert w^*\rVert$ gives the same line written with a unit vector and a rescaled margin $\gamma'=\gamma/\lVert w^*\rVert$, still positive. (Just as $y=mx+c$ and $2y=2mx+2c$ are the same line.)

2 · The proof: count the mistakes

Updates happen only on mistakes. So if the number of mistakes is bounded by a finite number, the algorithm must stop. Let $w^l$ be the vector after $l$ mistakes, and suppose $w^l$ errs on $(x,y)$, so $w^{l+1}=w^l+xy$.

Observation 1: the length can't grow fast.

$$\lVert w^{l+1}\rVert^2=\lVert w^l\rVert^2+\underbrace{2\,(w^{l\top}x)\,y}_{\le\,0\ \text{(it was a mistake)}}+\underbrace{\lVert x\rVert^2y^2}_{\le\,R^2}\ \le\ \lVert w^l\rVert^2+R^2 .$$

The middle term is $\le0$ because a mistake means $w^{l\top}x$ and $y$ have opposite signs. Unrolling back to $w^0=0$: $\ \lVert w^{l}\rVert^2\le l\,R^2$.

Observation 2: the alignment with $w^*$ must grow.

$$w^{l+1\top}w^*=w^{l\top}w^*+\underbrace{(w^{*\top}x)\,y}_{\ge\,\gamma\ \text{(margin)}}\ \ge\ w^{l\top}w^*+\gamma .$$

Each mistake adds at least $\gamma$. Unrolling: $\ w^{l\top}w^*\ge l\,\gamma$. We never know $w^*$, but we can still reason about it.

Observation 3: connect them with Cauchy–Schwarz, $(a^\top b)^2\le\lVert a\rVert^2\lVert b\rVert^2$ (a projection is never longer than the vector, by Pythagoras):

$$l^2\gamma^2\ \le\ (w^{l\top}w^*)^2\ \le\ \lVert w^l\rVert^2\,\underbrace{\lVert w^*\rVert^2}_{=1}\ \le\ l\,R^2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad l\ \le\ \frac{R^2}{\gamma^2}.$$
Radius-margin bound. The number of mistakes the perceptron makes is at most $R^2/\gamma^2$. With $\gamma>0$ that is finite, so the perceptron converges. With $\gamma=0$ (points allowed on the separator) the bound reads $l\le\infty$, which says nothing: exactly the looping example of L53.

4 · What the bound says

  • Bigger margin, fewer mistakes. The bound falls as $1/\gamma^2$: halve the margin and it quadruples.
  • Only the ratio $R/\gamma$ matters. Scale every point by 10 and both $R$ and $\gamma$ scale by 10; nothing changes.
  • No $n$ and no $d$. Neither the number of points nor the dimension appears.
  • It is a worst-case ceiling. Real runs usually stop far below it.

5 · Why this matters

A very simple algorithm with a provable convergence guarantee, one of the first in machine learning. Two major lines of work grew from the perceptron and its analysis: neural networks, and (taking the role of the margin $\gamma$ seriously) support vector machines. The course returns to the margin later.

Key takeaways

  1. Assume margin $\gamma>0$, radius $R$, and (for free) $\lVert w^*\rVert=1$.
  2. After $l$ mistakes: $\lVert w^l\rVert^2\le lR^2$ and $w^{l\top}w^*\ge l\gamma$.
  3. Cauchy–Schwarz joins them: $l^2\gamma^2\le lR^2$, so $l\le R^2/\gamma^2$.
  4. Finite mistakes means the perceptron converges on any dataset that is separable with a positive margin.
