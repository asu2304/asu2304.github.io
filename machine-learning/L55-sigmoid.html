Lecture 55 · Classification: softening the perceptron

From Scores to Probabilities: The Sigmoid

The perceptron's model says $P(y=1\mid x)$ is exactly 1 or 0, which is why one outlier makes a dataset impossible. Relax it: let the score $z=w^\top x$ set a real probability through a link function. The popular choice is the sigmoid, $g(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$. Now every dataset is possible under every $w$, just more or less likely, and choosing $w$ becomes a maximum-likelihood problem.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈20 min). Previous: L54 · Next: L56.

1 · Where the perceptron stands

It converges on linearly separable data, with at most $R^2/\gamma^2$ mistakes. The margin $\gamma$ deserves a longer discussion, which the course parks for later. Today's question is about the assumption:

$$P(y=1\mid x)=\begin{cases}1 & w^\top x\ge0\\0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$

Put one $-$ among the $+$ points and one $+$ among the $-$ points: whichever $w$ the labeller used, it would have labelled those two the other way. The data could not have come from this model. The culprit is that the probabilities are exactly 1 and 0.

2 · Score: how far, and on which side

For a point $x$, call $z=w^\top x$ its score. All points with the same score lie on a line parallel to the separator: $z=0$ is the separator itself, $z=1$ one step out on the positive side, $z=-1$ one step out on the other, $z=100$ far away.

Intuition to build in: a point with $z=100$ should be labelled $+1$ with much higher probability than a point with $z=1$ near the boundary. Every point may get either label; the further along $w$ it sits, the likelier $+1$ becomes, and the further the other way, the likelier $-1$.

3 · Turning a score into a probability

A score lives in $(-\infty,\infty)$; a probability must be in $[0,1]$. We need a function $g$, called a link function, with:

  • $g(0)=\tfrac12$: on the separator we can't tell, so a fair coin.
  • $g(z)\to1$ as $z\to\infty$.
  • $g(z)\to0$ as $z\to-\infty$.

Several functions qualify. One popular choice is the sigmoid (or logistic) function, named for its S shape:

$$g(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} .$$

The lecture's example: a point on the line $w^\top x=1$ gets $+1$ with probability $\frac{1}{1+e^{-1}}=\frac{e}{e+1}\approx0.73$. A point at $w^\top x=10$ gets $+1$ with probability $\frac{e^{10}}{e^{10}+1}\approx0.99995$. It can still come up $-1$; that's just very unlikely.

4 · The new story for labels

  1. Someone hands over $x$ (still no model of where $x$ comes from).
  2. Compute the score $z=w^\top x$ with the labeller's hidden $w$.
  3. Toss a coin with $P(\text{heads})=g(z)$. Heads: label $+1$. Tails: label $-1$.

This is called the sigmoid model or logistic model. An outlier is no longer forbidden: it is a coin that happened to land on its unlikely side.

5 · Every w is now a suspect

Under the sigmoid model, any $w$ could have produced the labels we see: each $w$ gives each point a nonzero chance of either label. So the data no longer rules lines in or out. Instead, each $w$ has a probability of generating the observed labels, and the natural choice is the $w$ that makes that probability largest. That is maximum-likelihood estimation again, which we know how to set up.

Key takeaways

  1. The perceptron model's 0/1 probabilities forbid any dataset with an outlier.
  2. Score $z=w^\top x$: sign says which side, size says how far.
  3. A link function turns scores into probabilities: $g(0)=\frac12$, $g\to1$ as $z\to\infty$, $g\to0$ as $z\to-\infty$. Sigmoid: $g(z)=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}$.
  4. Labels are coin tosses with bias $g(w^\top x)$, so every dataset is allowed and every $w$ has some likelihood. Pick the most likely $w$.
