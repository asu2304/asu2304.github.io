Lecture 6 · Principal component analysis, part 1

From Residues to an Orthonormal Basis

Fitting a line to the residues gives a direction orthogonal to the first. Keep going and you get an orthonormal basis $w_1,\dots,w_d$: the residue procedure is a change of basis. The pay-off comes when the residues vanish (or nearly vanish) after $k\ll d$ rounds: store $k$ shared directions and $k$ numbers per point instead of $d$. How much each round captures tells you when to stop.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈31 min). Previous: L5 · Next: L7.

1 · Where we left off

Data $\{x_1,\dots,x_n\}\subset\mathbb R^d$, centred. Round 1 finds

$$w_1=\arg\max_{\lVert w\rVert^2=1}\;w^\top C\,w,\qquad C=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_ix_i^\top .$$

Round 2 replaces each point by its residue $x_i'=x_i-(x_i^\top w_1)\,w_1$ (still a vector in $\mathbb R^d$), builds $C'=\frac1n\sum_i x_i'x_i'^\top$, and finds $w_2=\arg\max_{\lVert w\rVert^2=1}w^\top C'w$. How are $w_1$ and $w_2$ related?

2 · The second line is orthogonal to the first

  • Projections are perpendicular, so every residue is orthogonal to $w_1$: $\;(x_i')^\top w_1=x_i^\top w_1-(x_i^\top w_1)(w_1^\top w_1)=0$.
  • In 2-D there's only one line through the origin perpendicular to $w_1$, so all the residues line up on it, and the best line for them is exactly that line. Any other line would cost extra error.
  • In higher dimensions the residues lie in the hyperplane perpendicular to $w_1$, and any line that best fits them must also be orthogonal to $w_1$. (The lecture leaves this short proof as an exercise.)
$$\boxed{\;w_2^\top w_1=0\;}\qquad(-w_2\text{ is equally valid; the sign doesn't matter.})$$

3 · Keep going: a new basis

Repeating in $\mathbb R^d$ produces $w_1,\dots,w_d$ with $\lVert w_k\rVert^2=1$ and $w_i^\top w_j=0$ for $i\neq j$: an orthonormal set. Watch what the residues look like:

  1. After round 1: $\;x_i-(x_i^\top w_1)\,w_1$.
  2. After round 2, subtract this vector's projection onto $w_2$: $\;x_i-(x_i^\top w_1)w_1-\big[x_i^\top w_2-(x_i^\top w_1)\underbrace{w_1^\top w_2}_{0}\big]w_2=x_i-(x_i^\top w_1)w_1-(x_i^\top w_2)w_2$.
  3. After $d$ rounds: $\;x_i-\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_i^\top w_k)\,w_k$. There are only $d$ independent directions to remove, so this residue is the zero vector.
  4. Therefore $\;\boxed{x_i=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(x_i^\top w_k)\,w_k}$: every point written in the basis $w_1,\dots,w_d$ instead of the standard basis.

On its own a change of basis gains nothing: any basis can express any point. The gain comes from stopping early.

4 · Where the compression happens

If the data lives in a low-dimensional linear subspace, the residues become zero after $k $$x_i=(x_i^\top w_1)\,w_1+(x_i^\top w_2)\,w_2+(x_i^\top w_3)\,w_3 .$$

With $n=100$: $d\times n=10{,}000$ numbers versus $dk+kn=300+300=600$. Reconstruction is exact: take the linear combination of the representatives with the point's coefficients.

5 · Approximately low-dimensional data: when to stop

Real data rarely makes the residues exactly zero. A 2-D cloud around a line has no perfect 1-D fit, yet one line may be good enough; we have to choose a tolerance. Pythagoras gives a way to measure it. For any unit $w$ and any point,

$$\lVert x_i\rVert^2=\big\lVert x_i-(x_i^\top w)\,w\big\rVert^2+(x_i^\top w)^2 .$$

Average over $i$:

$$\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_i\lVert x_i\rVert^2}_{\text{fixed by the data}}=\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_i\big\lVert x_i-(x_i^\top w)\,w\big\rVert^2}_{\text{average error}}+\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w)^2}_{\text{captured}} .$$

Minimising the error is the same as maximising the captured part (that's the equivalence from L4). Record the captured amount $\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w_k)^2$ in each round $k$:

  • It's largest in round 1. If a later round could capture more, round 1 would have found that line instead. So it falls round by round.
  • Often it drops sharply at an elbow: the first $k$ directions are signal, the rest look like error.
  • Rule of thumb: keep the smallest $k$ whose rounds capture, say, 95% of the total over all $d$ rounds. It's a rule of thumb, not a guarantee that this $k$ is "best"; that would need more assumptions about the data.

6 · The algorithm so far

  1. Centre the data.
  2. Find the best line for the current points, record how much it captures, and replace the points by their residues.
  3. Repeat until what a new round captures is too small relative to the total (e.g. the first $k$ rounds already capture 95%).
  4. Represent each point by its $k$ coefficients $x_i^\top w_1,\dots,x_i^\top w_k$.

Key takeaways

  1. Residues are orthogonal to the line just found, so each new line is orthogonal to all earlier ones.
  2. Running all $d$ rounds yields an orthonormal basis, and $x_i=\sum_k(x_i^\top w_k)w_k$ exactly: a change of basis.
  3. Compression comes from stopping after $k$ rounds: store $dk+kn$ numbers instead of $dn$.
  4. Pythagoras: $\text{avg}\lVert x\rVert^2=\text{avg error}+\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w)^2$, so the captured amount per round measures progress.
  5. It decreases with each round; stop at the elbow or once ~95% is captured (a rule of thumb).
