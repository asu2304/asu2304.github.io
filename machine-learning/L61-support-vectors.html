Lecture 61 · Support vector machines: which points matter

Support Vectors and Complementary Slackness

At the optimum, each multiplier times its constraint is zero: $\alpha^*_i\,\big(1-(w^{*\top}x_i)y_i\big)=0$. So a point with $\alpha^*_i>0$ must lie exactly on a margin line, and every point beyond the margin has $\alpha^*_i=0$. The few points on the margin lines, the support vectors, determine $w^*$ completely. This sparsity, plus kernels, is the support vector machine.

1 · Complementary slackness

Let $w^*$ solve the primal and $\alpha^*$ the dual of $\min f(w)$ s.t. $g(w)\le0$ (convex). Evaluate both sides of $\min_w\max_{\alpha\ge0}L=\max_{\alpha\ge0}\min_wL$ at these solutions:

$$f(w^*)\ =\ \min_w\big[f(w)+\alpha^*g(w)\big]\ \le\ f(w^*)+\alpha^*g(w^*)\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\alpha^*\,g(w^*)\ \ge\ 0 .$$

The left equality: $w^*$ is feasible, so the inner max gives $f(w^*)$. The inequality: a minimum over all $w$ is at most the value at the particular point $w^*$. But feasibility also says $\alpha^*\ge0$ and $g(w^*)\le0$, so $\alpha^*g(w^*)\le0$. Both can only hold if

$$\alpha^*\,g(w^*)=0\qquad\text{and, with many constraints,}\qquad\alpha^*_i\,g_i(w^*)=0\ \ \text{for every } i .$$ Complementary slackness: if one factor has slack (is strictly non-zero), the other must be zero. $\alpha^*_i>0\Rightarrow g_i(w^*)=0$, and $g_i(w^*)<0\Rightarrow\alpha^*_i=0$.

2 · What it means for the SVM

Here $g_i(w)=1-(w^\top x_i)y_i$, so $\alpha^*_i\,\big(1-(w^{*\top}x_i)y_i\big)=0$ for all $i$. Therefore:

$$\alpha^*_i>0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(w^{*\top}x_i)\,y_i=1 .$$
  • A point that contributes to $w^*=\sum_i\alpha^*_iy_ix_i$ lies on one of the two lines $w^{*\top}x=\pm1$, the supporting hyperplanes.
  • Every point strictly beyond those lines has $\alpha^*_i=0$ and contributes nothing.
  • The implication runs one way: a point on a supporting hyperplane may still have $\alpha^*_i=0$.

The contributing points are called support vectors, and the algorithm, built around finding them, is the support vector machine (SVM; Vapnik and colleagues, 1990s). $w^*$ is a sparse combination of the data: with a billion points, perhaps only a hundred matter. The rest can be thrown away.

3 · Predicting with a kernel

For a test point we need $w^{*\top}x_{\text{test}}$. Using $w^*=\sum_i\alpha^*_iy_ix_i$:

$$w^{*\top}x_{\text{test}}=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha^*_i\,y_i\,(x_i^\top x_{\text{test}})\qquad\xrightarrow{\ \text{kernel}\ }\qquad w^{*\top}\phi(x_{\text{test}})=\sum_{i=1}^n\alpha^*_i\,y_i\,k(x_i,x_{\text{test}}) .$$

In the kernel version $w^*$ lives in a high-dimensional space and is never built explicitly. Prediction needs only kernel evaluations against the points with $\alpha^*_i>0$. Sparsity is what makes that cheap: keep the support vectors and their $\alpha^*_iy_i$, discard everything else.

4 · What's still missing: outliers

Everything so far assumes the data is separable (linearly, or in the kernel's feature space). With a stray $+$ among the $-$ points, no feasible $w$ exists and the algorithm gives up.

Can't a kernel fix that? It's the wrong tool. Kernels address structure: the true boundary is non-linear (quadratic, cubic…). Outliers are noise: the structure is still linear, a few points are just on the wrong side. Bending the boundary around noise with a powerful kernel fits the noise and hurts accuracy.

Key takeaways

  1. Complementary slackness: $\alpha^*_i\,g_i(w^*)=0$ for every constraint.
  2. For the SVM: $\alpha^*_i>0\Rightarrow(w^{*\top}x_i)y_i=1$. Contributing points sit on the supporting hyperplanes.
  3. Those points are the support vectors; $w^*$ is a sparse combination of the data.
  4. Kernel prediction: $\sum_i\alpha^*_iy_ik(x_i,x_{\text{test}})$, over support vectors only.
  5. Outliers are noise, not structure; kernels are not the fix.
