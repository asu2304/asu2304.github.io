Lecture 67 · Ensembles: bootstrap aggregation

Bagging and Random Forests

Averaging independent estimates keeps the mean and shrinks the variance. Bagging applies this to classifiers: build many datasets from one by sampling with replacement (bootstrapping), train an overfit, low-bias learner on each, and take a majority vote (aggregation). Random forests add feature bagging so the trees disagree more and the vote cancels more of their noise.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈31 min). Previous: L66 · Next: L68.

1 · Why average? A lesson from estimating a mean

Take $x_1,\dots,x_n$ drawn i.i.d. from a Gaussian with unknown mean $\mu$ (say $5.5$) and variance 1. Consider the estimators

$$\hat\mu_1=x_1,\quad \hat\mu_2=x_2,\quad\dots,\quad\hat\mu_n=x_n,\qquad \hat\mu_{\text{ML}}=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n x_i .$$
  • Every one of them is unbiased: $\mathbb E[\hat\mu_1]=\mathbb E[x_1]=\mu$, and by linearity $\mathbb E[\hat\mu_{\text{ML}}]=\mu$ too. So unbiasedness alone can't tell them apart.
  • But $\hat\mu_1$ jumps around a lot from one sample to the next, while the average barely moves. Fluctuation is measured by variance.
Averaging reduces variance, provided the things being averaged are decorrelated (here they're independent samples). Bagging borrows exactly this idea for classifiers.

2 · If we had many independent datasets

Suppose someone handed us $m$ datasets $D_1,\dots,D_m$, each of $n$ labelled points $(x,y)$ with $x\in\mathbb R^d$, $y\in\{-1,+1\}$, drawn independently from the same distribution. Train a weak learner on each, for example a decision tree grown to full depth (it overfits: low bias, high variance), to get $h_1,\dots,h_m:\mathbb R^d\to\{\pm1\}$. Then aggregate:

$$h^\star(x)=\operatorname{sign}\!\Big(\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m h_i(x)\Big),\qquad \operatorname{sign}(z)=\begin{cases}+1 & z\ge 0\\ -1 & z<0\end{cases}$$

That's a majority vote. The individual trees have low bias but high variance; averaging them should keep the bias low and bring the variance down, which is what lowers the error.

3 · We only have one dataset: bootstrapping

Nobody gives us $m$ datasets. Cutting our single dataset $D$ into $m$ pieces makes them independent, but each piece gets tiny, and a learner trained on very few points learns little. The only way to get many full-size datasets from one is to allow repeats:

  1. Bootstrap (uniform sampling with replacement): to fill a bag, pick a point from $D$ uniformly at random, put it back, and repeat $n$ times. With $D=\{x_1,\dots,x_5\}$ one bag might be $\{x_3,x_4,x_5,x_3,x_5\}$ and another $\{x_1,x_1,x_5,x_4,x_1\}$.
  2. Make $m$ bags $D_1,\dots,D_m$, each with $n$ points, and train a learner $h_i$ on each bag.
  3. Aggregate: $h^\star(x)=\operatorname{sign}\big(\frac1m\sum_i h_i(x)\big)$.

Bootstrap + aggregation = bagging.

4 · How much of the data lands in each bag?

A particular point $x_j$ is missed by one draw with probability $1-\frac1n$, so it's missed by all $n$ independent draws with probability $(1-\frac1n)^n$. Hence

$$P(x_j\in D_i)=1-\Big(1-\frac1n\Big)^{n}\;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\;1-\frac1e\approx 0.63 .$$

So each bag contains roughly two-thirds of the distinct points, and the rest of its slots are repeats. Each overfit tree fits the noise specific to its own bag, and different bags repeat different points. Their noise is different, so when the trees vote, the noise tends to average out: the variance goes down while the bias stays roughly where it was.

A small numeric note, beyond the video. The lecture rounds the limit as "something like 67%"; the exact value is $1-1/e=0.632$, so about 37% of each bag's slots are repeats. Already at $n=20$ the share is $0.642$, so the limit kicks in fast. The points a bag misses are a free validation set for that tree: averaging each point's error over the trees that never saw it gives the out-of-bag error estimate, without a separate hold-out set.

5 · Random forests: decorrelating the trees

Variance drops most when the learners being averaged are uncorrelated. Bagged trees on similar bags still tend to pick the same strong features, so they stay correlated. A random forest pushes them apart:

  • Bag decision trees, and grow each one to purity (zero training error, no cross-validation): low bias.
  • Feature bagging: at every node, instead of searching all $d$ features for the best "feature < threshold" question, draw a random subset of features and pick the best split among those only (by information gain, Gini, or your favourite impurity measure). One node might choose among $\{f_1,f_5,f_{23},f_{40}\}$, the next among $\{f_1,f_{10},f_{20},f_{40}\}$.
  • Rule of thumb: about $\sqrt d$ features per node for classification; for regression something of order $d$, like $d/3$. Tune if you have compute to spare.
  • Variants randomise even more, for example sampling the candidate thresholds too.

Random forests work for regression as well (use regression trees), and they are among the most widely used classifiers in practice: the variance goes down, the bias typically doesn't go up, so the error falls.

6 · A practical bonus: it's parallel

The bags are independent, so the $m$ learners can be trained at the same time on different cores. You only need all of them at the aggregation step. That's a big reason bagging and random forests are popular in practice.

7 · Two choices we made, and a better way

Bagging made two design choices: bootstrapping by uniform sampling with replacement, and aggregation by a plain average (justified by "averaging reduces variance"). Are these the best choices? Could the bags be sampled in a more principled way, and the classifiers combined more cleverly? Yes. Doing both steps in a principled way leads to boosting, the next lecture.

Key takeaways

  1. Averaging decorrelated estimates keeps them unbiased and reduces variance.
  2. Bagging = bootstrap (sample $n$ points with replacement, $m$ times) + aggregate ($h^\star=\operatorname{sign}\frac1m\sum_i h_i$, a majority vote).
  3. Each bag holds about $1-(1-\frac1n)^n\approx 63\%$ of the distinct points; different bags repeat different points, so the overfit trees fit different noise.
  4. Bagging mainly reduces variance; use low-bias, high-variance learners such as fully grown trees.
  5. Random forest = bagged full trees + feature bagging (≈ $\sqrt d$ random features per node) to decorrelate the trees.
  6. Bagging parallelises; boosting will replace both of its steps with principled ones.
