Lecture 68 · Ensembles: turning weak learners into a strong one

Boosting and AdaBoost

Boosting keeps bagging's two steps but makes both principled. Instead of uniform bags, it keeps a distribution over the data points and, after each round, raises the weight of the points the last learner got wrong, so the next learner focuses on them. Instead of a plain average, it takes a weighted vote, with weight $\alpha_t$ growing as the learner's weighted error shrinks. With learners only slightly better than random, the training error provably reaches zero.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈32 min). Previous: L67 · Next: L69.

1 · The goal: weak learner in, strong learner out

A weak learner here may be an underfit classifier, which is easy and cheap to get. The standard example is a decision stump: a decision tree stopped after one question, "feature $f$ < threshold?" (sometimes two levels). In 2-D it just cuts the plane into two half-planes with a vertical or horizontal line. Typical trees in practice have depth 10–15, so a stump is a big drop: high bias, low variance, and it won't reach 100% training accuracy on its own.

The question boosting answers: given a black box that outputs such weak learners, can we feed it cleverly chosen inputs and combine its outputs into a strong learner, one with zero training error? Yes, and AdaBoost ("adaptive boosting", Freund and Schapire, 1990s, who won the Gödel Prize for it) gives the recipe.

2 · The AdaBoost algorithm

Input: $S=\{(x_1,y_1),\dots,(x_n,y_n)\}$, $x_i\in\mathbb R^d$, $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$.

  1. Initialise a distribution over the points: $D_1(i)=\frac1n$ for every $i$ (the lecture writes $D_0$). (Sampling a bag from it is exactly bagging's uniform sampling with replacement; the difference is that boosting will change it every round.)
  2. For $t=1,\dots,T$: give $(S,D_t)$ to the weak learner and get $h_t:\mathbb R^d\to\{\pm1\}$. If the learner accepts weighted points, pass the weights directly; if not (vanilla decision trees), sample a bag from $S$ according to $D_t$, so a point with weight 0.7 has a 70% chance on each draw.
  3. Weighted error: add up the weights of the points $h_t$ gets wrong, $\;\operatorname{err}(h_t)=\sum_{i:\,h_t(x_i)\ne y_i}D_t(i)$, and set $\;\alpha_t=\ln\sqrt{\dfrac{1-\operatorname{err}(h_t)}{\operatorname{err}(h_t)}}$.
  4. Re-weight multiplicatively (mistakes go up, correct points go down): $$\hat D_{t+1}(i)=\begin{cases}D_t(i)\,e^{\alpha_t} & h_t(x_i)\ne y_i\\ D_t(i)\,e^{-\alpha_t} & h_t(x_i)=y_i\end{cases}$$
  5. Normalise so the weights sum to 1 again: $\;D_{t+1}(i)=\dfrac{\hat D_{t+1}(i)}{\sum_j\hat D_{t+1}(j)}$.
  6. Output the weighted vote $\;h^\star(x)=\operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_{t=1}^{T}\alpha_t\,h_t(x)\Big)$.
Why raise the weights of the mistakes? Say the first stump gets 600 of 1,000 points right and 400 wrong. Giving the 400 more weight makes them show up more often in the next bag, so the next learner, which is also better than random, has to find features that work for them. Each round corrects the previous round's mistakes.

Bagging vs boosting: bootstrapping becomes sampling from a carefully chosen distribution that pushes weight towards hard points; aggregation becomes a weighted average, with the learners' $\alpha_t$ as weights. The same $\alpha_t$ is used for both the re-weighting and the vote.

3 · The lecture's worked update

Three points with weights $D_1=(0.3,\,0.5,\,0.2)$. Suppose $h_1$ misclassifies $x_1$ and gets $x_2,x_3$ right. Then

$$D_2=\frac{\big(0.3\,e^{\alpha_1},\;0.5\,e^{-\alpha_1},\;0.2\,e^{-\alpha_1}\big)}{Z},\qquad Z=0.3\,e^{\alpha_1}+0.5\,e^{-\alpha_1}+0.2\,e^{-\alpha_1}.$$

A neat consequence of this α, beyond the video. After the update, the points $h_t$ got wrong carry total weight $\frac{\operatorname{err}\,e^{\alpha}}{\operatorname{err}\,e^{\alpha}+(1-\operatorname{err})e^{-\alpha}}$, and with $e^{\alpha}=\sqrt{(1-\operatorname{err})/\operatorname{err}}$ both terms equal $\sqrt{\operatorname{err}(1-\operatorname{err})}$, so the share is exactly 1/2. Under the new weights $h_t$ is no better than a coin toss, so the weak learner is forced to come up with something genuinely new. Check the third stat above: it's always 50%. Also note $\alpha=0$ when $\operatorname{err}=\frac12$ (a useless learner gets no vote) and $\alpha<0$ if $\operatorname{err}>\frac12$ (the vote flips it).

4 · The guarantee

Let $\gamma$ measure how much better than random the weak learner is: 60% accuracy means $\gamma=0.1$, 70% means $\gamma=0.2$, 90% means $\gamma=0.4$. The analysis of AdaBoost (not derived in the course) shows that with $\alpha_t$ set as above, the training error falls fast, and once the number of rounds passes a threshold that grows only logarithmically in $n$ and like $1/\gamma^2$, the training error is exactly zero:

$$T>\frac{\ln n}{2\gamma^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{training error of }h^\star=0 .$$
  • More data needs more rounds, but only logarithmically more.
  • A weaker learner (51% accurate, $\gamma=0.01$) needs many more rounds than a 75% one ($\gamma=0.25$).
  • The beautiful part: AdaBoost never needs to know $\gamma$. Earlier boosting algorithms had to be told how weak the learner was; this one adapts.
Where the threshold comes from, beyond the video. The standard bound is: training error $\le\prod_t 2\sqrt{\operatorname{err}_t(1-\operatorname{err}_t)}\le e^{-2\gamma^2T}$ when every $\operatorname{err}_t\le\frac12-\gamma$. The training error is a multiple of $\frac1n$, so once $e^{-2\gamma^2T}<\frac1n$ it must be 0, which is the condition $T>\frac{\ln n}{2\gamma^2}$. 5 · Training error zero ... and the test error?

What we really care about is the test error. You might expect that once the training error hits zero, adding more weak learners only fits noise, and the test error starts rising: overfitting. Surprisingly, on many datasets the test error keeps going down even after the training error is zero. It never reaches zero (the training data only carries so much information), and you can construct datasets where it does turn up, but typically it doesn't.

The explanation (beyond the course's scope): even after every training point is classified correctly, new rounds keep increasing the margin of the combined classifier, and a larger margin means better generalisation, as with SVMs. Practical advice: don't stop the moment the training error is zero; watch a validation error and stop when its trend reverses.

6 · Pros and cons

BoostingBagging
Weak learners can be underfit (stumps); boosting attacks bias.Learners are usually overfit (deep trees); bagging attacks variance.
Sequential: round $t$ needs the mistakes of round $t-1$, so it can't be parallelised and takes longer.Parallel: bags are independent.
Typically performs better in practice.Faster to train.
  • Boosting a strong learner (an SVM, say) usually brings no improvement; the gains come from boosting a quick-and-dirty learner like a stump. Boosted stumps and SVMs typically perform comparably, and each wins on some datasets.
  • Both bagging and boosting are off-the-shelf classifiers: very little tuning, and they work extremely well in practice.

Key takeaways

  1. Boosting turns weak learners (just better than random, e.g. stumps) into a strong learner.
  2. AdaBoost keeps weights $D_t$ over points: start uniform; multiply mistakes by $e^{\alpha_t}$ and correct points by $e^{-\alpha_t}$; normalise.
  3. $\alpha_t=\ln\sqrt{(1-\operatorname{err}_t)/\operatorname{err}_t}$, where $\operatorname{err}_t$ is the total weight of $h_t$'s mistakes; final classifier $\operatorname{sign}\sum_t\alpha_th_t(x)$.
  4. Training error reaches 0 once $T$ exceeds a threshold growing like $\ln n/\gamma^2$, and AdaBoost doesn't need to know $\gamma$.
  5. The test error often keeps falling after the training error is 0 (larger margins). Boosting is sequential; bagging is parallel.
