Lecture 7 · Principal component analysis, part 2

PCA: Eigenvectors and Variance Maximisation

The residue procedure has a linear-algebra shortcut: its lines are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix $C$, in order of eigenvalue. Each eigenvalue is the variance of the data projected on that direction. So on centred data, minimising reconstruction error is the same as maximising variance, and the new coordinates are de-correlated. This is principal component analysis.

Source: this lecture on YouTube (≈27 min). Previous: L6 · Next: L8.

1 · Enter linear algebra

The problem in each round is

$$\max_{\lVert w\rVert^2=1}\;w^\top C\,w,\qquad C=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_ix_i^\top\quad(\text{the covariance matrix, for centred data}).$$
  • Its solution $w_1$ is the eigenvector of $C$ with the largest eigenvalue. The lecture cites the Hilbert min-max theorem (a variant appears in linear-algebra courses).
  • More is true: the eigenvectors of $C$ form an orthonormal basis, and that's exactly the basis the residue procedure builds.
  • The line found in round $k$ is the eigenvector $w_k$ for the $k$-th largest eigenvalue. Equivalently: maximise $w^\top Cw$ over unit $w$ orthogonal to the top $k-1$ eigenvectors. That matches the residue view, because the residues in round $k$ are orthogonal to all earlier lines by construction.

2 · What does an eigenvalue mean?

Take $Cw_1=\lambda_1w_1$ with $\lVert w_1\rVert=1$. Then

$$\lambda_1=w_1^\top C\,w_1=w_1^\top\Big(\frac1n\sum_{i}x_ix_i^\top\Big)w_1=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_i^\top w_1)^2 ,$$

the same quantity L6 recorded each round to decide when to stop. In general $\lambda_k=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w_k)^2$, so the round-by-round plot is just the eigenvalues in decreasing order, and the stopping rule becomes

$$\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\lambda_i(C)}{\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i(C)}\;\ge\;0.95\qquad(\text{a rule of thumb: keep }\ell\text{ directions}).$$

The ratio makes sense because each $\lambda_k$ is an average of squares, so $\lambda_k\ge0$. That's also a proof that the covariance matrix is positive semi-definite.

3 · The eigenvalue is a variance

Fix any unit direction $w$ (not necessarily the best one) and collect the numbers $x_1^\top w,\dots,x_n^\top w$.

  1. Their mean: $\;\frac1n\sum_i x_i^\top w=\Big(\frac1n\sum_i x_i\Big)^{\!\top}w=0$, because the data is centred.
  2. Their variance: $\;\frac1n\sum_i\big(x_i^\top w-0\big)^2=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w)^2=w^\top Cw$.
  3. So $w^\top Cw$ is the variance of the projected values, and the best line maximises it.
On centred data, error minimisation ⇔ variance maximisation. The line with the smallest average reconstruction error is the line along which the projections are most spread out. (Centring matters: without it, the mean of the projections isn't 0 and the equivalence breaks.)

4 · Why we want spread

Compress the data, but keep the ability to tell points apart, because the compressed representation will be used later (say, for supervised learning). Project onto a bad line and the proxies crowd together. In the extreme, data lying on one line projected onto the perpendicular line all lands on the zero vector, and every point looks the same. So we want directions where the projections don't crowd, measured by variance: as high as possible, keeping as much information as possible.

5 · Example: height and weight

For centred (height, weight) data, taller people tend to be heavier, so one number tells you something about the other. The best line is roughly $w_1=\big(\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)$ and the next is perpendicular, $w_2=\big(-\tfrac1{\sqrt2},\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\big)$ (either sign works). The new coordinates are

$$x^\top w_1=\frac{\text{height}+\text{weight}}{\sqrt2},\qquad x^\top w_2=\frac{\text{weight}-\text{height}}{\sqrt2}.$$

Plot the data in these coordinates and the axes have effectively rotated by 45°. Knowing $(\text{height}+\text{weight})/\sqrt2$ tells you nothing about which side of the axis $(\text{weight}-\text{height})/\sqrt2$ falls. The principal directions are de-correlated: each one adds information the previous ones didn't give. Tick "plot in (w₁, w₂) coordinates" above to see it.

6 · The algorithm: principal component analysis

  1. Centre the data and form the covariance matrix $C=\frac1n\sum_i x_ix_i^\top$.
  2. Find its top $k$ eigenvectors $w_1,\dots,w_k$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_1\ge\dots\ge\lambda_k$, choosing $k$ so they explain ~95% of the variance.
  3. Represent each point by $\big(x_i^\top w_1,\dots,x_i^\top w_k\big)\in\mathbb R^k$.
  • The $w_1,\dots,w_k$ are the principal components.
  • Going from $\mathbb R^d$ to $\mathbb R^k$ with $k\ll d$ is dimensionality reduction; $k$ is the data's "essential" dimension.
  • Each new feature $x_i^\top w_j$ is a weighted combination of the original features, and different components are de-correlated (loosely, "independent").
Going beyond the video. The theorem cited as "Hilbert min-max" is usually called the Courant–Fischer (or Rayleigh–Ritz) theorem: $\max_{\lVert w\rVert=1}w^\top Cw=\lambda_1$, reached at its eigenvector, and the $k$-th eigenvalue is the maximum over unit vectors orthogonal to the first $k-1$ eigenvectors. "De-correlated" can be checked directly: the sample covariance of the new coordinates is $\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w_a)(x_i^\top w_b)=w_a^\top Cw_b=\lambda_b\,w_a^\top w_b=0$ for $a\ne b$.

Key takeaways

  1. The best line is the top eigenvector of $C$; round $k$ of the residue procedure gives the $k$-th eigenvector.
  2. $\lambda_k=w_k^\top Cw_k=\frac1n\sum_i(x_i^\top w_k)^2\ge0$, so $C$ is positive semi-definite.
  3. For centred data, $w^\top Cw$ is the variance of the projections, so error minimisation ⇔ variance maximisation.
  4. High variance means the projections don't crowd and points stay distinguishable.
  5. Choose $k$ by explained variance, $\sum_{i\le k}\lambda_i/\sum_i\lambda_i\ge0.95$; the components are de-correlated combinations of features.
