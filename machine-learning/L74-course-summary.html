Course Summary and Future Directions

The course in one sentence: unsupervised learning (representations, clusters, estimation) then supervised learning (regression, then classification), ending with ensembles and neural networks. This page maps all 74 lectures, traces the ideas that kept coming back, and lists what the course left out and the open problems of deployable machine learning: fairness, explainability, privacy, AI on the edge, and models that keep learning.

2 · Ideas that kept coming back

  • Write the problem as an optimisation, then solve it. Reconstruction error (PCA), the K-means objective, the likelihood, squared error, loss + regulariser. When the problem is convex we get a unique optimum; when it isn't (K-means, EM, neural networks) we settle for local optima and care about initialisation.
  • Linear methods + kernels. Whenever an algorithm only needs dot products between data points, a kernel buys a non-linear version for free.
  • Probabilistic views explain the choices. Gaussian noise ⇔ squared error, a Gaussian prior ⇔ ridge, logistic loss ⇔ maximum likelihood for a sigmoid model.
  • Fit versus generalisation. Memorising the training data is easy and useless; restricting the model, regularising, cross-validating and ensembling are all ways to generalise.

4 · Deployable AI / ML: what happens after training

This course covered the most important piece, the algorithm, but it's one piece of a practical pipeline. Deploying a model raises new questions; the lecture names them to make you curious rather than to solve them.

  1. Fairness. A risk-score system in the US, predicting whether a convict would reoffend, gave scores favourable to one ethnicity and not another. Nothing in logistic regression or an SVM prefers a group: the bias was in the data, because earlier judgements were biased, and any algorithm trained on biased data produces biased outputs. Can we make fair algorithms? There are some solutions; it's still a research area.
  2. Explainability. A doctor using a model to suggest a medication needs to know why. A decision tree can say: this feature was below a threshold, that one above, down to a leaf. A 100-hidden-layer network can't. If a self-driving car fails to brake and someone dies, who is accountable, and how can the model explain its decision? This is explainable AI.
  3. Privacy. Training data can be confidential (salaries, health records). If a model might leak information about its training points, people won't want to contribute data. What does "private" even mean, and how do we build private algorithms?
  4. AI for the edge. Networks with billions of parameters don't fit a phone's memory. Can we truncate precision, sub-sample, or train small networks that mimic large ones?
  5. Changing data: continual and transfer learning. A model predicting IPL winners from past scores and players goes stale as players, teams and play change. You can't retrain from scratch for every new point, so how do you keep learning after deployment? And how do you transfer knowledge from one domain to another? (The lecture also mentions distributed learning.)

5 · Where to go next

This was an introductory course. Next steps the lecture suggests: a deep learning course for unstructured data, and research in the deployable-AI directions above (fairness, explainability, privacy, distributed learning, AI for the edge, transfer and continual learning).

Key takeaways

  1. Unsupervised: PCA / kernel PCA, K-means / kernel K-means, estimation (ML, Bayesian, GMM + EM). Supervised: OLS, ridge, lasso; kNN, trees, logistic regression, perceptron, SVM, bagging, boosting, neural networks.
  2. Not covered: semi-supervised, self-supervised and sequential decision making.
  3. Deployment raises fairness (bias in the data), explainability (trees vs deep nets), privacy, edge constraints, and learning under change.
  4. The course is a foundation for deep learning and for these research directions.
