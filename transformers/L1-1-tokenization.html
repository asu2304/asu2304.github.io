Autoregressive LLMs and Tokenisation

A transformer only understands numbers, so text must first be chopped into units from a fixed vocabulary and each unit replaced by an ID. The units are neither characters nor words but subwords, found greedily by byte-pair encoding, so that rare and brand-new words are built from pieces the model already knows.

1 · Where this sequence of lectures goes ▶ 0:05

This lecture opens a short run of three or four lectures on large language models. Reinforcement learning is slotted in between, because modern reasoning models can't be explained without a little RL. A guest lecture on systems ML follows: how to make transformers run efficiently on GPUs.

The paradigm, in the lecturer's telling, starts with OpenAI's GPT-3: autoregressive large language models. Earlier transformer variants that are not autoregressive are skipped.

Autoregressive = generate tokens one by one, each new token depending on all the previous ones. The probability of a whole piece of text is modelled the same way, sequentially, one token at a time.

Before the "real meat" (the probability model and the transformer), one piece of setup: tokenisation.

2 · Why tokenise, and why not characters or words ▶ 1:48

Transformers are numerical models: they take in numbers. Text has to be turned into something they can read, and the first step is to decide the smallest unit of input handed to the model. The two obvious choices both have problems.

UnitWhat goes wrong
CharacterFar too many units: a text needs very many tokens, so very many steps.
WordLong, rare words. internationalization as a single unit is rare, and unrelated (as far as the model knows) to internationalized or nation. Nothing learned about one transfers to the others.
SubwordThe compromise used in practice: common words are single tokens, rare words split into reusable pieces.

If you understand internationalized, you should understand internationalization to some degree without seeing more data. Word-level units throw that away.

3 · Subwords let you understand new words ▶ 3:30

The lecturer's example is a word from his own notes, LLMefication: a brand-new word nobody used before about 2020, and still uncommon. As one unit, the model would have to see it many times to learn what it means. As subwords:

LLM + efication

The model has seen LLM many times and already understands it; -efication has its own meaning in English. Combine the two understandings and you understand the word: far more efficient than learning the whole string as an atom.

This matters even more in biology (very long technical words) and in German: long words that are really combinations of many subwords. Subword tokenisation lets the model understand each piece and then the combination.

4 · Vocabulary and token IDs ▶ 4:56

Tokenisation means having a predefined vocabulary: a list of subwords. "a" is a token; "dog" is a token; pretty much every common word is a token these days. The lecturer suggests playing with OpenAI's online tokenizer playground, where you find that even "black dog" is a single token: "black" merged with "dog".

  • Size. For open-source models, where we know the numbers, vocabularies are on the order of $10^5$ (100k) or more. The recent Qwen 3.5 open models have about 250k tokens (the lecturer guesses the exact size has to do with how many bits are used to store a token ID).
  • Tokenising a string: cover it with subwords that are in the vocabulary, turning it into a sequence of segments.
  • IDs. Every token has an ID. In his example sentence A black dog runs, maybe "A" is ID 1, "dog" is 356, "run" is 250 (numbers made up). Every text becomes a sequence of IDs, one per token; here most words are one token each, and the comma is its own token.

Splits are not always along meaningful lines. Checking a word like (un)happiness, the lecturer found an odd split, something like a prefix, then "h", then "appiness". In contrast a long biomedical term (one ending in -blastoma) split into three pieces, each with its own meaning, which is exactly what you'd want.

5 · Every model has its own tokenizer ▶ 8:02

The algorithm that turns text into tokens is the tokenizer. The exact vocabulary depends on the model: some models share a tokenizer, but generally it is model-specific, and companies have proprietary approaches.

A recent example: news (which the lecturer had not verified) that Anthropic updated Claude's tokenizer to be more granular, so the same text becomes roughly 1.5× as many tokens. Since you pay per token, the same amount of text then costs more.

6 · Byte-pair encoding: greedy merging of frequent pairs ▶ 9:10

The keyword for how vocabularies are built is the BPE tokenizer, byte-pair encoding. The lecture deliberately skips the precise algorithm (it is "a very small quick setup") but gives the idea:

  1. Start from the smallest units: all the characters.
  2. Find the most frequent combination of two adjacent units in the training text.
  3. Add it to the vocabulary as a new unit and merge it everywhere.
  4. Repeat. It is a greedy algorithm: frequent subwords end up in the vocabulary.
Going beyond the lecture: the exact algorithm. Training BPE records the ordered list of merges. To tokenise new text, split it into characters (real tokenizers start from the 256 byte values, hence "byte"-pair, so every string is representable) and replay the merges in the order they were learned. The vocabulary size is the number of base symbols plus the number of merges, which is how designers pick 100k or 250k.

7 · From here on, text is a sequence of IDs ▶ 10:16

From now on we assume a tokenizer is given, so "a text" simply means a sequence of tokens

$$x_1, x_2, \dots, x_T,\qquad x_i \in V,$$

where $V$ is the vocabulary (size $|V|$ maybe 250,000) and each $x_i$ is an index into it: "the 5th token", "the 105th token", and so on. The next part asks how to put a probability distribution on such sequences.

Key takeaways

  1. Modern LLMs (from GPT-3 on) are autoregressive: tokens are generated one at a time, each depending on all earlier ones.
  2. Characters give too many steps; words make rare and new words unlearnable. Subwords let the model reuse what it knows (LLM + efication).
  3. A tokenizer maps text to a sequence of IDs from a fixed vocabulary of ~$10^5$ tokens (about 250k for Qwen 3.5); each model family has its own.
  4. BPE builds the vocabulary greedily by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair.
  5. After tokenisation, a text is just $x_1,\dots,x_T$ with each $x_i\in V$.
