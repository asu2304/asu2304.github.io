Supervised Fine-Tuning and Instruction Tuning

Zero-shot and in-context abilities first appeared by accident. To strengthen them, collect pairs of (instruction, answer) and keep training the pretrained model to maximise the likelihood of the answer given the instruction. That is supervised fine-tuning; with instruction data it is called instruction tuning.

1 · Strengthening what arose by accident ▶ 64:18

At first, zero-shot and in-context learning arose naturally. Nobody trained for them, yet they worked, which was a surprise. Then people tried to reinforce these capabilities, and the way to do it is relatively easy: a kind of SFT that people call instruction tuning.

2 · The data and the loss ▶ 64:55

Collect a dataset of pairs $(x_i,y_i)$, $i=1,\dots,n$:

  • $x_i$ is the instruction (task description; the two words mean the same here), with $L_i$ tokens $x_{i,1},\dots,x_{i,L_i}$;
  • $y_i$ is the answer, with $T_i$ tokens $y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,T_i}$.

Take an existing checkpoint (the pretrained model) and continue training it to have this behaviour. The instruction $x$ is treated as given: you don't need to predict it. Only $y$ is predicted.

  1. Minimise the negative log-likelihood of each answer given its instruction, averaged over the dataset: $$\mathcal L(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n -\log p_\theta\big(y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,T_i}\,\big|\,x_i\big).$$
  2. Because $y_i$ is a sequence, the chain rule splits its probability into next-token terms, as in pretraining: $$\log p_\theta(y_{i}\mid x_i)=\sum_{t=1}^{T_i}\log p_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\,\big|\,x_i,\ y_{i,<t}\big).$$
  3. So each term says: given the instruction and the first $t-1$ answer tokens, predict the $t$-th answer token. Sum these losses, and that's it.

There is also a normalisation detail (how the per-token terms are averaged), which the lecturer calls minor and leaves to the lecture notes.

Going beyond the lecture: the normalisation detail. Two common choices: average over examples (as above), so a long answer contributes more terms than a short one; or divide by the total number of answer tokens, so each token counts equally. The demo shows both. For the next-token loss itself, see cross-entropy in my notes on output and loss functions.

4 · Does SFT force the human's way of solving? ▶ 68:12

A student objects: this loss pushes the model to follow exactly what the human wrote. If the model could find a different method, wouldn't it be discouraged from trying?

That's exactly right, in a sense, and it depends on what the "instruction" is. The dataset is designed to make the model follow the instructions humans specify. An instruction is mostly what the task is, plus preferences ("solve it in this fashion", "present the answer like this"). It is typically not how to solve the problem: "solve this maths problem for me" leaves the method to the model.

But what if $y$ contains a chain of thought, the working-out? Then the concern is real. These days people typically say the chain of thought should ideally be generated by the model itself. If humans write it, they may be micromanaging a model that would rather solve the problem another way. Some micromanagement can still be better than nothing, but it probably won't be as good as the RL approach, coming later in the course.

5 · Where does this happen? ▶ 71:09

  • Are weights changed? Yes, definitely: unlike in-context learning, this is training.
  • Is it what happens when I type into ChatGPT? No. This is the training phase. The model you are served was already instruction-tuned on collected (instruction, answer) pairs. Your conversations are not used to train it, at least not immediately; learning from that kind of data is another phase, discussed a few weeks later once RL is set up.
  • When, relative to pretraining? Typically a second phase after it. But the lecturer can't be sure how frontier labs do it now: some of this data can be mixed into pretraining, and it has become more complicated.

6 · Little data: fine-tune only part of the model? ▶ 72:38

The last question: with little fine-tuning data, should you fine-tune only some experts or some layers? The lecturer hasn't heard of fine-tuning a subset of experts (there may be papers) and thinks fine-tuning everything is probably still better. If you lack data, you can instead tune a smaller number of parameters with methods like LoRA.

Going beyond the lecture. The transcript is garbled at this point; "LoRA" is my best reading. LoRA (low-rank adaptation) freezes the pretrained weights $W$ and learns a low-rank correction $W+BA$ with $B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$, $A\in\mathbb R^{r\times d}$ and small $r$, so only $2dr$ numbers per matrix are trained instead of $d^2$.

Key takeaways

  1. Instruction tuning strengthens zero-shot and in-context abilities by training on (instruction $x_i$, answer $y_i$) pairs, starting from the pretrained checkpoint.
  2. The loss is $-\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)$ averaged over examples, which splits into next-token terms $-\log p_\theta(y_{i,t}\mid x_i,y_{i,<t})$: the instruction is given, only answer tokens are predicted.
  3. SFT is any supervised fine-tuning with this loss; instruction tuning is SFT on instruction data.
  4. Instructions say what the task is and how the answer should look. Human-written chains of thought risk micromanaging; model-generated reasoning and RL do better.
  5. SFT changes the weights during training, before deployment; with little data, parameter-efficient methods (e.g. LoRA) are an option.
