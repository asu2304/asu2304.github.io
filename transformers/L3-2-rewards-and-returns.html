Rewards, Returns and Discounting

The reward says how good a state is; the return adds rewards up along a trajectory, discounted by $\gamma^t$ so that sooner is worth more and infinite sums stay finite. With the reward and $\gamma$ added, the MDP is complete, and the goal is simply to maximise the expected return.

1 · The reward function ▶ 18:12

So far we only know how to interact with the world. We also need to say what success means, and the reward is also what we learn from. The reward is a function

$$R:\;S\to\mathbb{R},$$

often non-negative but negative values are allowed, and typically somewhere around $[-1,1]$. $R(s)$ says how good, how preferable, state $s$ is.

VariantScores
$R(s)$a state (used in this lecture, for simplicity)
$R(s,a)$a state–action pair
$R(s,a,s')$a transition

The theory barely changes between these.

Encoding a goal. On the tape (the lecturer redraws it a bit longer), say the goal is cell 9:

$$R(9)=1,\qquad R(s)=-0.1\ \text{ for every } s\neq 9 .$$

Reaching 9 earns reward; every step spent wandering elsewhere costs a little. So this reward encourages the robot to reach 9 as fast as possible and stay there.

2 · Reward shaping, and how it can backfire ▶ 21:02

A student asked: why not let the reward rise gradually as the robot gets closer to 9? You can, since the reward is your choice. But there are two different reasons to do it:

  1. Genuine preference. You really believe that being at 8 is better than being at 7, useful to you even if it isn't 9.
  2. Algorithmic help. You only care about 9, but a gradual slope may make learning easier: "you have more gradient". Changing a sparse, essentially discrete objective into a smoother one is called reward shaping.

The unexpected downside. Suppose there is a teleporting tunnel from cell 2 to cell 9 that you didn't know about when you designed the reward. "Closer to 9 is better" now points the wrong way: the best route might be to walk away to cell 2 and teleport. That sounds far-fetched on a tape, but it's common in robotics. Reaching for a cup, you might set rewards 0.7, 0.8, 0.9 as the hand gets closer. If something blocks the direct path and the hand must go around, that reward is misguiding the robot.

If you understand the environment well, you can shape rewards effectively. If you don't, or it's too complex, it's often better to reward only the final outcome. The lecture's analogy: a manager who knows exactly what to do can give fine-grained feedback; a hands-off manager says "this is my final goal; how you achieve it, I don't care".

Can the agent change the reward function? Not here. The reward is the overarching incentive mechanism for the agent; in most cases it can't be changed.

3 · Trajectories and the return ▶ 25:09

A reward scores one state; we need a score for a whole run. The sequence

$$s_0,\;a_0,\;s_1,\;a_1,\;s_2,\;\dots$$

is called a trajectory (in older RL, an episode; "trajectory" is more common now). Its return (or payoff) sums the rewards:

$$R(s_0)+R(s_1)+R(s_2)+\cdots$$

Even if you fix every action, the states are random (they come from $P_{sa}$), so we talk about the expected return, the expectation over that randomness.

Discounting. Commonly the reward at time $t$ is multiplied by $\gamma^t$:

$$\text{discounted return}=R(s_0)+\gamma R(s_1)+\gamma^2R(s_2)+\cdots=\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tR(s_t),\qquad 0<\gamma<1,$$ typically very close to 1, e.g. $\gamma=0.99$.

Think of an interest rate: one dollar today is worth a bit more than one dollar a hundred years from now. This does not make you myopic. It only says that if you can choose when to collect the same reward, collect it earlier. Sometimes the optimal plan still collects nothing at the beginning, because grabbing early reward would cost you more later.

4 · Why discounting keeps the return bounded ▶ 27:42

We never said how many steps there are; the process can run forever. A small bonus of discounting: if every reward is bounded, $|R(s)|\le M$, the return is bounded for any number of steps.

  1. Triangle inequality: $\left|\sum_t\gamma^tR(s_t)\right|\le\sum_t\gamma^t|R(s_t)|$.
  2. Each term is at most $\gamma^tM$: at most $M$ at the first step, $\gamma M$ at the second, $\gamma^2M$ at the third, …
  3. Geometric series: $M(1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\cdots)=\dfrac{M}{1-\gamma}$.
$$\Big|\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big|\;\le\;\frac{M}{1-\gamma}\quad\text{for every trajectory.}$$

So you can reason about infinitely many steps, which is less common nowadays but matters in some settings where the number of steps is a variable that may be unbounded. Discounting also rewards getting there earlier: reaching for the cup, you want the robot to take the shortest path, not spend a year on it.

5 · The MDP, complete, and the goal ▶ 29:39

An MDP is the tuple $\big(S,\;A,\;\{P_{sa}\},\;\gamma,\;R\big)$: states, actions, transition dynamics, discount factor, reward.

Questions from the room:

  • Are the transition dynamics a set of rules for which actions apply in which states? Roughly, but "rules" isn't the right word: they are probability distributions.
  • In the real world, isn't the state space intractable (e.g. a drone)? Not really. For a drone the state can be quite simple: height, some velocities, whether the motor is on.
  • But height is continuous; how do you list every state? You don't. States can be continuous (height is one scalar dimension of the state), and even a discrete state space needn't be finite.

The goal ▶ 31:57: choose actions to maximise the expected payoff,

$$\max\;\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big].$$

How to choose the actions is the subject of the next part.

Key takeaways

  1. The reward $R(s)$ scores states (variants score $(s,a)$ or $(s,a,s')$); a goal is encoded as e.g. $+1$ at the goal and $-0.1$ elsewhere.
  2. Reward shaping makes a sparse objective smoother, which can help learning, but it can misguide the agent when the environment holds surprises (tunnels, obstacles).
  3. A trajectory's return is $\sum_t\gamma^tR(s_t)$; we maximise its expectation because transitions are random.
  4. Discounting prefers earlier reward without forcing myopia, and bounds the return by $M/(1-\gamma)$.
  5. An MDP is $(S,A,\{P_{sa}\},\gamma,R)$; states may be continuous or infinite in number.
