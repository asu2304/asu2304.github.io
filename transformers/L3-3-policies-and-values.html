Policies and Value Functions

Because the next state depends only on the current state and action, the best action can depend only on the current state: what we look for is a policy, a map from states to actions. Some deterministic policy is always optimal, yet training uses randomised policies because they change smoothly. The value function scores a policy from every starting state.

1 · The Markov property ▶ 32:22

To maximise the expected return you have to pick the right sequence of actions. One fact makes that much simpler. The process is Markov: where you go next depends only on where you are now and what you do now,

$$s_{t+1}\sim P_{s_ta_t}\quad\text{depends on } (s_t,a_t)\text{ only, not on } s_0,a_0,\dots,s_{t-1},a_{t-1}.$$

E.g. the move to $s_2$ depends only on $s_1$ and $a_1$. You don't have to remember the history. Two consequences:

  1. The future forgets the past. Given $s_t$, what happens at $s_{t+1}$, or at any $s_{t+j}$, depends only on $s_t$ and the actions taken from $t$ on, not on anything before $t$.
  2. So the optimal action at time $t$ depends only on $s_t$. How you arrived at $s_t$ is irrelevant: "the past is the past".

2 · Policies ▶ 34:12

That justifies a very specific RL term. A policy is a map from states to actions:

$$\pi:\;S\to A,\qquad a_t=\pi(s_t).$$

Finding a good policy is the only thing you need to achieve the goal: you just need the optimal action as a function of the state.

Tape example (goal 9, no tunnel): the optimal policy is "left of the goal, go right; otherwise go left".

This is a deterministic policy: for a fixed state it outputs a single action. There is always a deterministic optimal policy ▶ 35:54, for a simple reason: in each state take the best action, and if there's a tie pick any of them. There's no reason to randomise, because one option is always at least as good as all the others.

3 · Randomised policies, and why we still want them ▶ 36:26

In practice people also use randomised (stochastic) policies, written

$$\pi(a\mid s):\ \text{given state } s,\ \text{a distribution over actions } a .$$

A randomised policy is in most cases not better than a deterministic one. Why bother? In training you need some continuity; you model the policy with a neural network plus a sampling procedure.

Why hedging can't win ▶ 37:19. Pick action 1 with probability 0.9 and action 2 with probability 0.1. Say action 1 gives payoff 1.0 and action 2 gives 2.0. Then

$$0.9\times1.0+0.1\times2.0=1.1\;\le\;\max(1.0,\,2.0)=2.0 .$$

A mixture's expected payoff is a weighted average, so it lies between the two payoffs, and at least one action is at least as good as the mixture. You should have just taken action 2.

Why randomise in training ▶ 38:35. There are several reasons depending on the setting, but the fundamental one is continuity. A deterministic policy makes a hard switch: for some parameters it picks action 1, and after an update it suddenly jumps to action 2. With a distribution you move along a continuous spectrum: 50/50 now, 40/60 after an update, 30/70 after the next. The dynamics are smoother. (Some policies must be stochastic for other reasons, which come later: policy gradient only works with stochastic policies.)

4 · Try it: hedging and continuity

One state, two actions. A parameter $\theta$ controls the policy. Stochastic: take action 2 with probability $\sigma(\theta)=1/(1+e^{-\theta})$. Deterministic: take action 2 if $\theta\ge0$, else action 1. The curves are expected payoff against $\theta$. It starts at the lecture's 0.9 / 0.1 split. "Gradient step" nudges $\theta$ uphill using the slope at the current point.

The stochastic curve never rises above the best single action (hedging can't win), but it has a usable slope everywhere. The deterministic curve is flat with one cliff, so its slope is zero almost everywhere and gradient steps can't move it.

5 · The value function ▶ 40:03

One more concept before algorithms. The value function of a policy $\pi$ is much like the return, but it fixes where you start and which policy you follow: the expected total payoff starting from state $s$ and applying $\pi$ forever.

$$V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t\ge0}\gamma^tR(s_t)\;\Big|\;s_0=s\Big],\qquad a_t\sim\pi(\cdot\mid s_t),\quad s_{t+1}\sim P_{s_ta_t}.$$

The superscript $\pi$ records that the actions inside the expectation come from $\pi$. It's just a definition.

Optimal value and optimal policy ▶ 41:51. Optimise over policies:

$$V^*(s)=\max_\pi V^\pi(s),\qquad \pi^*=\arg\max_\pi V^\pi(s).$$

$V^*$ doesn't depend on a policy. It answers "if all my future decisions are optimal, how much return can I get from $s$?", and it depends on $s$ because some starting states are better than others. $\pi^*$, the optimal policy, is the maximiser, almost a tautology: the optimal policy is the one that maximises future return from $s$.

Going beyond the lecture: one $\pi^*$ for all states. Written this way, the argmax could in principle differ from state to state. A standard MDP result says it doesn't: a single (deterministic) policy achieves $V^*(s)$ at every $s$ at once, which is why it makes sense to speak of the optimal policy.

Key takeaways

  1. Markov property: $s_{t+1}$ depends only on $(s_t,a_t)$, so the optimal action depends only on $s_t$.
  2. A policy maps states to actions; finding a good one is the whole problem.
  3. A deterministic optimal policy always exists: a mixture's payoff is an average, so hedging never beats the best action (e.g. $0.9\cdot1+0.1\cdot2=1.1\le2$).
  4. Stochastic policies $\pi(a\mid s)$ are used in training for continuity: probabilities move smoothly instead of jumping between actions.
  5. $V^\pi(s)$ = expected discounted return from $s$ under $\pi$; $V^*=\max_\pi V^\pi$, and $\pi^*$ is the maximiser.
