The Bellman Equation

An infinite sum can be pinned down by a recursion: peel off the first term and recognise the rest as a scaled copy of the whole. Doing that to $V^\pi$ gives the Bellman equation: the value of a state is its reward plus $\gamma$ times the average value of the next state. One such equation per state gives a linear system you can just solve.

1 · Why a recursion ▶ 43:12

The Bellman equation is one of the most important ideas of classical RL. It isn't that important for using RL on large language models, and the rest of this lecture won't need it, but you should know it exists and its general idea.

The problem: everything is sequential, unfolding over time, which is complex to reason about. The trick is to write a recursion, and solving the recursion gets rid of the sequential aspect.

2 · Warm-up: a geometric series ▶ 44:22

How do you compute $V=1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\gamma^3+\cdots$ without looking up the formula? (The lecturer names it $V$ on purpose: it's the same concept.)

  1. Take out the first term; every remaining term is a multiple of $\gamma$, so factor one $\gamma$ out: $$V=1+\gamma\,\big(1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\cdots\big).$$
  2. The bracket has exactly the form of $V$, so it is $V$: $$V=1+\gamma V .$$ That's a recursion: $V$ on the left, and also on the right. It doesn't hand you $V$ directly, but you can solve it.
  3. Move the $V$ term left: $V(1-\gamma)=1$, so $$V=\frac{1}{1-\gamma}.$$

(On the board the last line came out as "$V=1-\gamma$"; it should be $1/(1-\gamma)$, as the previous line shows.)

The Bellman equation is exactly this idea in a more complex setting.

3 · Deriving the Bellman equation for $V^\pi$ ▶ 46:09

Take a deterministic policy $\pi$, to keep it simple, so $a_0=\pi(s)$, and infinitely many steps, which keeps things tidy.

  1. Write the definition. $$V^\pi(s)=\mathbb{E}\big[R(s_0)+\gamma R(s_1)+\gamma^2R(s_2)+\cdots\;\big|\;s_0=s\big].$$
  2. Take out the first term. Given $s_0=s$, $R(s_0)=R(s)$ is a constant, not random: $$V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\mathbb{E}\big[\gamma R(s_1)+\gamma^2R(s_2)+\cdots\;\big|\;s_0=s\big].$$ (A student checked this. Yes: $s$ is a fixed number; $s_0$ is the random variable, conditioned to take the value $s$.)
  3. Factor out one $\gamma$, exactly as in the warm-up: $$V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\gamma\,\mathbb{E}\big[R(s_1)+\gamma R(s_2)+\cdots+\gamma^{t-1}R(s_t)+\cdots\;\big|\;s_0=s\big].$$
  4. Recognise the bracket. It's the total expected payoff if you start from $s_1$. The only snag is that $s_1$ is random. So enumerate every possible $s_1$: it occurs with probability $P_{sa_0}(s_1)$, where $a_0=\pi(s)$: $$V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\gamma\sum_{s_1\in S}P_{s\pi(s)}(s_1)\;\mathbb{E}\big[R(s_1)+\gamma R(s_2)+\cdots\;\big|\;s_1\big].$$ (By the Markov property, once you condition on $s_1$ the earlier history doesn't matter.)
  5. The conditional expectation is the value function again, just from a different initial state: it equals $V^\pi(s_1)$.
Bellman equation for a policy $\pi$: $$V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'\in S}P_{s\pi(s)}(s')\,V^\pi(s')\qquad\text{for every } s\in S .$$ Value now = reward now + $\gamma$ × expected value of where you land.

It's a recursion: $V^\pi$ on the left, $V^\pi$ on the right. And not just at one state: every $s'$ appears on the right, each weighted by its probability of being next.

Going beyond the lecture: a stochastic policy. If actions are drawn from $\pi(a\mid s)$, step 4 also averages over $a_0$: $$V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\gamma\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s'}P_{sa}(s')V^\pi(s').$$ Replacing the average over actions by a max gives the Bellman optimality equation for $V^*$, which the course's lecture notes use for value iteration.

4 · It's a linear system ▶ 51:30

Look at what we got. For every state there's one equation, and each equation is linear in the unknowns $V^\pi(\cdot)$. Index the states $0,1,\dots,n-1$:

$$\begin{aligned} V^\pi(0)&=R(0)+\text{a linear combination of }V^\pi(0),\dots,V^\pi(n-1)\\ V^\pi(1)&=R(1)+\text{another linear combination of the same unknowns}\\ &\;\;\vdots\\ V^\pi(n-1)&=R(n-1)+\text{another linear combination.} \end{aligned}$$

The coefficients differ from equation to equation (they are $\gamma P_{s\pi(s)}(s')$), but it's $n$ linear equations in $n$ unknowns. Hand it to a linear solver and you get every $V^\pi(s)$ at once. (A student's question about the board led to the indexing fix: with $n$ states numbered from 0, the last one is $n-1$.)

Does it need $S$ finite? For a solver, yes. But the equation itself holds for infinitely many or continuous states too; you just have infinitely many equations, and it becomes a linear functional equation.

Going beyond the lecture: matrix form. Stack the values into a vector $V$, the rewards into $R$, and let $P_\pi$ be the $n\times n$ matrix with entries $P_{s\pi(s)}(s')$. Then $V=R+\gamma P_\pi V$, i.e. $(I-\gamma P_\pi)V=R$. Since $P_\pi$'s rows are probability distributions and $\gamma<1$, $I-\gamma P_\pi$ is always invertible, so the solution exists and is unique. The demo solves exactly this system.

Key takeaways

  1. Recursion trick: $V=1+\gamma V$ gives $V=1/(1-\gamma)$, with no formula lookup needed.
  2. Peel off $R(s)$, factor out $\gamma$, average over the next state: $$V^\pi(s)=R(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P_{s\pi(s)}(s')V^\pi(s').$$
  3. The Markov property is what lets the tail be "the value from $s_1$".
  4. One equation per state, linear in the unknowns: a linear solver gives $V^\pi$ exactly (for finite $S$).
  5. Bellman equations matter most for classical RL; policy gradient, next, doesn't need them.
