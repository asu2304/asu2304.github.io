Policy Gradient and the Log-Derivative Trick

Policy gradient (REINFORCE) maximises the expected return $\eta(\theta)$ of a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta$ by gradient ascent. The catch: $\theta$ appears only in the distribution we sample from, not inside the expectation. The log-derivative trick turns $\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)]$ back into an expectation, $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\,f(x)]$, which you can estimate by sampling.

1 · Why jump straight to policy gradient ▶ 55:39

There are many algorithms for finding the optimal policy, and the course's lecture notes cover several. This quarter focuses on large language models, so the lecture skips the ones rarely used there and goes directly to the precursor of the algorithms used to train LLMs: policy gradient, also called REINFORCE (all capitals, for historical reasons: that's what its inventor named it).

Two things stand out about it:

  • It doesn't need most of the background above (no Bellman equation, no value functions). In some ways it's much simpler, which is probably why it transfers to LLMs, whose quirks only allow relatively simple algorithms.
  • It works only for stochastic policies, not deterministic ones.

2 · A parameterised stochastic policy ▶ 57:39

Use a neural network with parameters $\theta$ to output a distribution over actions:

$$\pi_\theta(a\mid s).$$

The full distribution might not be listable: actions can be continuous, or there may be infinitely many. The algorithm needs only two things:

  1. Evaluate the density: for any $a$ and $s$, the network gives $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$.
  2. Sample: you can draw $a\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)$.

3 · The objective $\eta(\theta)$ ▶ 58:49

In this literature people usually work with finitely many steps $T$. The expected discounted return of the policy $\pi_\theta$ is

$$\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big],\qquad s_0\sim D_0,\quad a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t),\quad s_{t+1}\sim P_{s_ta_t}.$$

($\eta$ rather than $V$ because that's the letter this literature uses.) The difference from the value function is small: $V^\pi(s)$ fixes $s_0=s$, while here the initial state is random, drawn from a known distribution $D_0$, and we only track the dependence on $\theta$. (A student caught an index slip on the board: the next state is $s_{t+1}$.)

The goal is to find $\theta$ that maximises $\eta(\theta)$, and the approach is simple: take the gradient of $\eta$ and follow it. Because we maximise, that's gradient ascent, $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)$. (The board said "descent"; on $-\eta$ it's the same thing.) See my notes on gradient descent for the update rule itself.

4 · Why the gradient isn't straightforward ▶ 60:54

Look inside the expectation in $\eta$: there's no $\theta$. $R$ is known, $\gamma$ is known, $s_t$ is just what you sampled. All the dependence on $\theta$ comes from the distribution you sample from, not from what you average.

Compare a case you already know how to handle:

$$\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\big[f(\theta,w)\big]=\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal N(0,I)}\big[\nabla_\theta f(\theta,w)\big].$$

There the expectation is over a fixed random thing, so the gradient just moves inside. Try that with $\eta$ and you get the gradient of something with no $\theta$ in it: zero. That's wrong, because changing $\theta$ clearly changes which trajectories you see.

6 · The log-derivative trick ▶ 64:30

  1. Make the $\theta$-dependence explicit. An expectation is probability × value, integrated: $$\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)]=\nabla_\theta\int p_\theta(x)\,f(x)\,dx .$$
  2. Swap gradient and integral. The integration is ordinary integration over $x$ (Lebesgue measure); nothing about it depends on $\theta$: $$=\int\nabla_\theta\big(p_\theta(x)\,f(x)\big)\,dx .$$
  3. $f$ doesn't depend on $\theta$, so only $p_\theta$ is differentiated: $$=\int\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)\;f(x)\,dx .$$ This is a correct formula, and each piece can be computed, but there's no practical way to integrate over all $x$. We want a form we can sample.
  4. Multiply and divide by $p_\theta(x)$. By the chain rule, $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=\dfrac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}$, so $\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)=\big(\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\big)\,p_\theta(x)$: $$=\int\big(\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\big)\,f(x)\;p_\theta(x)\,dx .$$ (Check: the $p_\theta$ in the denominator cancels the $p_\theta$ we multiplied in, giving back step 3.)
  5. Read it as an expectation again. "Probability times value, integrated" is an expectation under $p_\theta$: $$=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\;f(x)\big].$$
Log-derivative (score-function) identity $$\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[f(x)]=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\,f(x)\big].$$ Every piece is available: sample $x\sim p_\theta$, evaluate $f(x)$, differentiate $\log p_\theta(x)$. Averaging over samples gives an unbiased estimate of the gradient. It estimates the gradient, not the function.

The whole point of the log is to make the factor $p_\theta(x)$ reappear, so the expression is an expectation again and we can sample instead of integrating. (On the board the $f(x)$ briefly went missing from one line; a student pointed it out and it was restored.)

A student's question here is partly inaudible; it seems to ask whether the log is there because the probability is a product (as a trajectory's will be). The lecturer's answer: not really. You could take the gradient of a product of probabilities directly; there may be some numerical-stability benefit, but that isn't the main reason. The main reason is getting back to an expectation.

Going beyond the lecture: a two-line sanity check. The identity implies $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)]=0$ (take $f\equiv1$: the gradient of $\mathbb{E}[1]=1$ is 0). So the estimate is "the score, weighted by $f$": samples with large $f$ pull $\theta$ towards making them more likely, and adding a constant to $f$ changes no expectation, only the noise.

Key takeaways

  1. Policy gradient (REINFORCE) needs a stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ that you can evaluate and sample; nothing else from the MDP machinery.
  2. Objective: $\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t}\gamma^tR(s_t)]$ with $s_0\sim D_0$; maximise it by gradient ascent.
  3. $\theta$ sits in the sampling distribution, so you can't push $\nabla$ inside the expectation as you would for $\mathbb{E}_{w}[f(\theta,w)]$.
  4. Log-derivative trick (write as an integral, swap, multiply and divide by $p_\theta$): $$\nabla_\theta\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[f]=\mathbb{E}_{p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\,f(x)].$$
  5. The result is an expectation, so a sample average gives an unbiased gradient estimate.
