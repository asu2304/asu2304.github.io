REINFORCE on Trajectories

Apply the log-derivative trick with $x$ = a whole trajectory. The trajectory's log-probability splits into a sum, and every term that comes from the environment drops out of the gradient because it doesn't depend on $\theta$. What's left uses only the policy's own log-probabilities and the observed rewards: you never need to know the dynamics.

1 · Plug in a trajectory ▶ 69:19

From part 5, with $x$ the trajectory $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots)$ and $f(\tau)=\sum_t\gamma^tR(s_t)$:

$$\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)=\nabla_\theta\,\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big]=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)\cdot\sum_{t}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big].$$

The $f$ part is kept as is. What is $p_\theta(\tau)$? Follow the generative story step by step: draw $s_0$, then $a_0$ given $s_0$, then $s_1$ given $(s_0,a_0)$, and so on:

$$p_\theta(\tau)=D_0(s_0)\;\pi_\theta(a_0\mid s_0)\;P_{s_0a_0}(s_1)\;\pi_\theta(a_1\mid s_1)\;P_{s_1a_1}(s_2)\cdots\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\;P_{s_ta_t}(s_{t+1})\cdots$$

At this point the problem is essentially solved: everything in the formula can be evaluated, sampled or differentiated. Almost. Simplify it first.

2 · The dynamics drop out ▶ 71:38

"Everything is computable" isn't quite right: $D_0$ and the transition probabilities $P_{sa}$ may be unknown. So far we never assumed we know them, and the algorithm doesn't need them.

  1. Log of a product is a sum of logs: $$\log p_\theta(\tau)=\log D_0(s_0)+\sum_t\log P_{s_ta_t}(s_{t+1})+\sum_t\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$
  2. Differentiate in $\theta$. $D_0$ and $P_{s_ta_t}$ describe the environment; they don't depend on $\theta$, so their gradients are 0 and they're gone: $$\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t).$$
  3. Substitute back into the expectation.
Policy gradient (REINFORCE) $$\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big)\Big]$$ Needs: samples of trajectories from the current policy, the rewards you observed, and $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ from your own network. Doesn't need: the transition dynamics or $D_0$.

Read it as the "reinforce" story from part 1, made precise. Each sampled trajectory pushes up the log-probability of every action it took, scaled by how good the whole trajectory was. High-return trajectories get their actions made more likely.

One more simplification ▶ 73:34 is available, coming from the fact that the formula is a product of two sums, with many cross terms. The lecture ran out of time here and pointed to the lecture notes. As it stands, though, the estimator is already fully computable: compute each sum and multiply.

Going beyond the lecture: what that simplification is (standard form). Expand the product into cross terms $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\cdot\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'})$. For $t'\le t$, the reward was already decided before $a_t$ was drawn, and the score has mean zero given the past (part 5's $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log p]=0$), so these terms average to 0. Dropping them gives the "reward-to-go" form $$\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\sum_{t'>t}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'})\Big]:$$ same expectation, less noise. (With rewards on states, $R(s_t)$ itself is fixed before $a_t$; I checked this numerically against the exact gradient on a small tape.)

3 · "Isn't this intractable for LLMs?" ▶ 74:29

  • Q: If you enumerate all possible generations of length $T$, isn't that exponentially many? A: You never enumerate. You sample: one trajectory, sometimes five or ten.
  • Q: So it's a Monte Carlo estimate? A: It's just sampling: to compute the expectation of a random variable, sample it and take the empirical mean.
  • Q: How large is the variance? A: Hard to say in general. Next time: ways to reduce it. In the lecturer's view it isn't crazily big, not "exponential in $T$" or astronomical, but in practice you always want to reduce variance, even when it isn't huge, and people have many ways to do so.

4 · Try it: train a policy with REINFORCE

The actions are only L and R, so at 9 the robot steps off and must come back. Going beyond the lecture: the baseline toggle. Variance reduction is next lecture's topic; this toggle previews the most common trick. Replace $\sum_t\gamma^tR(s_t)$ for trajectory $i$ by that return minus $b_i$, the mean return of the other trajectories in the batch. Because $b_i$ doesn't depend on trajectory $i$'s own actions, and the score has mean zero, the expected gradient is unchanged. Trajectories now count as "good" only if they beat their batch-mates. That removes whatever part of the return all trajectories share, and only that. Use the "measure gradient noise" button: with a fixed start cell (and especially early on, when nearly every return is the same string of $-0.1$s) the baseline cuts the variance sharply. With a random start cell, most of the spread in returns comes from where each trajectory started, which a batch average can't remove, and the baseline helps little or even adds noise. Variance reduction is about matching the baseline to what is actually shared.

See my notes on stochastic vs batch gradient descent for the same "noisy but unbiased gradient" idea in supervised learning; $n$ plays the role of the mini-batch size.

Key takeaways

  1. The log turns the trajectory probability's product into a sum: $$p_\theta(\tau)=D_0(s_0)\prod_t\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,P_{s_ta_t}(s_{t+1}).$$
  2. Environment terms don't depend on $\theta$, so they vanish from the gradient: REINFORCE is model-free, with no knowledge of $P_{sa}$ needed.
  3. Raise the log-probability of the actions taken, in proportion to the trajectory's return: $$\nabla\eta=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\sum_t\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)\Big(\sum_t\gamma^tR(s_t)\Big)\Big].$$
  4. Estimate it by sampling a few trajectories and averaging; you never enumerate.
  5. The estimate is unbiased but noisy; reducing its variance comes next.
