Policy Gradient as Weighted Log-Likelihood, and Reward-to-Go

The policy gradient pushes up the log-probability of every sampled trajectory, weighted by how much reward it earned. Because the score $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta$ averages to zero, any weight that an action cannot influence drops out, so each action only needs to be weighted by the reward that comes after it: the reward-to-go.

1 · Where we left off ▶ 0:55

A stochastic policy $\pi_\theta(a\mid s)$ (a neural network that gives the probability of action $a$ in state $s$) is judged by its expected return:

$$\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t R(s_t,a_t)\Big]$$

Here $\tau=(s_0,a_0,s_1,a_1,\dots)$ is a trajectory: $s_0$ from the initial distribution, each $a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)$, each $s_{t+1}$ from the transition dynamics. The discount $\gamma$ "is not that important" here (for language models it is usually 1).

The difficulty. Nothing inside the expectation depends on $\theta$. The dependence on $\theta$ lives in the sampling probabilities of the actions. If you just swapped $\nabla_\theta$ and $\mathbb{E}$, you'd differentiate a sum of rewards that has no $\theta$ in it and get zero, ignoring the real dependence. That's why we needed the policy-gradient estimator. Last lecture's derivation (the transition probabilities drop out because they don't depend on $\theta$) gave

$$\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big)}_{\nabla_\theta\log(\text{prob. of the actions in }\tau)}\;\underbrace{\Big(\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^t R(s_t,a_t)\Big)}_{\text{total reward }R(\tau)}\Big]$$

The sum of the log-probabilities is the log-probability of the whole sequence of actions in the trajectory (given the states), so you can put $\nabla_\theta$ in front of each term or in front of the sum; it doesn't matter.

2 · Reading the formula: reward-weighted maximum likelihood ▶ 2:48

Last time this came out of the algebra. Stare at it and it makes sense on its own:

  • Following this gradient maximises the log-likelihood of the trajectories you sampled, the same thing supervised learning does with data.
  • But each trajectory gets a weight, its total reward. High reward: you push its likelihood up hard. Reward 0: weight 0, so you don't increase its likelihood at all.
  • Good trajectories get more weight, bad ones less. That's all policy gradient does.

3 · The key fact: the score has mean zero ▶ 4:00

To simplify further we need one quantity. Sample $a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)$, evaluate its log-probability under the same policy, take the gradient in $\theta$ (a vector of numbers), and average over $a_t$. The answer is zero. In the abstract form:

Claim. If $x\sim p_\theta$, then $\;\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}\big[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\big]=0.$
  1. Write the expectation as an integral: $$\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)]=\int p_\theta(x)\,\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)\,dx$$
  2. Chain rule with $(\log u)'=1/u$: $\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)=\dfrac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)}{p_\theta(x)}$. The two $p_\theta(x)$ cancel, leaving $\displaystyle\int\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x)\,dx$.
  3. Swap the integral and the gradient (every derivative is with respect to $\theta$): $\displaystyle\nabla_\theta\int p_\theta(x)\,dx$.
  4. A probability distribution integrates to 1, so this is $\nabla_\theta 1=0$.

What it means. Compare with the policy gradient. There, each action's log-likelihood is weighted by reward. Here there's no weighting at all, and the gradient is 0. That makes sense: if you have no idea which actions are good or bad, you have no preference, so the update should be nothing.

The useful corollary. Multiplying by any constant doesn't change that zero, as long as the constant is not a function of $a_t$. Something that depends only on $s_t$ is fine:

$$\mathbb{E}_{a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\;c(s_t)\big]=c(s_t)\cdot 0=0 .$$

4 · Reward-to-go: the past can't be changed ▶ 7:40

Now use the corollary. Pull the sum over $t$ outside the expectation and rename the reward index to $t'$:

$$\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\sum_{t'=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'},a_{t'})\Big]$$

(Nothing has been used yet; this is just rewriting.) Split the inner sum into rewards before step $t$ and the rest:

$$\sum_{t'=0}^{T-1}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'},a_{t'})=\underbrace{\sum_{t'<t}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'},a_{t'})}_{\text{past: }R_{<t}}+\underbrace{\sum_{t'\ge t}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'},a_{t'})}_{\text{future: }R_{\ge t}}$$

Claim: $\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,R_{<t}\big]=0$. Reason: $a_t$ has nothing to do with what happened before step $t$; all of that happened before $s_t$ was even seen. Mathematically:

  1. Law of total expectation. Split the randomness into two batches: first the history $s_0,a_0,\dots,s_{t-1},a_{t-1},s_t$, then $a_t$ given that history: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,R_{<t}\big]=\mathbb{E}_{s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t}\Big[\,\mathbb{E}_{a_t}\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,R_{<t}\;\big|\;s_0,a_0,\dots,s_t\big]\Big]$$
  2. Condition, then factor out. Once the history is fixed, $R_{<t}$ is literally a constant, so it moves outside the inner expectation: $R_{<t}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{a_t}\big[\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\mid s_0,\dots,s_t\big]$.
  3. Markov property. Conditioning on the whole history is the same as conditioning on $s_t$ alone: $a_t\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)$.
  4. Score has mean zero (section 3): the inner expectation is 0. A constant times 0 is 0, and the outer expectation of 0 is 0.

A student asked whether this uses $\mathbb{E}[AB]=\mathbb{E}[A]\,\mathbb{E}[B]$. It doesn't (that's false in general). It uses the fact that, after conditioning on the past, $B=R_{<t}$ is a constant (▶ 20:38).

Intuitively: the past reward is something you can't change, so it shouldn't affect how you weight this decision. Only the reward you can still influence matters. What survives is

$$\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,R_{\ge t}\Big],\qquad R_{\ge t}=\sum_{t'=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{t'}R(s_{t'},a_{t'})$$

$R_{\ge t}$ is usually called the reward-to-go: the future reward that action $a_t$ can affect. (The lecture calls it "$R$ larger than $t$"; it includes the reward at step $t$ itself, since $a_t$ affects $R(s_t,a_t)$.)

Check the claim on a made-up MDP: 2 states, 2 actions, horizon $T=4$, $\gamma=0.9$, tabular softmax policy (4 parameters). The exact gradient is computed by enumerating all 256 trajectories. Then we draw single trajectories and use each one as a gradient estimate, with the total return $R(\tau)$ as the weight or with the reward-to-go $R_{\ge t}$.

Going beyond the lecture: the variance. The lecture's argument is that the two estimators have the same expectation. The table also shows the other half of the story: dropping $R_{<t}$ removes terms that only add noise, so the reward-to-go estimator usually has a smaller spread per trajectory. The next page makes this the explicit goal.

Key takeaways

  1. $\eta(\theta)$ depends on $\theta$ only through the sampling distribution, so you can't just push $\nabla_\theta$ inside the expectation; the policy-gradient estimator handles this.
  2. The policy gradient is maximum likelihood on your own samples, with each trajectory weighted by its reward.
  3. $\mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_\theta}[\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(x)]=0$; multiplying by anything that doesn't depend on the sampled action keeps it zero.
  4. Rewards earned before step $t$ are constants once you condition on the history, so they drop out: weight $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ by the reward-to-go $R_{\ge t}$ only.
