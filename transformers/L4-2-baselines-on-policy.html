Baselines, the Policy-Gradient Loop, and Why It Is On-Policy

Subtracting a baseline $b(s_t)$ from the reward-to-go leaves the expected gradient unchanged but can shrink its variance enormously, and it turns "reinforce everything a little" into "reinforce the good, penalise the bad". The resulting algorithm is simple: sample, estimate, step. Its weakness is that every sample can be used only once.

1 · Adding a baseline ▶ 15:57

The whole calculation on the previous page rested on one point: inside $\mathbb{E}\big[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,(\cdots)\big]$ you may add any term that is not a function of $a_t$. Anything that depends only on $s_t$, and not on any future state or action, contributes exactly zero. So subtract such a term, $b(s_t)$:

$$\nabla_\theta\eta(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,\big(R_{\ge t}-b(s_t)\big)\Big]$$

$b$ is called a baseline. With or without it, the expectation is identical. So why bother? Because in practice you never compute the expectation exactly; you sample some trajectories and average. The baseline is there to reduce the variance of that estimate.

2 · The 10-to-11 example ▶ 17:28

Suppose the reward is always between 10 and 11. Then the weight $R_{\ge t}$ is always a number between 10 and 11, large and nearly the same for every sample. Subtract about 10.5 and the adjusted reward lives between −0.5 and 0.5. This is what people actually do these days: estimate the average reward-to-go and subtract it, so the rewards are balanced around zero.

Why does this reduce variance? It's the classical control variate idea. You have a random variable whose variance you want to reduce. Find something that is correlated with it but has mean zero, and subtract it. Here $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,b(s_t)$ has mean zero and is correlated with $\nabla\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\,R_{\ge t}$ (over all the randomness), so subtracting it lowers the variance without changing the mean.

A student's objection ▶ 19:36: "isn't that just subtracting a constant 10.5?" The lecturer agreed the example was oversimplified: the real gain comes from a baseline that depends on $s_t$. Say in one state the reward is between 10 and 11 and in another between 0 and 1. You subtract 10.5 in the first and 0.5 in the second, and now everything is on the same scale. Without it, one kind of trajectory multiplies its score by 10 or 11 and the other by 0 or 1.

Another way to see it ▶ 22:14. One state, two actions: one gets reward 10, the other 11. Since 10 is the worst you can do, you really ought to penalise the 10-action. But with raw rewards, the gradient reinforces both: the 11-action a bit more strongly, the 10-action a bit less. In expectation that's fine, because only the relative difference matters. In practice it's more direct to recentre, so that the 10-action is a negative example and the 11-action a positive one.

Train it yourself. Two equally likely states, two actions each: in state A the rewards are 10 (bad action) and 11 (good action); in state B they are 0 and 1. Every run starts at a uniform policy and does REINFORCE with $N$ samples per step. Each curve averages 30 independent runs.

Going beyond the lecture: a constant baseline isn't useless either. Mathematically, even one constant changes the variance (not the mean). For a single state with rewards 10 and 11, subtracting 10.5 is a constant and cuts the variance a lot. With states on different scales, the best single constant (5.5 here, the average reward) helps only partly, and the per-state baseline is far better, as the table's first column shows. The "stuck" column shows the failure the lecturer describes: with all-positive weights, whichever action you happen to sample gets reinforced, and a run can lock into the bad action.

3 · A warning before PPO ▶ 23:39

That's everything about policy gradient. Next comes PPO, a variant of it (the name stands for proximal policy optimization). The lecturer is candid about its status:

  • Exactly why it works, and in which cases, is still somewhat of a mystery, especially at the theoretical level. There are many explanations; none is very convincing to him. What follows repeats existing justifications with some of his own tweaks.
  • It isn't perfect: later in the lecture come several ways people have tried to fix it, and the fixes aren't perfect either.
  • Stability of post-training is a big issue in general; it takes some "magic". There's no consensus on the best way to stabilise it, at least in the open-source community. Frontier labs may know more.

4 · The policy-gradient loop ▶ 26:38

Every policy-gradient algorithm has the same structure:

repeat: 1. sample many trajectories τ⁽¹⁾, …, τ⁽ⁿ⁾ from the current policy π_θ 2. estimate ∇η(θ): replace the expectation by the empirical mean over the sampled trajectories (say ~120 of them) 3. update θ ← θ + α · (estimated ∇η(θ))

Step 3 is gradient ascent, since we maximise return. Step 2 is the same move as mini-batch SGD, an average over samples standing in for the expectation (see stochastic vs mini-batch gradient descent).

5 · The catch: it is on-policy ▶ 28:02

The estimator needs trajectories sampled from the policy whose gradient you are computing. You sample from $\pi_\theta$, take the gradient at $\theta$, update $\theta$. Algorithms like this are called on-policy.

What if you want two gradient steps on one batch? Strictly speaking, that isn't allowed. After the first step you're at a new $\theta$, and estimating the gradient there needs fresh samples from the new policy; trajectories from a different distribution don't estimate the right thing. So each trajectory is used for one gradient step and then thrown away. For language models, where generating trajectories is the expensive part (see part 6), that's painful.

PPO's answer is importance sampling: reuse trajectories sampled from an old policy to estimate the gradient at the current one. That's the next page.

Key takeaways

  1. Any $b(s_t)$ that doesn't depend on $a_t$ or the future can be subtracted from $R_{\ge t}$ without changing the expected gradient.
  2. The point is variance: a baseline is a control variate, and recentring rewards around zero (for example, subtracting the average reward-to-go) makes samples comparable.
  3. A state-dependent baseline puts states with very different reward scales on one footing, and turns worse-than-average actions into negative examples.
  4. Policy gradient = sample from $\pi_\theta$, average to estimate $\nabla\eta$, step $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\alpha\hat\nabla\eta$.
  5. It is on-policy: samples must come from the current $\theta$, so each batch is used once. PPO tries to fix that.
