The PPO Clipped Objective, Asymmetric Clipping, and CISPO

PPO packs its four cases into one line: the smaller of $r_t\hat A_t$ and the same term with $r_t$ clipped to $[1-\epsilon_\text{low},\,1+\epsilon_\text{high}]$. Where the clipped branch wins, the term is a constant and gives no gradient. The two clip widths control different things: learning speed on one side, diversity on the other. CISPO takes a different view: what's dangerous is a big ratio, not a big step in the right direction.

1 · The formula you'll see in papers ▶ 44:09

People often get confused by it, but it does exactly the four-case analysis from the previous page:

$$J_\text{PPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{s_t,a_t\sim\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}}\Big[\min\Big(r_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t,\;\operatorname{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\,1-\epsilon_\text{low},\,1+\epsilon_\text{high}\big)\,\hat A_t\Big)\Big]$$

$\operatorname{clip}(r,lo,hi)$ cuts $r$ off at $lo$ and $hi$. Unpacking it ▶ 45:00:

  1. $\hat A>0$, $r_t>1+\epsilon_\text{high}$. Say $r_t=10$. The first branch is $10\hat A$; the second is only $(1+\epsilon_\text{high})\hat A$. The min takes the clipped branch. That value is a constant: once clipped, it's no longer a function of $\theta$, so no gradient.
  2. $\hat A>0$, $r_t\le1+\epsilon_\text{high}$. Now $r_t\hat A$ is the smaller (or equal) branch, so you get the normal gradient, pushing $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$ up. Check with $r_t=0.5$ and $\epsilon_\text{low}=0.2$: the clipped branch is $0.8\hat A$, the plain one is $0.5\hat A$, and the min is the plain one (▶ 47:34). Between the two thresholds the branches are equal.
  3. $\hat A<0$: the same with signs reversed. Multiplying by a negative number flips which branch is smaller. For $r_t<1-\epsilon_\text{low}$ the min picks the clipped constant $(1-\epsilon_\text{low})\hat A$, so no gradient. For $r_t\ge1-\epsilon_\text{low}$ it's $r_t\hat A$, the normal push down.

2 · Why clip at all? ▶ 48:06

The original motivation: don't move too far from $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$. Stay in its neighbourhood, so that the big approximation from the previous page (sampling states from the old policy) still roughly makes sense. If the new policy is already doing great on a sample (much better than the old one), don't update on it. Only if it isn't yet much better do you update. The lecturer isn't sure that's exactly the reason, "but that's the story". There's a buffer: the threshold isn't $1$, it's $1\pm\epsilon$.

Doesn't that slow learning? ▶ 49:01 Yes, exactly: clipping slows learning down.

3 · Should εlow and εhigh be equal? ▶ 49:13

Papers set them differently; the magic numbers are around 0.2 and 0.28. The lecturer offers an argument, while saying he doesn't fundamentally understand it either:

  • $\epsilon_\text{high}$ (good actions): clipping here only slows learning, shrinking updates on samples where you're already doing OK. Being less strict is probably no big deal: "even if I'm 2× better than the old policy, I'm still learning" is fine. So people are more willing to raise $\epsilon_\text{high}$.
  • $\epsilon_\text{low}$ (bad actions): this side is about diversity. Making a bad action's probability ever smaller can backfire. Penalise it too hard and the policy becomes too deterministic and converges to a local minimum. Give the action some benefit of the doubt: maybe it's bad now, but combined with other actions it becomes useful later. So there's more reason not to over-learn on this side.
Going beyond the lecture. The asymmetric setting $\epsilon_\text{low}=0.2$, $\epsilon_\text{high}=0.28$ is the "clip-higher" trick from DAPO (ByteDance Seed, 2025). Its stated motivation is the same: keep exploration (entropy) from collapsing by letting low-probability good tokens grow faster.

4 · CISPO: clip the weight, don't drop the gradient ▶ 51:20

CISPO is a newer, popular algorithm. In the lecturer's own group's training runs it's what they mostly use instead of PPO. (He doesn't claim it's fundamentally right; it's popular and probably the best option for now.) Picture it against PPO ▶ 51:57:

  • $\hat A>0$. Business as usual is the straight line $r_t$. PPO (and GRPO, a more advanced version of PPO that behaves the same here) goes to zero learning above $1+\epsilon_\text{high}$. CISPO doesn't disable learning there: it clips the value to a constant but keeps the gradient. You still learn, just less aggressively than the linear line would.
  • $\hat A<0$ ▶ 53:25. PPO zeroes everything below $1-\epsilon_\text{low}$ and is linear above it, with no cap. CISPO does the same as on the positive side: above $1+\epsilon_\text{high}$ it takes the constant, and below it keeps the ordinary weight.

The reasoning ▶ 54:51. CISPO's view is that any case where $r_t$ is big is a bad case, because $r_t$ multiplies the gradient. A big ratio gives that term too much weight and makes training unstable. That's true whether or not the policy has "already learned" this sample; the problem is simply that the update is too big. And ratios can get very big because they're ratios of probabilities: $0.6/0.2=3$, or $0.6/0.01=60$. So CISPO just caps anything big.

The cost. On the bad-action side, CISPO keeps pushing a bad action's probability down even after it's already below the old policy's. That's exactly where you lose diversity, and the lecturer has seen CISPO sometimes lose diversity in his own projects.

Going beyond the lecture: the formula. In the MiniMax-M1 paper (2025) that introduced it, CISPO's objective is a REINFORCE term with the clipped ratio as a constant weight (stop-gradient, "sg"): $$J_\text{CISPO}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\Big[\operatorname{sg}\big(\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon_\text{low},1+\epsilon_\text{high})\big)\,\hat A_t\,\log\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)\Big]$$ so every token keeps a nonzero gradient with weight at most $1+\epsilon_\text{high}$. As far as I recall the paper, only the upper bound is tuned and the lower bound is effectively switched off, which is what the demo draws.

5 · Still an open question ▶ 55:53

Every few months there's a different clipping scheme. Another paper, for example, combines CISPO and PPO by clipping in yet another way. This is still an open research area; nobody really knows the true answer.

Key takeaways

  1. $J_\text{PPO}$, the expected minimum of $r_t\hat A_t$ and its clipped version, is just the four cases in one line. A clipped branch is a constant, so it gives zero gradient.
  2. The story: stay close to $\pi_{\theta_\text{old}}$ so the state-distribution approximation holds. The price is slower learning.
  3. $\epsilon_\text{high}$ mostly controls speed and can be loosened (e.g. 0.28); $\epsilon_\text{low}$ protects diversity, so be reluctant to over-penalise bad actions.
  4. CISPO caps the ratio's weight instead of dropping the gradient, because large ratios mean large, unstable updates. It can lose diversity on the bad-action side.
  5. Stabilising RL post-training is unsettled; the clipping recipe keeps changing.
