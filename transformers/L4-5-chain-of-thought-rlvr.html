Chain of Thought and RL with Verifiable Rewards

Letting a model "think" in extra tokens before answering helps on multi-step problems. Prompting can elicit it and supervised data can teach it, but the best way to train it is reinforcement learning. Treat generation as an MDP (state = prompt plus tokens so far, action = next token) and give a reward only at the end: 1 if the extracted answer matches the ground truth, 0 otherwise.

1 · Chain of thought ▶ 56:31

With the remaining time, the lecture turns to applying all this to large language models. The first notion needed is chain of thought (CoT), and it's very simple.

So far: a prompt $x_1,\dots,x_l$ (the question, say "what is 1 + 1?") and the model generates the answer $y_1,\dots,y_k$ right away. It turns out that answering immediately is not necessarily best. You should let the model think for a while first:

prompt (given) generated by the model x₁ … x_l → <think> z₁ … z_n </think> y₁ … y_k thinking tokens answer

The thinking tokens often start and end with special tokens, but not always: some models don't produce any such separator and simply start thinking, then answer. (The lecturer mentions Qwen and DeepSeek models.)

2 · How it was discovered: few-shot CoT ▶ 58:02

CoT was first found useful on math problems, or any question that needs several steps. No human can produce the answer to such a question immediately; it takes steps. The $z_1,\dots,z_n$ lay out that step-by-step calculation.

In the first paper, people used few-shot learning (from earlier in the course) to encourage this behaviour. The prompt contains, say, three similar questions, each with a handcrafted step-by-step solution followed by the answer. All of these are concatenated, then comes: now solve my question the same way. That's few-shot CoT.

3 · Zero-shot CoT ▶ 59:30

Later people found something much simpler: no examples at all. Add to the question

Let's think step by step.

and the model naturally starts reasoning step by step before answering. These few magic words improve accuracy by 5%, 10%, sometimes 20% or more.

4 · Training for it with supervised data, and why that's limited ▶ 60:27

If this is so useful, why not train the model so the capability becomes more prominent? The first idea was instruction tuning: manually create a dataset of step-by-step solutions and do supervised fine-tuning on it. That's limited, because we don't really know the best way to think step by step:

  • Look at the thinking tokens of open-source models (closed models only show a summary of their thinking). They are not very logical: lots of back and forth, sometimes very terse shorthand, sometimes skepticism about the question itself. It isn't a coherent, shortest path. It's lengthy back and forth, probably much like how people really solve problems: try ideas, reflect on what you've done.
  • So if you handcraft thinking processes, you can't even produce that kind of messy, useful process.
  • There was a study where people generated very succinct thinking processes. Those turned out not very useful for the model; it's too hard to think that way. The model needs to spend more tokens and think in a less "logical" way.

Supervised CoT works to some degree, but it's hard to scale.

5 · Reinforcement learning: reward the answer only ▶ 62:39

The benefit of RL: you don't need labels showing how to answer. You only need to judge whether the answer is correct. Treat the whole generation as a trajectory and reward it based on the final answer alone. You don't care how the thinking was done: think however you like, as long as the final answer is right and can be verified.

6 · Language-model generation as an MDP ▶ 63:55

Formally, view generation as an MDP and apply the RL tools. For this you don't even need to distinguish thinking from answer: there's an input $x=(x_1,\dots,x_l)$ and a generation $y_1,\dots,y_n$, part of which is thinking and part answer.

MDPlanguage model
state $s_t$the prompt and everything generated so far: $(x,\,y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$
action $a_t$the next token, $y_t$
policy $\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)$the model's next-token distribution $\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})$
transition $s_{t+1}$ given $(s_t,a_t)$concatenation: $(x,y_1,\dots,y_{t-1})$ followed by $y_t$. Completely deterministic and trivial.
reward $R(s_t,a_t)$zero at every step except the very end, where the whole trajectory $(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ is scored

The dynamics are trivial, but that doesn't make the problem easy. You take an action at every step, over a long sequence, and you have to produce the right tokens all along to reach the answer. (Generating $y_t$ conditioned on $x$ and $y_{<t}$ is exactly what a masked decoder does; see the transformer decoder.)

7 · The verifiable reward ▶ 66:16

The reward of the trajectory is literally whether the answer part matches the ground-truth answer. This requires a dataset where you know the ground truth: for a math calculation you know the final result and check whether the model's result matches. The reward is usually binary:

$$R(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)=\begin{cases}1 & \text{if the extracted answer matches the ground truth}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}$$

In reality there are many variations. Formatting matters: you must be able to extract the answer quickly and in a mechanical way, not by calling another AI model. So the model is asked to wrap its answer in a tag, XML style, <answer> … </answer>. A parser pulls out what's inside, compares it with the ground truth, and gives 1 or 0. Models are better now and can be asked for other formats, but early on it was this simple.

Turn a response into an MDP trajectory and score it. Tokens here are just words and symbols, to keep it readable; real tokenizers split into sub-word pieces.

Key takeaways

  1. Chain of thought: let the model generate thinking tokens $z_1,\dots,z_n$ (often between special tokens) before the answer.
  2. It was first elicited with few-shot CoT (worked examples in the prompt), then zero-shot ("Let's think step by step"), worth 5–20% accuracy or more.
  3. Supervised CoT data is limited: useful thinking is messy and long, and humans can't easily write it; terse, tidy reasoning helps less.
  4. RL only needs a verdict on the final answer. Generation is an MDP with $s_t=(x,y_{<t})$, $a_t=y_t$, deterministic concatenation dynamics, and a reward only at the end.
  5. Verifiable reward: parse the answer from a fixed format (e.g. <answer> tags) and give 1 if it matches the ground truth, else 0.
